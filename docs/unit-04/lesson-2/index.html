Vocabulary — Ethnic groups, their homes and their way of life

ethnic group

/ˈeθnɪk ɡruːp/1

nouncountable

a group of people who share the same language, culture and origins

dân tộc, nhóm dân tộc

  • Viet Nam has 54 ethnic groups.
  • Each ethnic group has its own costume and festivals.
  • The Tay are the largest ethnic group in the north.

Word family

ethnic minority

/ˌeθnɪk maɪˈnɒrəti/2

nouncountable

an ethnic group that is small in number compared with the main group in a country

dân tộc thiểu số

  • The school teaches children from three ethnic minorities.

an ethnic minority group · ethnic minority languages

Opposite of majority. The plural is minorities — change y to ies.

majority

/məˈdʒɒrəti/3

nounsingular, + of

  • The Kinh are the majority in Viet Nam.

Watch out

Opposite: minority. With the majority of + plural noun, the verb is usually plural: the majority of students are …

communal house

/kəˈmjuːnl haʊs/19

nouncountable

a large building that belongs to a whole village and is used for meetings, festivals and ceremonies

nhà rông, nhà sinh hoạt cộng đồng

  • The Bahnar build the tallest communal house in the middle of the village.
  • Every decision about the harvest is made in the communal house.
  • Visitors are welcomed at the communal house before they go to anyone's home.

build a communal house · meet at the communal house · the village communal house

Watch out

Two words, and the stress falls on the second syllable of communal: /kəˈmjuːnl/. It is a building for the whole community, not a house anybody lives in — so my communal house is almost never right.

livestock

/ˈlaɪvstɒk/20

noununcountable, plural verb

the animals a farm keeps for work, food or wool — cattle, buffaloes, pigs, goats, sheep

gia súc, vật nuôi

  • Her family's livestock graze on the hillside above the village.
  • In the dry season there is not much grass for the livestock.
  • Under the floor of the stilt house they used to keep tools and livestock.

keep livestock · raise livestock · feed the livestock

Watch out

No plural -s: ❌ many livestocks → ✅ a lot of livestock. The word looks singular but takes a plural verb: the livestock are in the field. It does not cover chickens and ducks — those are poultry.

/ˈɡɑːdnɪŋ/22

soil

/sɔɪl/23

noununcountable

the top layer of the earth that plants grow in

đất trồng

  • The soil on a steep slope is thin and washes away in heavy rain.
  • Coffee grows well in the red soil of the Central Highlands.
  • They spread ash over the field to feed the soil.

rich soil · poor soil · dry soil

Watch out

Uncountable: ❌ many soils → ✅ a lot of soil. Soil is what you grow things in; ground is what you stand on; land is an area you own or farm. Rice grows in the soil, on someone's land.

/ˈfəʊk dɑːns/29

nouncountable

a traditional dance of a particular people, learnt from older dancers rather than at a school

Folk /fəʊk/ has a silent l — say it like joke with an f. The same word builds folk song, folk music and folk tale, and in all of them it means belonging to ordinary people, passed down by ear.

Pronunciation — /k/ and /ɡ/

These two sounds are made in exactly the same place — the back of the tongue against the soft palate. The only difference is voice.

/k/ — voiceless /ɡ/ — voiced
Voice box silent, no buzz buzzing
Air a small puff of air at the start of a word almost no puff
Test cover your ears and say it — quiet cover your ears — you hear a hum inside your head
Examples coat, custom, costume, cave, culture, market, basket, folk goat, group, gong, garment, guitar, ethnic group, language

Spelling traps — check the sound, not the letter:

  • gh in ghost /ɡəʊst/ is a plain /ɡ/ — the h is silent.
  • c in ceremony /ˈserəməni/ is /s/, not /k/. Before e, i and y, the letter c is usually /s/: ceremony, city, cycle.
  • g in village /ˈvɪlɪdʒ/ is /dʒ/, not /ɡ/.
  • l in folk /fəʊk/ is silent, but the /k/ at the end is not.

Mẹo: Đặt tay lên cổ họng. Nói coat — cổ họng im. Nói goat — bạn thấy rung. Hãy giữ độ rung đến hết âm: dog, ba**g, flag**.

Go further

Final /ɡ/ is outside the Vietnamese coda inventory — that is why it drifts to /k/

The end of the word is the hard part, and there is a structural reason for it. A Vietnamese syllable may close with only eight segments: the three unreleased voiceless obstruents /p t k/, the three nasals /m n ŋ/, and /j w/. No voiced obstruent closes a Vietnamese syllable at all — so English final /b d ɡ/ sit outside the inventory the mouth already has, and /ɡ/ drifts to the nearest thing that is inside it, /k/.

That is why the drill is hold the buzz to the end, not say it louder. The sound is not missing from Vietnamese — /ɡ/ is a perfectly ordinary onset. It is missing from that position.

So expect it to take a few goes. You are not fixing a sound you cannot make — you are putting a sound you make easily into a place your mouth has never had to put it.

Tiếng Việt: Âm tiết tiếng Việt chỉ khép lại bằng /p t k m n ŋ j w/, và không có phụ âm hữu thanh nào đứng cuối. Vì vậy /ɡ/ cuối từ trong tiếng Anh nằm ngoài hệ thống có sẵn, và dễ trượt về /k/.
