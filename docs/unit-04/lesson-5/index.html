Skills 1: Reading & Speaking

Reading — The house that stands on legs

Tí, 13, writes down the Saturday he went up the river with ten questions.

ReadingOne clock — 18 minutes

You have 18 minutes for the text and every exercise below it. The clock covers everything: reading the text, finding the answers and typing them in. It does not stop while you type, and there is no extra time at the end.

Reading passage

AWe walked the old path for two hours and passed nothing — no village, no gate, no person. Ten questions in my pocket and nobody to ask them. What do you put in an answer column when there is nobody on the path? So I sat on the bank and waited for the water to turn, and Bống said the two words. This far up the river it still turns, twice a day, the same as at home. The bamboo across the water moved and stopped, because a house was standing in it: a stilt house, clear of the ground on tall wooden legs, with a wooden staircase to the door.

BThảo got there before I did. "Who built it? How long does wood like that last?" Neither of those was on my sheet.

CIt was built of wood and bamboo, and the roof was palm leaves. Underneath it people had kept tools and firewood and, by the smell, animals, and the floor above was dry, the way a floor stays dry in the rainy season. Cool air moved under it while we stood in the sun. Inside was one large room, an open fire in the middle, three baskets and a gong on the wall. How many people sleep around one fire? How much firewood does a room like that need in a wet month?

D"The sea gives back what it took," Bống said. "You don't choose which, and it comes back the way it went. That is four marks on our wall now." Cô Yến's sheet says there are fifty-four ethnic groups, that the Kinh are the majority, and that the other fifty-three are ethnic minorities. Which of the fifty-three put this house here? The sheet does not say, and neither did the house. Down in Bãi Sẻ the new houses go up in a month in brick and concrete; a few villages upriver have begun to restore their old ones. Nobody restored this one. Cô Yến calls a house like that heritage, and I did not understand the word until Saturday.

EThen I asked it my questions. Who lived here? How many people slept in this room? How much rice did they grow, and how many years did they grow it for? Thảo left the answer column empty. Two of the baskets were half finished. Somebody had put the work down meaning to come back to it.

5.1

True, False, or Not Given

ReadingTrue/False/Not given

False means the text says the opposite. Not Given means the text does not say either way — and wanting to answer from what you already know about ethnic groups is exactly the pull this type is built to catch.

  • One mark each. Nothing is part-marked.
  • One answer per item — a second one scores zero.
  1. T
  2. F (the Kinh are the majority)
  3. F (it was built of wood and bamboo; brick and concrete are the new houses down in Bãi Sẻ)
  4. T
  5. NG (the writer asks how much rice they grew, and the text never answers)
  6. NG (the text never says what kind of home the writer lives in)

Speaking — Talking about homes

Working alone: You have no partner, so do this in three steps. It still works — speaking practice is mostly about producing language out loud.

Step 1 — Prepare. Write short notes (not full sentences) for each question.

Question Your notes
What kind of home do you live in?
How many rooms does it have, and who lives there?
What is your home made of?
What are two advantages of a stilt house?
Would you like to live in one? Why / why not?

Step 2 — Speak. Answer each question aloud from your notes, in full sentences. Then ask the five questions aloud as if you were interviewing someone — an interview is questions, and questions are this unit's grammar.

Step 3 — Record and check. Record yourself on your phone. Listen back and check three things:

  • Did every Wh- question have the right word order — *Wh- + operator + subject
  • verb? And did your subject questions correctly have no do/does*?
  • Did you say How many rooms but How much rice?
  • Did /k/ and /ɡ/ sound different in coat and goat, custom and gong?

Repeat Step 2 until each answer runs clearly from its first word to its last.

Go further

Answer, then develop — two beats for every reply

Build every peer-interview answer on two beats, rather than on length:

  1. Beat one — one sentence that answers the question that was asked.
  2. Beat two — one sentence that extends it: a reason, an example, a contrast.

One-sentence answers stall a conversation: your partner has to think of a whole new question every time. The second beat is what keeps it going, and it is a habit, not a trick — build it in until you stop noticing you are doing it.

Tiếng Việt: Mỗi câu trả lời gồm hai nhịp: một câu trả lời đúng câu hỏi, một câu mở rộng. Đây là thói quen giao tiếp cần luyện thành phản xạ.
