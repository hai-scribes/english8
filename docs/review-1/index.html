⚠️ Bẫy thường gặp: more earlier và frequentlier đều sai. Từ ngắn thì thêm -er, từ dài thì dùng more — không bao giờ dùng cả hai, và cũng không tự thêm -er vào những từ kết thúc bằng -ly.

  2. c (after enjoy the verb takes -ing)
  3. a (after decide the verb takes to)
  4. c (frequently is a long adverb, so it takes more, and it stays an adverb)
  5. b (after detest the verb takes -ing)

Reading — Three Saturdays

From the school magazine of Nguyen Du Secondary School.

ReadingOne clock — 18 minutes

You have 18 minutes for the text and all three exercises under it. The clock covers everything: reading the text, finding the answers and typing them in. It does not stop while you type, and there is no extra time at the end.

Reading passage

AOur school magazine asked three students in Year 8 the same question: what do you do on a Saturday? We expected three answers that were more or less alike. The three of them live within twenty kilometres of each other, and their Saturdays have almost nothing in common.

BMai's Saturday begins at half past five. She lives in a village where her family keeps poultry and a small orchard, and before breakfast she has collected the eggs and carried water out to the barn. The work is finished by ten. After that the day belongs to her: she and her cousins take a kite up to the dyke and stay there until the light goes. "People tell me the countryside is boring," she says. "It is not boring. The day is simply longer here."

CDuy lives above his family's shop in the town. He is crazy about board games, and last year, in the middle of a week of tests, he joined the school chess club because a teacher told him it would help. He did not believe her. He now plays every Saturday morning, and he says he is far more self-confident than he was in September. He also sleeps better in any week when he has played.

DLinh spends most of Saturday indoors, and she is not sorry about it. Her grandmother taught her to make bracelets out of old cloth, and she sells them at the market on the first Sunday of every month. One bracelet takes about two hours. "It is slow," she says, "but nothing else empties my head like it."

EMs Hoa, who edits the magazine, noticed one thing that all three answers share. Not one of these Saturdays happens on a screen. "The students who take up something outside lessons seem to keep up with their schoolwork better," she says. "I cannot prove that. But I have stopped being surprised by it."

Speaking — Saturday, and where you spend it

Working alone: there is no partner here, so do this in three steps. It still works — most of the value is in producing the language out loud.

Step 1 — Prepare. Think of one person you know well: a cousin, a neighbour, an older brother or sister. Make short notes, not sentences.

Question Your notes
What does this person do on a Saturday?
Where — indoors, in town, outside the village?
How long have they been doing it?
What do they like about it?
Is there anything they can't stand about it?
Do they do it more often now than a year ago?

Step 2 — Speak. Answer each question aloud, in full sentences. Use at least one verb of liking or disliking in every answer — be into, be keen on, be fond of, can't stand, detest — and remember which of them are followed by -ing.

Step 3 — Record and check. Record yourself on your phone and listen back once. Three things to listen for:

  • Did you use a comparative anywhere — earlier than, more often than?
  • Did you join any two ideas with and, but, so, or or yet, or was every sentence short and separate?
  • Did good and food come out with different vowel lengths?

Repeat Step 2 until each answer runs from its first word to its last without stopping.

The voice is your device's speech synthesiser. It says the words clearly, but it is not a real speaker: no accent range, and none of the run-together sounds of natural speech.

Script

Tom: You have never lived in a city, have you, Mai?

Mai: No. And you have never spent a whole week in a village, so we are even.

Tom: All right. Tell me about a Saturday.

Mai: I am up at half past five. I feed the poultry and carry water out to the barn, and by ten the work is finished. After that the day is mine. My cousins and I take a kite up to the dyke and stay there until the light goes.

Tom: I am asleep until nine. I live in the middle of town, above my father's shop, so everything is close. In the afternoon I meet my friends at the shopping centre and we walk around and talk. We hardly spend anything.

Mai: That is the part I could not stand. All those people.

Tom: And the part I could not stand in a village would be the quiet.

Mai: It is not quiet. The noise is birds instead of traffic, that is all. People tell me the countryside is boring. It is not boring — the day is simply longer here.

Tom: Is there anything you miss?

Mai: A library. The nearest one is in town, so I get there twice a month.

Tom: I would miss space. Our flat has no garden, not even a balcony.

Writing — A family member's free time

Task. Write one paragraph of 80–100 words about the free-time activities of one member of your family. Answer three questions inside the paragraph: what do they do, where and how often, and why do they like it.

Model paragraph — read this first, then cover it

My uncle Thanh lives in a village outside Hue, and his free time is nothing like mine. He is crazy about fishing, so every Saturday he takes his rod down to the stream behind the orchard and stays there most of the morning. In the afternoon he hangs out with his neighbours under the trees, and they play a long, slow board game that I have never understood. He detests television. My aunt says he sleeps better than anyone else in the family, and I believe her, because he is outdoors from six until dark.
