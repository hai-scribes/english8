Reading — Two ideas that are changing everyday life

An article from a school science magazine.

ReadingOne clock — 19 minutes

You have 19 minutes for the text and every exercise below it. The clock covers everything: reading the text, finding the answers and typing them in. It does not stop while you type, and there is no extra time at the end.

Reading passage

Think about the last time you unlocked your phone. You probably did not type a password. You put your thumb on a small square, or you simply looked at the screen and it opened. That is biometrics: technology that identifies a person by a part of the body instead of by something the person has to remember.

Fingerprints are the most familiar example, but they are not the only one. Some devices use face recognition, and some read the iris, the coloured ring around the pupil of the eye. The idea behind all of them is the same. A password can be stolen or forgotten. A body is much harder to copy.

Biometrics is not perfect. A cut finger, a new pair of glasses or a very dark room can confuse a scanner. There are also questions about privacy. Once a company stores your face, you cannot change it the way you change a password.

A second idea is changing the way we study. Nanolearning breaks a subject into very short lessons, often two or three minutes long, with one clear point in each. Instead of sitting through a whole hour, you learn one thing, use it, and stop. Learners who are busy, or who lose concentration easily, often find this easier than a long class.

Nanolearning has a drawback too. Short bursts are excellent for facts and small skills, but a difficult idea still needs long, slow, uncomfortable thinking. No app removes that.

Speaking — The technology you use, and why

Working alone: You have no partner, so do this in three steps. It still works — speaking practice is mostly about producing language out loud.

Step 1 — Prepare. Write short notes (not full sentences) for each question.

Question Your notes
Which device do you use most, and what for?
What is its main benefit?
What is its biggest drawback?
Which app or website helps you study? Why?
What has a teacher or parent said to you about screen time?
Would you like a robot teacher? Why / why not?

Step 2 — Speak. Answer each question aloud from your notes, in full sentences. Every answer must contain a reason (because…, so…). For the fifth question you must use reported speech: My mother told me that…

Step 3 — Record and check. Record yourself on your phone. Listen back and check three things:

  • Did you use say and tell correctly — no person after said, a person after told?
  • Did you stress the content words and squeeze the function words, instead of reading every word with the same weight?
  • Did your pauses fall between points rather than inside a sentence?

Repeat Step 2 until each answer runs clearly from its first word to its last.
