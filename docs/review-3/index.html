Reading — The market that moved uphill

From a local newspaper, translated for the school magazine.

ReadingOne clock — 18 minutes

You have 18 minutes for the text and all three exercises under it. The clock covers everything: reading the text, finding the answers and typing them in. It does not stop while you type, and there is no extra time at the end.

Reading passage

ATwo Novembers ago, a storm sat over the coast for eleven hours and the river behind Cua Lo came over its bank in the middle of the night. By four in the morning the water in the market was as high as a person's waist. Nobody was hurt. The warning had reached the town at nine the evening before, and most of the traders had already gone home.

BThe damage was counted over the following week. Sixty-two stalls were ruined. Most of the stock was ruined with them — rice, cloth, dried fish, everything stored at floor level — and the wooden roof over the eastern half collapsed under the weight of the water it had held. The market had stood on that ground for ninety years.

CThe council expected to rebuild in the same place. The traders did not. After two long meetings they voted to move the whole market forty metres up the slope, onto a concrete platform a metre above the old floor. It took eight months and it cost almost twice as much as a repair, and the traders paid a third of the difference themselves.

DA second change came with the new roof, and nobody had expected this one. The council banned single-use plastic bags inside the market. The traders were against it: their customers complained, and for a month people carried their vegetables home in their hands. Then every stall was given two hundred cloth bags to hand out. Shoppers now bring their own, and the drains no longer block.

EAsk a trader whether it was worth it and you get a practical answer. The prices are what they always were, and you can still bargain for anything except rice. The market opens at five and closes at eleven, as it always has. And when the river came up again last September, the floor of the market stayed dry.

Speaking — Where you buy things, and what it costs

Working alone: three steps, out loud. A recording is enough of an audience.

Step 1 — Prepare. Think about the last four things your family bought. Notes only.

Question Your notes
Where were they bought — market, shop, centre, online?
Which was the cheapest place, and which the most convenient?
What came wrapped in plastic?
Has anything about the way you shop changed in two years?
Is there a shop near you that a storm or a flood would reach?

Step 2 — Speak. Answer each one aloud, in full sentences. Use at least one time clause in each answer — before we go, until the delivery arrives, as soon as the market opens.

Step 3 — Record and check. Listen back once:

  • Did the time clause keep its own subject and verb, or did it collapse into a phrase?
  • Did you say store and speciality with one syllable at the start, not two?
  • Which of national, natural and arrival did you stress correctly?

First you hear a short spoken introduction — it is not written down, so listen to it. Then you get 30 seconds to read the questions, and then the recording plays once.

Practising does not use up your one play. When you want the real thing, take it once below.

The voice is your device's speech synthesiser. It says the words clearly, but it is not a real speaker: no accent range, and none of the run-together sounds of natural speech.

Script

Good morning. I want to talk about the air in this city, and about one thing in particular that surprises people.

Ask anybody what makes the air dirty and they will say traffic. They are not wrong. Exhaust fumes from cars and motorbikes are the largest single source, and on a still day you can see the result lying over the main road.

But transport is not the only reason. A great deal of what we breathe comes from building sites — dust off the cement and the sand, thrown up again every time a lorry goes past. Some comes from factories outside the city, and some from people burning rubbish in their own yards, which is illegal and extremely common.

These gases are especially harmful for children. Children breathe faster than adults do, and their lungs are still growing. Doctors at the city hospital say they see far more asthma now than they saw ten years ago.

And here is the part that surprises people. Less of today's pollution comes from factories than in the past — the old ones have closed or moved away. More of it comes from us: from what we drive, what we build, and what we burn in the yard on a Sunday.

Writing — Where your family shops, and why

Task. Write one paragraph of 80–100 words about where your family shops and why. Say something about at least two of these: how convenient the place is, the prices, how fresh the food is, and what the shopping comes wrapped in.

Model paragraph — read this first, then cover it

My family shops in three different places, and each one is for something different. My mother buys vegetables and fish at the farmers' market near our house, because they are fresher than anything in the shopping centre and she can bargain a little. Once a month we go to the shopping centre for the heavy things that come cheaper in a large pack. My father orders his books online, so they arrive in two days. The one thing I would change is the single-use plastic every small purchase still comes in.
