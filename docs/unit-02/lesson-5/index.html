Skills 1: Reading & Speaking

Reading — The old way to Bến Vàng

Tí, 13, writes about a road he did not take.

ReadingOne clock — 17 minutes

Reading passage

I missed the ten o'clock bus to Bến Vàng by four minutes. The next one was at two, and my grandmother had said before dark twice, in the voice she keeps for when she means it.

So I sat down at the edge of a paddy field with the rice sack in my lap and did the only stupid thing I had left. I lit the lamp and said, "Come back."

Mun climbed out and looked at the vast green fields with no interest at all.

"You know the rule by now," he said. "Light it, and the nearest lost thing comes back. You don't choose which. Ten measures after this one."

Nothing happened for a long minute. Then the bamboo behind me moved, and there was a gap in it, and behind the gap there was a path.

It was narrow and hard and swept clean, as if a hundred people had walked it that morning. It ran along the stream, climbed the hill far more steeply than any road climbs anything, and dropped down the other side.

I took it. It was shorter than the road by half, and it carried me over the top so much more quickly than the two o'clock bus that I reached my uncle's gate with dry shoes and shaking legs.

Chú Bảy took the medicine. Then he looked at the board game under my arm for a while and did not ask me where it came from.

"I was bottom of my class every year I was in it," he said. "I made that board instead of my homework, and I am forty-six, and I am perfectly all right. Tell your grandmother that."

Then he looked past me at the hill for a long time.

"Which way did you come?" he asked.

"The road," I said.

"You came more quickly than the road allows," he said.

5.1

True, False, or Not Given

ReadingTrue/False/Not given

False means the text says the opposite. Not Given means the text does not say either way — and wanting to answer from what you know about villages is exactly the pull this type is built to catch.

  • One mark each. Nothing is part-marked.
  • One answer per item — a second one scores zero.
  1. T ("by four minutes")
  2. F (he looked at them "with no interest at all")
  3. F (it was hard and swept clean, as if a hundred people had walked it that morning)
  4. NG (the text never says either way)
  5. F (his uncle says he came more quickly than the road allows)

Speaking — Describing a village or town you know

Working alone: You have no partner, so do this in three steps. It still works — speaking practice is mostly about producing language out loud.

Step 1 — Prepare. Choose one village or small town you actually know: your grandparents' place, somewhere you visited, or the area where you live. Write short notes (not full sentences).

Question Your notes
Where is it, and how do you get there?
What can you see when you arrive?
What do people do there for a living?
How is it different from a big city? (use a comparative adverb)
What do you like most, and least, about it?

Step 2 — Speak. Answer each question aloud from your notes, in full sentences. Use at least three comparative adverbs across your answers, and at least four words from the Lesson 2 table.

Step 3 — Record and check. Record yourself on your phone. Listen back and check three things:

  • Did you use -er with short adverbs and more with -ly adverbs — and never both together?
  • Did you say better and worse, not more good / more bad?
  • Did your pauses fall between points rather than inside a sentence?

Repeat Step 2 until each answer runs clearly from its first word to its last.
