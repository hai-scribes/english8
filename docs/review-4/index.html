Reading — Science Week

A report written by two Year 8 students for the school noticeboard.

ReadingOne clock — 18 minutes

You have 18 minutes for the text and all three exercises under it. The clock covers everything: reading the text, finding the answers and typing them in. It does not stop while you type, and there is no extra time at the end.

Reading passage

AScience Week ran from Monday to Friday at the start of April, and for once almost nothing happened in a classroom. Four events were open to Year 8, and between them they took up most of the week. Two hundred and ten students signed up, which is more than the school had planned for, so the library talk had to be given twice.

BMonday's talk was about the ways people may communicate in fifty years' time. The speaker, an engineer from a company in Da Nang, was cheerfully unsure about most of it. She thought holographic calls would arrive first, that a reliable translation machine was further away than people imagine, and that telepathy, which several students asked about, is not something any laboratory is close to. What impressed us was how often she said she did not know.

COn Wednesday the debating club argued about robot teachers. The side against them won, but not with the argument we expected. Nobody claimed a machine could not explain a rule clearly. Their case was that a robot cannot tell the difference between a student who is quiet because the lesson is easy and a student who is quiet because it is not.

DThursday evening was the telescope. Twenty of us stayed until nine on the football pitch with two borrowed instruments. The sky was clear enough to see the four largest moons of Jupiter, which look like a short line of dots beside it. Our physics teacher said the same four dots are what convinced people four hundred years ago that not everything goes round the Earth.

EThe poster contest closed on Friday. Each entry had to describe one planet and say honestly whether anything could survive there. The winning poster was about Venus, and it was mostly a list of reasons why nothing could. The judges said afterwards that most of the other entries had made their planet sound far too welcoming.

Speaking — The device you actually use

Working alone: three steps, out loud. Record yourself; that is the listener.

Step 1 — Prepare. Choose one device or one app you use for studying. Notes, not sentences.

Question Your notes
What is it, and what do you use it for?
How often — every day, once a week?
What does it let you do that nothing else does?
What goes wrong with it?
Will you still be using it in five years?

Step 2 — Speak. Answer each one aloud, in full sentences. Then do it a second time in reported speech, as if you were telling somebody else what you had just said: I said that I used it every evening…

Step 3 — Record and check. Listen back once:

  • In the reported version, did the tense step back, and did the question word order go back to normal?
  • Did the stressed words in your questions carry the meaning, or did you stress do, will and is?
  • When you listed things, did your voice fall on the last item?

Script

Hello, everyone. My name is Mark and I look after the planetarium here. Before we go inside, let me take you round the solar system in about two minutes.

Eight planets go round our Sun, and many of them have moons. Jupiter has dozens of them.

The four inner planets — Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars — are quite small, and they have rocky surfaces. You could in principle stand on any of them, though I would not advise it on Venus.

Between Mars and Jupiter lies the asteroid belt, and it holds millions of rocky objects. Some are the size of a house. One or two are the size of a small country.

The outer planets are a different thing altogether. They are enormous, and they are mostly made up of gas rather than rock, so there is no surface to land on at all. They all have thick layers of cloud around them, and what is underneath the cloud nobody is entirely sure about.

We are looking for life, naturally. What life seems to need is liquid water — which is why, just now, the moons interest us rather more than the planets do.

Writing — Your favourite means of communication

Task. Write one paragraph of 80–100 words about your favourite means of communication. Answer four things inside the paragraph: what it is, how often you use it, what is good about it, and whether you think you will still be using it in the future.

Model paragraph — read this first, then cover it

My favourite means of communication is the video call. I use it almost every evening, because my sister studies in Da Nang and a written message is not the same as a face. The best thing about it is that I can see she is tired before she tells me she is fine. It is not perfect: the connection drops whenever it rains, and we talk over each other constantly. Even so, I think I will still be using it in ten years, and no device will replace it for me.
