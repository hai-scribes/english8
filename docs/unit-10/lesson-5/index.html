Skills 1: Reading & Speaking

Reading — *Will we talk without speaking?*

An interview from a school magazine. Linh, 14, talks to Dr Bảo, a brain researcher.

Linh: Dr Bảo, thank you for coming. Some people say that in fifty years we will not need phones at all. Is that possible?

Dr Bảo: It is possible, but not in the way films imagine. Telepathy in films means one person reading another person's private thoughts. That is not what scientists are working on.

Linh: What are they working on, then?

Dr Bảo: A small device that sits above the ear. It reads the electrical signals your brain makes when you plan to say a word. The device translates those signals into text and sends the text across a network. Your friend's device turns the text back into a voice. Nobody reads your thoughts. You still choose every word.

Linh: So it is really a very fast text message?

Dr Bảo: Exactly. A silent one.

Linh: Would that be better than a video call?

Dr Bảo: Faster, certainly. Better, I am not sure. When we talk face-to-face we send hundreds of small signals — a gesture, a pause, a look at the wrong moment. Those signals carry about half the meaning. A machine that sends only words would lose the other half.

Linh: What worries you most?

Dr Bảo: Privacy. If a device can read a signal before you speak, someone must decide who owns that signal. My answer is simple: it is yours. It belongs to nobody else.

Linh: And when will we see it in the shops?

Dr Bảo: In the laboratory, now. In your pocket — not in my lifetime, and possibly not in yours.

5.1

True, False, or Not Given

Reading item typeTrue/False/Not given

False means the interview says the opposite. Not Given means it does not say either way — and answering from what you already believe about brain research is exactly the pull this type is built to catch.

  • One mark each. Nothing is part-marked.
  • One answer per item — a second one scores zero.

One of the official eleven Reading question types, marked by the published rules rather than by ours.

  1. F (he says that is not what scientists are working on)
  2. T
  3. T
  4. F (he says such a machine would lose about half the meaning)
  5. NG (the interview never mentions testing on anybody)
5.2

Answer the questions

Reading item typeShort-answer questions

Take the words from the interview. Do not rephrase them — this is the one place in the course where using your own words costs you the mark.

Write NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS for each answer.

  • One mark each. Nothing is part-marked.
  • Spelling and grammar mistakes lose the mark — here, because they do there.
  • British and American spellings are both accepted.
  • A hyphenated word counts as one word.
  • Two answers in one gap score zero, even if one of them is right.

One of the official eleven Reading question types, marked by the published rules rather than by ours.

  2. text
  3. (a) network
  4. (about) half
  5. privacy

Speaking — The pros and cons of online calls

Working alone: You have no partner, so do this in three steps. It still works — speaking practice is mostly about producing language out loud.

Step 1 — Prepare. Write short notes (not full sentences) in both columns.

Pros (Ưu điểm) Cons (Nhược điểm)
Cost / distance
Time
Feelings and body language
Technical problems
Family and friends far away

Step 2 — Speak. Talk for one minute using this frame:

"An online call has two big advantages. First, … Second, … On the other hand, there are problems. One is … Another is … In my opinion, an online call is better than / worse than a face-to-face meeting when …"

Step 3 — Record and check. Record yourself on your phone. Listen back and check three things:

  • Did you use in / on / at correctly every time you gave a day or a time?
  • Did you say mine, hers, theirs with no noun after them?
  • Did you stress the last syllable in Vietnamese, Japanese, guarantee?

Repeat Step 2 until you can speak for a full minute with your pauses falling between stages rather than inside a sentence.
