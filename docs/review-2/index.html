Reading passage

ABan Lac sits in a valley in Mai Chau, three hours west of Ha Noi. Almost everyone in it belongs to the White Thai, one of the fifty-four ethnic groups of Viet Nam. Thirty years ago a visitor was an event. Today the village takes in guests almost every weekend, and it has changed the place in ways nobody planned.

BThe houses are the first thing anyone notices. Each one is a stilt house: the family lives on a wooden floor a metre and a half above the ground, and the space underneath holds the tools, the looms and, in the cold months, the livestock. Guests sleep on the same floor as the family, under one long roof. There are no separate rooms for them, and the villagers see no reason to build any.

CMrs Ha, who is sixty-one, weaves under her house most mornings. Her mother taught her, and her mother's mother taught her mother. One scarf takes about three days. For twenty years almost nobody young wanted to learn, because the work was slow and nothing came of it. That has quietly reversed: her two nieces weave now, and they sell what they make to the people staying upstairs.

DThe village year still turns on its own calendar. Before the new rice is eaten, every family prepares an offering, an elder burns incense and prays for a good year, and in the evening the drums start. The drums are not only music. They are there to chase away bad spirits, and even the families who say they no longer believe that turn up and beat them.

EAsk what has changed and the answers are ordinary. There is a road. There is a signal on the hill, so the teenagers have as much screen time as anyone. Four of Mrs Ha's grandchildren have moved to Ha Noi. But she is not gloomy about it. "A custom that is useful survives," she says. "We found a use for ours."

Speaking — One custom, two generations

Working alone: three steps, out loud. A listener is welcome but not needed.

Step 1 — Prepare. Choose one custom your family keeps: a festival day, a meal, a visit, a thing nobody is allowed to do. Make notes, not sentences.

Question Your notes
What is the custom, and when does it happen?
Who gets together for it?
What did your grandparents do that your parents do not?
What do you think you will keep when you are older?
What would be lost if it stopped?

Step 2 — Speak. Answer each question aloud in full sentences. Somewhere in your answers, use one first conditional — If nobody cooks it, … — and one sentence with unless.

Step 3 — Record and check. Record yourself and listen back once:

  • After if and unless, did you use the present simple, or did will slip in where it does not belong?
  • Did you say the where the sentence needed nothing at all?
  • Are sing and thin still ending differently?

Script

Hello. My name is Phong, and I have lived in the same village in the north all my life, so I am the wrong person to ask about cities.

My family belongs to the Tay ethnic group. My grandmother still weaves, and she is teaching my sister, though my sister says the loom is much harder than it looks. At Tet the whole family comes back, even the cousins who work far away, and we cook together for two days before anybody eats.

What has changed is the ordinary week rather than the festivals. My mother used to walk to the market every single morning. Now she orders rice and oil on her phone and a van brings them on Thursday. My father used to walk out to the fields as well; he rides a motorbike now, and he is home an hour earlier.

I am not sure all of it is better. We used to eat together every evening. Now my brother eats in front of a screen, and my grandmother minds that far more than she minds the motorbike.

Writing — A custom, and a life that has changed around it

Task. Write one paragraph of 80–100 words about one custom your family keeps and how the daily life around it has changed. Answer three things inside the paragraph: what the custom is, who keeps it, and what is different now from your grandparents' time.

Model paragraph — read this first, then cover it

On the day before Tet, my family gets together at my grandmother's house to wrap banh chung. She says the custom has been passed down through four generations, and she will not let anybody near it with a machine. My mother's lifestyle is nothing like hers: she works in an office in Da Nang, she cooks in twenty minutes, and she has never lit a fire in a yard. Yet on that one afternoon the two of them do exactly the same thing, and I think that is why the tradition is still alive.
