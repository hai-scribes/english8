## 1 · Getting Started — "Lifestyle differences" (track 32)

**Dialogue.** Nam and Tom compare the lifestyle in Viet Nam with Tom's country.
Students here call teachers by their **title** — "teacher" — not by name; in
Tom's country they say "Hello" or "Good morning" plus **Mr, Mrs or Miss** and the
**surname**. People here buy and sell a lot of **street food**; in Tom's country
people usually buy food in a **store or a restaurant**. Buying street food is a
**common practice** in Nam's city. Many people have **breakfast on the street**;
in Tom's country they typically have a **light breakfast at home**. Many adults
here are **in the habit of** having breakfast outside their homes, and if they're
not **in a hurry** they'll even have a **leisurely** coffee there.

## 3 · A Closer Look 2 — Future simple and first conditional (book pp. 63–64)

> **Remember!** (future simple)
> We use the future simple to describe future possibilities or conditions.
> *We have to leave now, or we **will miss** the train.*
> *If I fail the test, I **will have to** take the course again.*

> **Remember!** (*unless*)
> We can also use ***unless*** in conditional sentences. *Unless* means
> "if … not" or "except if …". *You will fail the test **unless** you study harder.*

## 5 · Skills 1 (book pp. 65–66)

### Reading — the lifestyle of Alaska

**Ex 1** — group lead-in: what do you know about Alaska (location, climate,
native people, culture)? Then read and check.

**Text.** Three paragraphs, ~200 words. Alaska is quite large — nearly **1.7
million square kilometres** — with a small population of about **730,000**. The
native peoples still **maintain** many of their traditions, keeping old ways of
making arts and crafts alive; various native groups have their own special
**styles** of carving or weaving as well as unique **tribal dances** and drumming,
so visitors may **experience** some of their culture in the villages, and see
traditional music and **native art** in galleries and museums. Alaska is also
known for an unusual method of transport — the **dogsled**. Today dog sledding
(= mushing) is more a sport than a true means of transport. The best-known race
is the **Iditarod Trail Sled Dog Race**, **1,510 km** from **Anchorage to Nome**;
**mushers** from all over the world come to Anchorage each **March** to compete
for cash and prizes.
