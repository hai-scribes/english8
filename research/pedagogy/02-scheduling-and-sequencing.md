# 02 — Scheduling: what to mix, and when to review

**What this file establishes.** Whether to interleave or block practice material,
and whether any concrete review-day offset is warranted. The first question has
a real result pointing one way for adults and the **opposite** way for the
adolescents this site serves; the second remains the open GAP the IELTS base
already recorded.

This file does **not** revisit spacing-versus-massing or expanding-versus-uniform
intervals. Both are settled in `../ielts/09-design-principles.md` §2.1 and are
not re-litigated here.

---

## §1 Interleaving beat blocking — on adults, on a receptive test, once

Nakata & Suzuki (2019), *Modern Language Journal* 103(3):629–647, DOI
10.1111/modl.12581. Randomised, 115 analysed of 151 recruited (39 blocked / 40
increasing / 36 interleaved), Japanese EFL learners aged 18–22, placed by TOEIC
Bridge "between the A2 (elementary) and B1 (intermediate) levels in the CEFR
benchmark". Material: 50 multiple-choice fill-in-the-blank items with L1 glosses
over five structures — simple past, present perfect, and the first, second and
third conditionals. One training session, plus an unannounced delayed test a
week later.

**The result** **[S — 3-0 on the reported figures; see §1.1 on why not [V]]**:

> "on the delayed posttest, the main effect of condition was significant,
> F(2,107) = 4.512, p = .013, ηp2 = .078… the interleaved group significantly
> outperformed the blocked group (p = .021), and a close-to-medium effect size
> was observed (**d = 0.64**)."

Adjusted d-prime: interleaved 2.07 > increasing 1.86 > blocked 1.60. On the
immediate posttest, nothing: "neither the main effect of condition nor the
interaction between condition and pretest was significant" (0.02 < d < 0.51).

**Two companion findings make it a desirable-difficulty result rather than
merely a win.** Interleaving produced the *worst* in-session accuracy —
**77.0% vs 87.2%** correct during training, blocked over interleaved d = 0.83
**[V 3-0]** — and learners could not tell the schedules apart, rating all three
equally effective (5.31 / 5.61 / 5.63 on a 7-point scale; F = 0.820, p = .443,
η² = .014) **[V 3-0]**. Those ratings were collected *after* the immediate
posttest but *before* the delayed one, so no feedback channel existed to correct
them.

### §1.1 Why this is marked [S] and what it does not license

Verification sustained the figures but found the claim as usually stated
overreaches. Carry all of this:

- **The effect is fragile.** The omnibus mixed ANCOVA showed **no significant
  main effect of Condition** (F = 2.122, p = .125) and only a marginal
  Time × Condition interaction (p = .064). The delayed result comes from
  follow-ups probing a marginal interaction.
- **Only 1 of 3 pairwise contrasts was significant** (blocked vs increasing
  p = .339; increasing vs interleaved p = .667), and the paper's own second
  hypothesis — that blocked-then-interleaved beats both — was **not supported**.
  So "interleaving produced the best retention" is wrong; the safe statement is
  **the schedule with the worst in-session accuracy beat blocking a week later**.
- **d = 0.64 is ANCOVA-adjusted** for three covariates, not a raw contrast.
- 24% post-hoc attrition.
- **The manipulation mixed five *structures* inside one exercise format.** It is
  not evidence for mixing exercise types, task formats or skills, and must never
  be restated that way.
- **The outcome is an untimed receptive grammaticality-judgment test.** It
  warrants sequencing of judgment-type grammar exercises, not production.
- The practice-phase percentages could not be seen in an open copy of the paper.

Two moderators worth keeping: lower-pretest learners gained *more* from
interleaving (r = −.420, p = .009, exploratory, and the authors call the related
test marginal at p = .083); and Pan et al. (2018) found the blocked/interleaved
difference **only across multiple sessions, not within one** — the opposite
arrangement to how this study demonstrated it.

## §2 The adolescent counter-finding — direction currently undetermined

The population mismatch is the decisive problem, not a footnote. Nakata & Suzuki
are university adults. Verification surfaced **Kim (2024), *Language Learning***,
which found the **opposite direction for low-achieving adolescents** on
vocabulary: interleaving alone acted as an *undesirable* difficulty, and
blocked-then-interleaved won **[S]**.

The target learner is precisely the case where the two studies disagree — an
adolescent with low prior knowledge.

> **GAP** — **Whether to interleave or block for a 13–14-year-old at A2 is
> genuinely undetermined.** One study says interleave (adults, receptive
> grammar); one says block first (adolescents, vocabulary). This repo may not
> adopt a mixing policy from this evidence. Any rule it does adopt is **[SPEC]**
> and needs a dedicated research pass on adolescent interleaving first.

What *does* survive, and is worth holding regardless of which way the mixing
question resolves, is the pair of meta-findings in §1: **in-session accuracy is
not a proxy for retention, and the learner's felt sense of a schedule is not a
design input.** Both are marked **[V 3-0]** and both are about measurement
rather than about interleaving.

## §3 Concrete day offsets — the GAP stands, unclosed

`../ielts/09-design-principles.md` §2.1 records that no evidence supplies
absolute interval values ("review at 1, 3, 7, 21 days"). **This pass did not
close it.** Both candidate findings that would have closed it were refuted 0-3:

- **Suzuki (2019)**, four one-hour sessions 3.3 days apart beating the same four
  7 days apart on oral production **[X]** — refuted for stripped hedges, a
  cherry-picked side of the result, and a provenance failure (the quoted
  sentence is not the cited paper's finding as claimed).
- **Cepeda et al. (2008)**, the "optimal inter-session interval is 8–43% of the
  retention interval" rule **[X]** — refuted as a generalisation, with more
  recent L2 grammar work contradicting its transfer.

Neither claim nor its negation is asserted. **Nothing here licenses "review on
day 2 and day 7" over any other schedule.**

### §3.1 The one authentic-classroom test, and the line between its two halves

Rogers & Cheung (2020), *Studies in Second Language Acquisition* 43(5):1138–1156,
DOI 10.1017/S0272263120000236 — verified through the Crossref-deposited abstract
after Cambridge returned HTTP 429 repeatedly.

**The descriptive finding is sustained** **[V 4 voters, verbatim]**:

> "Following a pretest, treatment, delayed posttest design, 66 primary school
> students (Cantonese L1) studied 20 vocabulary items over three training
> episodes under spaced-short (1-day interval) or spaced-long (8-day interval)
> learning conditions… this study found large learning gains across the
> experimental conditions with no significant differences between the two
> learning schedules."

**The inference drawn from it is refuted** **[X]**. The rider "therefore there is
no evidential basis for preferring a specific day offset" was voted down,
because the study's own predecessor — Rogers & Cheung (2018) — found a *slight
advantage for the 1-day interval* under minimal overall learning. A null in one
replication does not establish equivalence across the window.

> This split is worth recording as a method note, because it is why an earlier
> synthesis of this same pass reported the study as confirmed and a later one
> reported it as refuted. **They were scoring different claims: the observation
> and the generalisation drawn from it.** The observation stands; the
> generalisation does not.

Three limits on the observation itself: it is a **null with no equivalence test**
(no TOST, no Bayes factor) and no schedule-contrast effect size at abstract
level; the population is **primary-school children, younger than this learner**;
and the authors' caution that adult laboratory results "might not generalize to
authentic child learning contexts" is *their inference on top of a null*, not a
demonstrated failure of transfer.

The counterweight is the L2 spacing meta-analysis already in the IELTS base
(Kim & Webb 2022, 98 effect sizes, 48 experiments, N = 3,411), which found
spacing itself medium-to-large and **shorter spacing less effective than longer
on delayed posttests**.

**The defensible statement is: no lab-derived optimal day offset is warranted for
a school-age schedule. Not: interval length is irrelevant.**

## §4 What this file does not establish

> **GAP** — **Optimal single-session length for adolescents.** Nothing was
> retrieved. The site's session length is an unevidenced design choice.

> **GAP** — **The daily-solo-plus-weekly-tutor architecture has no direct
> support.** No study tests short daily solo sessions combined with a weekly
> consolidation session with a tutor. The shape of this learner's week rests on
> reasoning, not warrant. See `05-tutoring.md` §5.

> **GAP** — **Whether interleaving transfers to productive work.** The one L2
> demonstration is a receptive judgment task. Nothing establishes the effect for
> speaking or writing.
