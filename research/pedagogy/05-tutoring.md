# 05 — The weekly session: what a non-expert tutor is worth

**What this file establishes.** That the headline tutoring effect does not
transfer to an untrained family tutor working with a lower-secondary learner, and
that parental involvement in *daily homework* carries no positive association to
lean on. What it does **not** establish is what the weekly hour should actually
contain — which was the question, and it is open.

Context for why this file exists: this learner studies alone on weekdays and with
her uncle on Saturdays.

---

## §1 Tutor type — the headline is not this tutor's number

Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan (2020), NBER Working Paper 27476, peer-reviewed
successor in *AERJ* (2024). Table 3A, pooled effects on standardised test scores
by tutor type **[V 3-0]**:

| Tutor type | Effect | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **All** | 0.37 [0.032]*** | 96 studies, 732 estimates |
| Teacher | **0.50** [0.075]*** | |
| Paraprofessional | **0.40** [0.040]*** | |
| **Non-professional** | **0.21** [0.064]*** | |
| **Parent** | **0.23** [0.114]* | 11 studies, p<.10, 95% CI ≈ 0.01–0.45 — the table's weakest cell |

> "Effects are stronger, on average, for teacher and paraprofessional tutoring
> programs than for nonprofessional and parent tutoring."

**The bare "0.37 SD" benchmark claim was refuted 0-3** **[X]** — it is a pooled
average across tutor types, settings and grades, and using it as the bar a
weekly family session should clear is exactly the misuse the panel rejected.
**The relevant cells are 0.21 and 0.23, not 0.37.**

**Scope limit that matters most here: the corpus contains no L2 or
foreign-language studies at all.** It is US PreK-12 reading and mathematics.

## §2 Effects attenuate with grade — measured through grade 5, extrapolated after

> "Effects also tend to be strongest among the earlier grades." **[S/NS — 2-1]**

The grades 6–11 pooled estimate is **0.16**, but three qualifications make it
close to unusable as a number:

- Only **7%** of interventions in the corpus involve grade 6 or above.
- There are "not enough studies… even to calculate a reliable standard error for
  the sixth grade and up pooled estimate" — so attenuation is *measured through
  grade 5 and extrapolated beyond it*.
- The attenuation is "explained entirely by literacy programs"; **mathematics
  shows the reverse**.

**Read as: the age band this learner is in is where this literature is thinnest,
and the direction of the grade effect is subject-dependent.**

## §3 Parental homework involvement carries no positive association

Xu, Guo, Feng, Ma, Zhang, Núñez & Fan (2024), *Psicothema* 36(1):1–14,
doi 10.7334/psicothema2023.92 — 28 studies, 32 independent samples, 252 effect
sizes, **378,222 participants** **[V 3-0]**:

> "an overall weak negative relationship between parental homework involvement
> and students' achievement (**r = −0.064**, p < 0.001)."

**The scope note is the whole point of this section and must never be dropped.**
This measures *a parent supervising or assisting with daily homework*. It does
**not** measure a scheduled weekly one-to-one session, which is a different
arrangement studied in a different literature (§1).

So it bears on **"should the daily solo assignments require adult help?"** —
no — and **not** on **"is a weekly session with her uncle worthwhile?"**, which
this file cannot answer either way.

It is also correlational, and the dominant explanation is **reverse causation**:
parents intervene more when a child is already struggling. It licenses *"there is
no positive association to lean on"*, never *"parent help harms"*.

## §4 The most useful thing in this literature was refuted, and needs re-verifying

The same meta-analysis decomposes involvement by *dimension*, and reports that
only **autonomy support** relates positively to achievement (r = 0.164, 95% CI
[0.114, 0.213]), with **content support** (direct help with the assignment's
content), **parental control/monitoring** and **involvement frequency** all null.

**This was voted down 0-3** and is therefore marked **[X]** — neither it nor its
negation is asserted.

> It is flagged here rather than buried because it is **the single most
> design-relevant statement in this area**: it would distinguish an uncle who
> supports independence from an uncle who supplies answers, which is precisely
> the design question. It appears in the meta-analysis's own moderator
> decomposition. **Treat it as unresolved rather than false, and re-verify it
> before any use.** It is the highest-value target for the next pass.

## §5 What this file does not establish

> **GAP** — **What a fluent-but-untrained adult should do with 60 minutes.** The
> tutoring literature answers *who tutors* and *how much*, and answers neither
> *what they should do* nor *what they should do in a language they speak but
> were never trained to teach*. Nothing retrieved addresses this.

> **GAP** — **Whether structured materials or scripting raises a non-professional
> tutor toward the paraprofessional 0.40.** This is the one lever a static site
> could actually pull — it could hand the uncle a prepared session — and nothing
> establishes whether pulling it works.

> **GAP** — **Productive and speaking practice for a learner with no daily
> conversation partner.** Nothing retrieved. Note that this repo separately
> forbids Speaking rubrics and Speaking feedback tooling
> (`../ielts/README.md` §5), so the gap and the prohibition compound: there is
> neither evidence for what to do nor permission to score it.

> **GAP** — **No direct support for the weekly-consolidation-plus-daily-solo
> architecture.** Restated from `02-scheduling-and-sequencing.md` §4 because it
> is as much a tutoring question as a scheduling one. The shape of this
> learner's week is a reasoned design choice, not an evidenced one.
