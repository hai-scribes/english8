## §1 "TBLT beats PPP" is not available as a build rule

The figure usually cited is **d = 0.93**, from Bryfonski & McKay (2019),
*Language Teaching Research* 23(5):603–632 **[S/NS — 2-1]**:

> "Findings based on a sample of 52 studies revealed an overall positive and
> strong effect (d = 0.93) for TBLT implementation on a variety of learning
> outcomes. The study further examined a range of programmatic and methodological
> features that moderated these main-effects (program region, institution type,
> needs analysis, and cycles of implementation)."

**That number does not mean what it is usually used to mean.** It is a pooled
effect over 52 heterogeneous studies of TBLT *implementation* — many of them
single-group pre-post — **not a head-to-head contrast with another method**. The
full text is closed access (Unpaywall `is_oa: false`), so no confidence interval,
no heterogeneity statistic and no per-moderator significance is verifiable, and
the moderator list arrives with **neither directions nor magnitudes** **[V 3-0]**.

The field's principal published criticism, surfaced through citation contexts:
Harris & Leeming (2022, *IRAL*, doi 10.1515/iral-2022-0082) fault the
meta-analysis for including studies that "(1) lacked a control group and/or
lacked or used different pre- and post-tests, (2) had different instructional
orientations" **[S]**.

> **Do not cite d = 0.93 as a warrant that task-based sequencing beats
> presentation-practice-production.** The pooled designs do not license the
> comparison, and a moderator list with no directions yields no design rule.

Three stronger claims — that *no* warranted TBLT effect size exists at all, that
the comparative subset was wholly unfit for purpose, and that the obstacle is
definitional (no consistent definition of "a task") — were each **refuted 0-3**
**[X]** and are logged so they are not re-derived. The absence of a usable
warrant is established; the stronger claim that the literature is worthless is
not.

## §2 The one head-to-head result, and its split

Harris & Leeming's own head-to-head study found the answer **depends on when you
measure** **[S]**: PPP better for **immediate** task performance, TBLT better for
**longer-term** development.

That split — not the pooled d — is the finding closest to actionable, and it
rhymes with the pattern in `02-scheduling-and-sequencing.md` §1: the arrangement
that looks better in the session is not the one that looks better later. It is a
single study and is marked accordingly.
