## 1. The test, exactly as it is

| Fact | Status | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 minutes total, **transfer time included** | **[C]** 3-0 | "Time allowed: 60 minutes (including transfer time)" ([Academic format — Reading](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-reading)) |
| **No extra transfer time** — unlike Listening | **[C]** 3-0 | "Unlike the Listening test, no extra transfer time is given." (same); "No extra time is allowed to copy answers to the answer sheet." ([Cambridge Academic FAQs](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/269898-ielts-academic-faqs.pdf)) |
| 3 sections, 40 questions | **[C]** 3-0 | "Number of sections: 3 … Number of questions: 40." (same) |
| Total text length 2150–2750 words | **[C]** 3-0 | "the total text length is 2150–2750 words" (same) |
| Each correct answer = 1 mark | **[C]** 3-0 | "Each correct answer receives 1 mark." ([GT format — Reading](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-general-training-test/ielts-general-training-format-reading)) |
| Suggested ~20 minutes per text | **[Q]** | "You have 60 minutes to read three texts and answer 40 questions. You should spend about 20 minutes on each text." ([Cambridge Academic FAQs](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/269898-ielts-academic-faqs.pdf)) |

The 20-minutes-per-passage figure is often taught as a strategy invented by
tutors. It is not — it is printed in Cambridge's own FAQ. It is guidance rather
than a rule (nothing enforces it), but it has a Tier-1 source.

### 1.1 The transfer asymmetry, stated once so it is never confused again

| Paper | Listening | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recording/reading time | ~30 min | 60 min |
| Transfer window | **+10 min, separate** | **none — included in the 60** |
| Computer-delivered | 2 min review, no transfer | 60 min, no transfer |

Listening figures **[C]** 3-0 from ielts.org and
[IDP](https://ielts.idp.com/prepare/article-how-computer-delivered-ielts-works);
Reading **[C]** 3-0. The rule holds for General Training Reading too: "60
minutes (including transfer time) … Unlike the Listening test, no extra transfer
time is given." **[C]** 3-0

You *may* draft on the question paper, but it earns nothing: "You may write your
answers on the question paper first, if you like, but **the marker will not read
these**." ([Cambridge Academic FAQs](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/269898-ielts-academic-faqs.pdf))
**[Q]**

> **Teaching implication.** A Reading tool must run one clock covering
> everything, including writing answers down. A Listening tool must run two.
> This is the single most common simulation error in home-made practice
> material, and it flatters the learner by roughly ten minutes.

## 2. The passages

Verbatim from the
[Academic format — Reading](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-reading)
page **[C]** 3-0:

- "Texts come from books, journals, magazines, newspapers and online resources,
  written for a non-specialist audience"
- "If texts use technical vocabulary, then a simple dictionary definition is
  provided."
- "At least one text contains detailed logical argument."

British Council adds the difficulty span: "three long texts which range from the
descriptive and factual to the discursive and analytical… selected for a
non-specialist audience but are appropriate for people entering university
courses or seeking professional registration"
([BC, Dealing with T/F/NG](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/sites/default/files/dealing_with_true_false_not_given_questions.pdf))
**[Q]**.

Two consequences worth stating plainly. First, **specialist vocabulary is
glossed, so the test is not a vocabulary-knowledge test in the obvious sense** —
Cambridge is explicit that "All the words you will need in order to answer the
questions will be given in the text"
([Cambridge Academic FAQs](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/269898-ielts-academic-faqs.pdf))
**[Q]**. Second, the "detailed logical argument" requirement means at least one
passage is structurally, not lexically, hard.

**General Training Reading differs by genre, not by mechanics** **[C]** 3-0:
Section 1 everyday texts ("notices, advertisements and timetables"), Section 2
work-related ("job descriptions, contracts, staff development and training
materials"), Section 3 "a topic of general interest… longer and more complex
than the texts in Sections 1 and 2". Timing, marking and question types are the
same. Our default target is Academic (see `01-exam-structure.md`).

### 2.1 What item writers select for — Tier 2

Green et al.'s interview study of eight IELTS Academic Reading item writers
gives the selection criteria in the writers' own words
([Green et al. 2010](https://ielts.org/cdn/Research/investigation-of-process-of-writing-academic-reading-test-items-for-ielts-green-et-al-2010.pdf))
**[T2]**:

- Testability first: "An ongoing challenge for Anne was to locate texts that
  included enough discrete points of information or opinions to support enough
  items to fulfil an IELTS commission: 'with a lot of articles, the problem is
  they say the same thing in different ways'."
- The ideal shape: "a main idea with a variety of examples rather than just one
  argument repeated" (Elizabeth).
- Density without technicality: "A good IELTS text would be propositionally
  dense, but not overly technical."
- Length ceiling: texts are cut to "the maximum IELTS word limit of 950 words".
- Coverage: "We need to cover the whole text – every paragraph is tested"
  (William).
- Three task types must fit one text: "In other papers you choose a text with
  one tasks type – IELTS needs a text that will work with three: sometimes this
  is quite difficult" (Jane) *[quoted as printed]*.

And a documented editing goal, from the Cambridge general-English item-writer
guidelines quoted in that report: source texts are cut or amended "to avoid
candidates being able to get the correct answer simply by **word matching**,
rather than by understanding the text" **[T2]**. Note the qualification: Green
et al. attribute this list to the *general English* item writer guidelines via
Khalifa and Weir, not to the IELTS Reading guidelines directly. Do not cite it
as an IELTS-specific rule.

The net effect of editing, as the researchers measured it: "to increase the
coherence and information density of the texts and to make them more accessible
to readers from non-English speaking backgrounds. The changes also served to
reduce technical and cultural specificity, colloquialism, journalistic touches"
**[T2]**.

> **Teaching implication.** The passages are *denser than authentic prose*, not
> merely longer — density is a deliberate product of editing. Grade-8 reading
> practice can move toward IELTS by tightening a text (removing repetition and
> aside) rather than by raising its vocabulary level.

## 4. True/False/Not Given versus Yes/No/Not Given

This is the highest-value section for teaching because it is where prep folklore
is thickest and where the official position is unusually clear.

### 4.1 The official distinction — target, not logic

| | True/False/Not Given | Yes/No/Not Given |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question asked | "Do the following statements agree with the **information** in the text?" | "Do the following statements agree with the **views of the writer**?" |
| Officially stated target | "tests your ability to recognise **specific information** given in the text" | "tests your ability to recognise **opinions or ideas**" |
| Typical text | "more factual texts" | "discursive or argumentative texts" |

Columns 1–2 quoted verbatim from
[Academic format — Reading](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-reading)
**[C]** 3-0, with the identical formulation on the
[GT format — Reading](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-general-training-test/ielts-general-training-format-reading)
page **[C]** 3-0. Row 3 from
[BC, Dealing with T/F/NG](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/sites/default/files/dealing_with_true_false_not_given_questions.pdf)
**[Q]**.

**The difference is what the statement is aimed at, not a difference in
reasoning.** Both types use the same three-way logic and, per British Council,
the same procedure: **"NB (1) Both task types can be answered using the SAME
strategy."** **[Q]** Any lesson that teaches YNNG as logically harder than TFNG
is asserting something the official material contradicts.

### 4.2 False versus Not Given, defined officially

British Council gives the cleanest definitions in the corpus **[Q]**:

> "'False' means that the passage states the opposite of the statement in
> question; 'not given' means that the statement is neither confirmed nor
> contradicted by the information in the passage."

> "'No' means that the views or claims of the writer explicitly disagree with
> the statement, i.e. the writer somewhere expresses the view or makes a claim
> which is opposite to the one given in the question; 'not given' means that the
> view or claim is neither confirmed nor contradicted."

And the rule candidates most often break: "students need to understand that any
knowledge they bring with them from outside the passage should not play a part
when deciding on their answers." **[Q]** Cambridge's teacher notes say it twice:
"It is important to remember that the answer is always in the text" and "Do not
use your own knowledge about the topic in the text or make assumptions – use
only the information in the text."
([Cambridge, Task Types 2 and 3 activity](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/ielts-academic-reading-task-type-2-identifying-information-and-task-type-3-identifying-writers-views-and-claims-activity.pdf))
**[Q]**

### 4.3 Answers are in text order — for this type, officially

"NB (2) The answers are in the same order as the text."
([BC](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/sites/default/files/dealing_with_true_false_not_given_questions.pdf))
**[Q]** Cambridge's activity turns it into procedure: "Start from the place in
the text where you found your last answer. **You do not need to start at the
beginning again as the answers are in the text order.**"
([Cambridge](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/ielts-academic-reading-task-type-2-identifying-information-and-task-type-3-identifying-writers-views-and-claims-activity.pdf))
**[Q]**

**Critically, this is *not* a universal Reading rule** the way it is in
Listening. The Cambridge FAQ hedges it by task type: "Don't go back to the
beginning of the text for each question **when you know from the task type**
that the answers will come in the order of the information in the text."
([Cambridge Academic FAQs](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/269898-ielts-academic-faqs.pdf))
**[Q]** So: ordered for TFNG/YNNG (stated); conditional elsewhere (implied, not
enumerated).

> **GAP** — not established in this research pass: **which Reading question
> types follow text order and which do not.** No official enumeration was found.
> Matching-headings and matching-information tasks are commonly taught as
> unordered; that is not sourced here.

### 4.4 Worked Not Given examples from official material

Both Tier 1, both showing the same construction — the text addresses an
*adjacent* proposition and stops short of the one asserted.

From Cambridge's activity key
([source](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/ielts-academic-reading-task-type-2-identifying-information-and-task-type-3-identifying-writers-views-and-claims-activity.pdf))
**[Q]**:

- Statement: *If one partner in a marriage smokes, the other is likely to take
  up smoking.* → **Not given** — "the text talks about the effects on the
  partner of passive smoking but doesn't mention if the partners tend to start
  smoking themselves."
- Statement: *Opponents of smoking financed the UCSF study.* → **Not given** —
  "the text states that the American Medical Association is an opponent of
  smoking but doesn't say whether UCSF is or not."
- Contrast, a **No**: *Thirty per cent of deaths in the United States are caused
  by smoking-related diseases* → "the text talks about percentages of deaths
  from cancer whereas in the statement it talks about deaths in general."

From British Council's key **[Q]**:

- **Not given** on a quantity word: statement *Most animals are active during
  the daytime* against text saying "Numerous creatures… are largely diurnal" →
  "the text says 'numerous' which might refer to most species, but also might
  not; we don't have enough information."
- **Not given** on outside knowledge: statement *Dublin is the capital of the
  Republic of Ireland* against a text that only gives a wifi code → "even if you
  know Dublin is the capital, based on the information given in the
  TEXT/PASSAGE, the correct answer is NOT GIVEN."

The recurring generators visible in these official keys: **scope substitution**
(cancer deaths → all deaths), **quantifier slippage** (numerous → most),
**attribute transfer to the wrong entity** (AMA opposes smoking → UCSF does),
and **plausible-but-unstated consequence** (passive smoking harms the partner →
the partner starts smoking).

### 4.5 How Not Given items are actually built — Tier 2

Green et al. watched item writers construct and repair these items in editing
meetings. This is the closest thing in the corpus to distractor engineering for
Reading, and it is self-report plus observed practice, not official rule
**[T2]**:

- **The clean recipe** — put the unstated content entirely outside the text.
  Of an accepted Not Given item: "The text compares the operation of the two
  systems, but does not make any statement about the organisations that
  manufacture them. Here both the noun phrase 'organisations' and the verb
  phrases 'produce' and 'make' relate to information that is **entirely absent**
  from the text. This item was accepted by the group without changes."
- **The one-substitution technique** — "William's two Not Given items both
  repeat lexis from the text to point to the location of the necessary
  information. In each case one phrase in the text is inaccurately paraphrased
  in the item stem so that in both cases, **substitution of one phrase would
  yield a True item**."
- **The failure mode is inference.** One item was rejected because "the text
  implied that this 'Not Given' statement was true"; the repair added
  "would benefit from further training", of which "There is no relationship…
  and the information given in the text so the new item appears to be
  unambiguously Not Given."
- **Location cues are deliberately present or deliberately absent.** For a
  harder item: "there seems to be no clear signal of where the necessary
  information occurs in the text… Recognising that such information is not
  available is likely to require reading both more of the text and more careful
  reading."
- **The known fairness worry**, raised by item writers themselves: "test-takers
  may infer differently from the item-writer, but plausibly, yet be penalised
  even when their understanding of the point concerned is not wrong."

Green et al. also found the type is **not written to a common standard**:
"different interpretations of the guidelines appear to lead writers to produce
items that target different types of reading on the part of test takers", with
implications "for the comparability of different forms of the test" **[T2]**.
Some writers paraphrase heavily; one writer's item had "almost half of the words
also occurring in the text" **[T2]**.

> **GAP** — not established in this research pass: **any official convention on
> the ratio of True to False to Not Given in a set.** Item writers in the Green
> study asked whether such a convention exists; the report does not answer.
> "There are never more than two Not Givens in a row" and similar are **teacher
> lore with no basis in this evidence base**.

## 5. The mechanical marking rules

| Rule | Status | Verbatim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Marks lost for incorrect spelling and grammar | **[C]** 3-0 | "You will lose marks for incorrect spelling and grammar." ([GT format — Reading](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-general-training-test/ielts-general-training-format-reading)) |
| Over-limit answers are marked **incorrect** | **[C]** 2-0 | "Answers which are over the word limit will be marked as incorrect." ([Cambridge Academic FAQs](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/269898-ielts-academic-faqs.pdf)) — the same FAQ text appears under both Listening and Reading |
| Copy words exactly from the text | **[Q]** | "Copy words accurately from the text. Spelling mistakes will mean that you will lose the mark for that question." (same) |
| Written answers must be grammatical | **[Q]** | "Make sure your answer is grammatically correct where you have to write your answer in words, e.g. short-answer questions, sentence completion, summary completion." (same) |
| Two answers where one is wanted scores zero | **[Q]** | "Don't write more than one answer when only one is required. Even if one of your answers is correct, you will not receive a mark." (same) |
| Hyphenated words count as one word | **[C]** 3-0 | "Hyphenated words such as 'check-in' count as single words" ([Academic test format in detail](https://ielts.org/organisations/ielts-for-organisations/test-types/ielts-academic-test/academic-test-format-in-detail)) |

The word-limit rubric is per-task and appears in forms such as "NO MORE THAN
TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER" and "NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS" **[C]** 3-0.

Note the interaction between two of these rules: answers must be **copied
exactly** from the text *and* must be **grammatically correct in the sentence
frame**. Where those conflict — the text has a plural, the frame wants a
singular — nothing in this corpus resolves it.

> **GAP** — not established in this research pass: **what happens when copying
> exactly and writing grammatically pull in opposite directions.** Widely
> discussed in prep material; unresolved by any Tier-1 document found here.

## 6. Time economics

The official positions, all Tier 1:

- 60 minutes, 40 questions, transfer included **[C]** 3-0.
- ~20 minutes per text **[Q]**.
- Work forward, don't stall: "Start at the beginning of the test and work
  through it. If you cannot do a particular question, leave it and go on to the
  next… Put a mark next to this question on the question paper so that you can
  find it again quickly."
  ([Cambridge Academic FAQs](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/269898-ielts-academic-faqs.pdf))
  **[Q]**
- Partial understanding is enough: "Don't worry if you don't understand every
  word. It may not be necessary to understand all the words in order to answer
  the questions correctly." (same) **[Q]**

**But the measured time pressure is lower than the folklore suggests.** Weir et
al. calculated it directly: the longest passage in their test form was 1,034
words and the shortest 586; "If a participant were to spend about one third of
the available time reading the questions and writing the responses, he or she
would still only need to read at the very slow rate of around **50 to 75 words
per minute** in order to read through each text once."
([Weir et al. 2009](https://ielts.org/cdn/Research/cognitive-processes-underlying-academic-reading-construct-as-measured-by-ielts-wier-et-al-2009.pdf))
**[T2]** Their conclusion was that "IELTS allows for very intensive careful
reading" — and, as a criticism, that the test therefore *under*-tests fast
reading. Their recommendation to the IELTS partners was to "Impose time
constraints on part of the test to encourage the use of the expeditious reading
strategies necessary for university study" **[T2]**.

There is a countervailing observation in the same report, offered tentatively:
unanswerable items clustered late in sections, "suggesting the effects of time
pressure" **[T2]**. Weir et al. never manipulated or measured time pressure
directly; all their statements about it are inferential and hedged.

> **Teaching implication.** The honest framing for a learner is not "IELTS
> Reading is a race". It is: there is enough time to read each passage once
> carefully *plus* time to search it repeatedly — provided you never stall on a
> single item. Stalling, not reading speed, is what the official advice and the
> research both point at.

## 7. What reading the test actually elicits

### 7.1 The framework everything downstream uses

Weir et al. work from the Khalifa and Weir model, itself following Urquhart and
Weir (1998), which crosses two dimensions **[T2]**:

| | **Local** — within the sentence | **Global** — beyond the sentence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Careful** — "processing a text thoroughly with the intention to extract complete meanings" | "Understand sentence" | "Comprehend main idea(s) / Comprehend overall text / Comprehend overall texts" |
| **Expeditious** — "quick, selective and efficient reading to access desired information in a text" | Scanning: "reading highly selectively to find specific words, figures or phrases in a text" | Skimming: "reading quickly by sampling text to abstract the gist, general impression and/or superordinate idea" |

Definitions verbatim from
[Weir et al. 2009](https://ielts.org/cdn/Research/cognitive-processes-underlying-academic-reading-construct-as-measured-by-ielts-wier-et-al-2009.pdf)
**[T2]**. Their fuller definitions:

- **Global comprehension** — "the understanding of information beyond the
  sentence, including main ideas, the links between ideas in the text and the
  way in which these are elaborated."
- **Local comprehension** — "the understanding of propositions within the
  sentence… involves word recognition, lexical access and syntactic parsing and
  establishing explicit propositional meaning at the phrase, clause and sentence
  level."
- **Search reading** sits across the local/global divide: "Where the desired
  information can be found within a single sentence the search reading would be
  classified as local and where information has to be constructed across
  sentences it would be seen as global."

### 7.2 What candidates actually do — and a correction to a popular claim

The widely repeated claim that IELTS Reading tests only careful, local reading
comes from an earlier CRELLA study. Weir et al. 2009 quote it — "'the major
focus of the IELTS test appears to be on careful reading whereas the survey data
reported here suggest that for university students expeditious skills and
strategies are just as critical'" — and then report evidence **against** it
**[T2]**:

> "The current study provides clear evidence that, for most participants across
> the different task types, expeditious reading in fact plays an important role
> in the way they seek to answer the questions. We found that, consistently
> across Test Sections, the majority of participants chose to read the text
> through quickly and selectively before reading the question."

Quantified: 61% skimmed before reading the questions in the first section, 55%
in the second, 46% in the third **[T2]**. On the whole-text worry: "Fears that
IELTS was not addressing understanding at the whole text level also appear to be
ill grounded" — the option "by understanding how information in the whole text
fits together" was chosen at least once by 82% of participants **[T2]**.

The most-used single strategy across the test: "quickly match words that
appeared in the question with similar or related words in the text… the most
frequently endorsed item on ten of the fifteen Test Sections with 83% of
participants reporting using this strategy at least once" **[T2]**.

The residual criticism the 2009 study *does* make: "this is almost invariably
associated with careful reading of relevant passages. Perhaps the only means of
testing expeditious reading is to enforce time constraints" **[T2]**.

**Do not cite the "IELTS is careful-local only" claim to Weir et al. 2009.** It
is a claim the paper reports from elsewhere and partly overturns.

### 7.3 The counterweight: Moore et al. on local-literal dominance

Moore et al. analysed 108 IELTS Academic Reading tasks / 494 items against real
university reading tasks, on two dimensions: *level of engagement* (local ↔
global) and *type of engagement* (literal ↔ interpretative)
([Moore et al. 2010](https://ielts.org/cdn/Research/construct-validity-in-the-ielts-academic-reading-test-moore-et-al-2010.pdf))
**[T2]**. Their finding:

> "The majority of the IELTS tasks were found to have a 'local-literal'
> configuration, requiring mainly a basic comprehension of relatively small
> textual units. In the academic corpus, a sizeable proportion of tasks had a
> similar local-literal orientation, but others involved distinctly different
> forms of engagement, including tasks that required a critical evaluation of
> material… or which stipulated reference to multiple sources."

Per task type **[T2]**:

- TFNG: "most tasks of this type required engagement at or around sentence
  level… assigned to the more local end"; and "a strongly 'literal' engagement".
  The Not Given option is the partial exception: "To establish whether certain
  information is or is not contained within a text requires some appraisal of
  the content of the whole text."
- Short answer: "the short answer format constitutes the most 'literal' and most
  'local' of all the task types considered".
- Multiple choice is the versatile one: "distinctive for implying no particular
  level of engagement with text… different items probing quite different levels
  of text."

The one quadrant-level number they give: items requiring engagement with the
**whole text** — choosing a title for the passage — were "five instances of this
item type… accounting for **1% of items**" **[T2]**.

What they say IELTS Reading does *not* measure **[T2]**: multiple-source reading
("all tasks in the IELTS corpus were focused on engagement with a single text"),
critical/evaluative reading ("the apparent absence of these modes among the
various IELTS tasks collected for the study"), genre and textual analysis,
information literacy and source selection, and the reading-writing nexus.

Their recommendation is to test developers, not teachers: "one useful principle
to employ… would be to seek to push test tasks, or at least a proportion of
them, in the direction of the more global/more interpretive regions" **[T2]**.
They add a caution against over-reading their own critique: nearly half their
academic informants rated the test favourably, and only two of twelve — from
Media Studies and Linguistics — were sharply critical **[T2]**.

**Reconciling the two studies.** They are not in direct contradiction: Moore
et al. classify *what the items demand*; Weir et al. observe *what candidates
do*. A local-literal item can still be approached by skimming first and then
reading carefully. Where they agree is that global, interpretative, multi-text
reading is thinly represented.

> **Teaching implication.** IELTS Reading is not a test of critical reading, and
> our course should not pretend it is. The honest claim for a grade-8 lesson is
> that precise local comprehension — resolving *what exactly this sentence
> asserts* — is what the test rewards, and that critical evaluation, while more
> valuable in itself, trains toward university rather than toward the test.

## 8. What separates strong readers from weak readers

The best evidence in this corpus is Bax's eye-tracking work: two studies using
the same test items, one with a Malaysian cohort (n = 71 tested, 38 tracked;
[Bax 2013](https://oro.open.ac.uk/47504/1/Bax%20ELTRA%20report.pdf)) and a
replication with a multinational cohort (n = 41 tested, 30 tracked;
[Bax 2015](https://www.ielts.org/researchers/our-research/research-reports/using-eye-tracking-to-research-the-cognitive-processes-of-multinational-readers-during-an-ielts-reading-test)).
All Tier 2 **[T2]**.

### 8.1 The headline: locating, not reading

> "Unsuccessful students were apparently poor at locating the site of a correct
> answer in the text, unlike successful students." (Bax 2015)

> "weaker students apparently unable to locate the site of a correct answer as
> effectively as stronger students on some items and thus spent longer on larger
> chunks of text… while stronger students demonstrated that they could locate a
> smaller, particular part of a text and focus more expeditiously on it so as to
> extract the answer." (Bax 2013)

**Successful readers spent less time on the whole text and more time on the
right part of it.** The direction is consistent across both cohorts:

| Measure | Unsuccessful | Successful | Study |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fixation count, whole text (item 5) | median 367 | median 159 | Bax 2015 (*U* = 63.0, *Z* = −1.988, *p* = .047) |
| Total fixation duration, whole text (item 5) | median 122.5 s | median 65.39 s | Bax 2013 |
| Total visit duration, correct paragraph (item 10) | median 1.7 s | median 16.95 s | Bax 2015 |
| Fixation count, correct paragraph (item 10) | median 5 | median 51 | Bax 2015 |
| Fixation count, key synonym phrase (item 4) | median 3 | median 12 | Bax 2015 (*U* = 35.0, *Z* = −3.232, *p* = .001) |

On several key phrases, "more than half did not find them or focus on them at
all, which is why the median scores are zero in each case" (Bax 2015) **[T2]**.

### 8.2 Strategy, not eye mechanics, is the underlying difference

> "Interview data showed that the reason for this was the use by successful
> readers of relatively conscious metacognitive strategies. Unsuccessful
> students, however, appeared to use no such conscious strategies, apparently
> searching relatively randomly through the text to find the location of the
> answer." (Bax 2015)

> "what differentiated successful readers from unsuccessful readers was the fact
> that most of them used pre-determined and conscious strategies systematically,
> whereas unsuccessful students for the most part appeared to be more aimless in
> their approaches." (Bax 2015)

Weir et al. found the same shape from protocol data: "higher scoring
participants were more likely to use an approach combining **search reading with
careful re-reading** of relevant sections of text" — and noted this "parallels
the most widely adopted approach to academic reading" among real undergraduates
**[T2]**.

### 8.3 Vocabulary depth — where it actually bites

Successful readers fixated more on the *lexical link* between question and text.
On one item, "The question used the phrase *home owners* so that in order to
locate the answer, participants had to find and interpret the word *housing* in
the text and identify it as lexically related to *home*"; weak readers "fixated
on this target word almost not at all, [which] explains in large part why they
failed to find the correct answer" (Bax 2015) **[T2]**.

But lexical similarity also misleads: on another item "elsewhere in the text
other athletes were mentioned, and analysis of the video data shows that
incorrect students were distracted by that" (Bax 2013) **[T2]**. And prolonged
dwelling on a difficult phrase marks failure, not effort: "weaker students
focused on them repeatedly in a vain attempt to answer" (Bax 2013) **[T2]**.

Bax's own teaching implication, stated by him **[T2]**: "Successful candidates
also showed better abilities at the lexical level (in matching words in the
question and the text, and in doing the same with synonyms), which suggests that
it is valuable to work extensively on their lexical knowledge and their ability
to identify **lexical matches of various kinds**." He adds syntax: successful
readers "showed better ability in terms of dealing with syntactic ambiguities".

> **Teaching implication.** The trainable skill the eye-tracking data actually
> identifies is **synonym recognition under search conditions** — not vocabulary
> size, and not reading speed. A grade-8 exercise that shows a question phrase
> and asks the learner to find its paraphrase in a paragraph *as fast as
> possible* trains exactly the behaviour that separated the groups. It is
> auto-scorable and level-appropriate.

### 8.4 Honest limits on the eye-tracking evidence

These matter, because the study is easy to over-read:

- **Skimming the whole text first was not shown to help.** Bax's two research
  questions about global reading were abandoned as unanswerable: "of those few
  who did read large parts of the text in advance, it was impossible in practice
  to distinguish in any principled way between careful and expeditious reading
  in terms of eye movements… it was not possible to answer Research questions 1
  and 2 in any satisfactory way." Also: "only a handful read the whole text in
  advance, while some read part of it and others none at all." **[T2]**
- **Re-reading and regressions were never measured.** Bax speculates about them
  as future work; there is no finding.
- **Most items showed no difference at all.** "with a number of the test items
  (7 of 11), there were no significant differences between successful and
  unsuccessful participants in terms of eye-tracking evidence" (2015); "in half
  of the test items (five of ten), no significant differences were identified"
  (2013) **[T2]**.
- **Only two task types were studied** — sentence completion (targeting careful
  local reading) and matching (targeting expeditious local reading). TFNG,
  matching headings, multiple choice and summary completion were **not** in
  either study.
- The two cohorts diverged on which items discriminated, using identical items.

> **GAP** — not established in this research pass: **what distinguishes band 8–9
> readers specifically.** All the evidence above contrasts *correct versus
> incorrect on an item*, not band 8 versus band 6. No study in this corpus
> stratifies reading behaviour by band. Any claim that "band 9 readers do X" is
> unsupported here.

> **GAP** — not established in this research pass: **per-question-type
> differences in reader behaviour.** Bax covered two task types; TFNG and
> matching headings — the two most taught — were not examined.

## 9. Folklore audit

The plan requires separating official rule from research finding from teacher
lore. Applied to the most-repeated Reading claims:

| Widely taught | Status on this evidence |
| --- | --- |
| "Spend 20 minutes per passage" | **Official guidance** — printed in the [Cambridge FAQs](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/269898-ielts-academic-faqs.pdf), though not enforced |
| "There is no extra time to transfer answers" | **Official rule**, stated on three separate pages **[C]** 3-0 |
| "Skim the passage before reading the questions" | **Official advice**, and independently observed: "Read through the text quickly so that you are familiar with the topic and how it is developed" **[Q]**; 46–61% of Weir's participants did it **[T2]**. But Bax's eye-tracking **could not show it helps** — see §8.4 |
| "Read the questions first, then hunt" | **Official advice** in the same document — "Skim the questions, where appropriate, before reading the text so that you have a purpose for reading" **[Q]**. Note that ielts.org publishes both this and the skim-the-text-first advice; neither is presented as the correct order |
| "Don't read the whole text carefully" | **Official advice** — "Don't waste time reading the whole text each time for each set of questions… you need to skim quickly through the text rather than reading it all carefully" **[Q]** |
| "Turn the statement into a Yes/No question" | **Official technique**, published by British Council as a taught step **[Q]** — one of the few pieces of prep technique with a Tier-1 source |
| "Never use outside knowledge on T/F/NG" | **Official rule**, stated by both British Council and Cambridge **[Q]** |
| "T/F/NG answers come in text order" | **Official for this task type** (BC, Cambridge) **[Q]**; **not established** for other types — see §4.3 |
| "Y/N/NG is harder than T/F/NG because the logic is different" | **Lore, contradicted.** BC: "Both task types can be answered using the SAME strategy" **[Q]**. The official difference is the target of the statement |
| "Never more than two Not Givens in a row" | **Lore with no basis found.** Item writers in the Green study asked whether such a convention exists; nothing answers it **[T2]** |
| "Absolute words like *always*, *never*, *entirely* signal False" | **Lore, with one adverse data point.** In a Green editing meeting a writer warned that "the word 'entirely' could be a trigger to test wise candidates, but this objection was overridden" **[T2]** — i.e. the cue is known to item writers and not systematically removed. Nothing supports it as a reliable rule |
| "The passages get harder from 1 to 3" | **Not established here.** ielts.org states the difficulty gradient explicitly for *General Training* ("three sections of increasing difficulty") **[C]** 3-0 but no equivalent statement for Academic was found |
| "Guessing is hopeless" | **Contradicted for one type.** Weir et al. found some items "particularly vulnerable to guessing – a point underlined by the discovery that the researchers were also able, without reading the texts, to give the correct answer" to several, and recommended IELTS "Give close attention, in trialling, to the possibility of guessing correct answers, particularly to Yes/No/Not Given items" **[T2]** |

## 10. Item construction — what is known about distractors

For Reading, unlike Listening, there is real evidence, all of it Tier 2 and all
of it from item writers' own accounts in
[Green et al. 2010](https://ielts.org/cdn/Research/investigation-of-process-of-writing-academic-reading-test-items-for-ielts-green-et-al-2010.pdf)
**[T2]**:

- Plausibility is the hard part, and subject knowledge threatens it: "if it's a
  scientific text, I may not know nearly enough to know what would be a
  plausible distractor. What seems plausible to me could be instantly rejected
  by somebody who knows a little more about the subject." (William)
- The triviality trade-off: "MCQ: coming up with four plausible opinions which
  are wrong is difficult: the danger is that you are pushed into testing
  something that is trivial… they should all be important pieces of information
  or opinions or functions."
- Two distractors that mean the same thing raise the guess rate — an editing
  meeting flagged "having two incorrect alternatives which mean the same (though
  in different words) was in a way increasing the test-taker's chance of
  selecting the right alternative."
- A distractor drawn from a distant part of the text is a "give-away".
- Key position is balanced deliberately: options were reordered "so that the key
  (originally D) would not come last in the set" and again "to make D the key,
  balancing the number of each option that appeared as the key."
- Whether a distractor may contain information *not in the text at all* was
  raised in editing as an open question and not settled.

Two structural findings with teaching value **[T2]**:

- **Item difficulty is not predictable by the people writing the items.** "the
  group agreed that the difficulty of items was not easy to predict. None of the
  writers seemed to have a clear sense of the proportion of items associated
  with a text that a successful IELTS candidate at band 6.0 or 6.5 might be
  expected to answer correctly. Pretesting results often revealed items to be
  easier or more difficult than expected."
- **Difficulty comes from the questions, not the passage.** An item writer
  worried her text was too easy and "was reminded by her colleagues that a
  straightforward text might well be accompanied by difficult questions."

> **GAP** — not established in this research pass: **any official IELTS rule on
> distractor construction.** Green et al. reproduce none, and explicitly report
> that the guidance is missing: untrained writers "felt that they would have
> benefited from information on devising MCQ distractors and on the skills being
> targeted by items of different types." Everything in this section is writer
> self-report and observed editing practice.

> **Teaching implication.** The one directly usable fact here is that difficulty
> lives in the item, not the text. A grade-8 reading passage can carry
> IELTS-shaped difficulty without being made harder to read — by asking
> questions whose answer requires resolving a paraphrase rather than spotting a
> word.

## 11. Where the evidence runs out

Collected gaps:

> **GAP** — **what distinguishes band 8–9 readers.** No study in this corpus
> stratifies by band; all contrasts are item-level correct/incorrect. §8.4.

> **GAP** — **which question types follow text order.** Confirmed for TFNG/YNNG
> only. §4.3.

> **GAP** — **the officially stated sub-skill for nine of the eleven question
> types.** §3.

> **GAP** — **T/F/NG distribution conventions.** §4.5.

> **GAP** — **reader behaviour on matching headings and TFNG.** Not covered by
> the eye-tracking work. §8.4.

> **GAP** — **the copy-exactly versus write-grammatically conflict.** §5.

> **GAP** — **whether Academic Reading passages increase in difficulty across
> the three sections.** Stated officially for General Training only. §9.

> **GAP** — **raw-score-to-band conversion for Reading.** Not established here;
> see `01-exam-structure.md`. No tool may report a predicted band without it.
