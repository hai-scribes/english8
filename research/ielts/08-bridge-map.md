# Bridge Map — English 8 → IELTS

**What this is.** Phase 5 of the IELTS knowledge base
(`research/ielts-research-plan.md`): the payoff document. It connects what the
twelve-unit grade-8 course already teaches (`curriculum/syllabus.md`) to the
IELTS competencies established in Phases 1–4, and it defines the arc above
grade 8 so that today's decisions do not dead-end.

**Research date.** 2026-08-08. No new research was conducted for this file. It
is a synthesis over `01`–`07` and the syllabus; **every alignment claim below
resolves to one of those eight documents**, and this file introduces no new
Tier-1 or Tier-2 sources of its own.

**Dependency.** Unusable without `01-exam-structure.md` (what the test is),
`02-band-descriptors.md` (what the scale says), `03-listening.md`,
`04-reading.md`, `05-writing.md`, `06-speaking.md` (what each skill demands),
and `07-language-foundation.md` (what is trainable, and at what CEFR level).
Where those files disagree with each other, the disagreement is reproduced here
rather than resolved.

## 0. Conventions, and the two rules this file must not break

### 0.1 Markers

Evidential markers are **inherited, never upgraded**. A claim that is Tier-2,
singly-sourced, or unsustained upstream stays that way here. This document sits
at the end of a citation chain and is the easiest place for a hedged finding to
harden into a fact; the legend exists to stop that.

| Marker | Meaning | Origin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **[C]** *n-n* | Confirmed by an adversarial panel upstream, vote shown | `03`–`06` |
| **[V]** *n-n* | Passed the upstream verification panel | `01`, `07` |
| **[Q]** | Tier-1 verbatim — the quote *is* the claim | `01`, `03`–`06` |
| **[D]** | Band-descriptor wording quoted in `02`. `02` carries no per-claim marker because every descriptor line in it is verbatim Tier-1 | `02` |
| **[S]** | Quoted from a primary document in the research cache, not panel-verified | `01`, `07` |
| **[S/NS]** | In the cache and quoted, **but** the corresponding panel claim was not sustained — well-evidenced but singly-sourced | `07` |
| **[T2]** | Tier-2 evidence — a tendency in candidate performance, never a rule of the test | `03`–`07` |
| **[X]** | Tested upstream and **not sustained**. Neither the claim nor its negation is asserted | `03`–`06` |
| **[INF]** | **This document's own reasoning.** A mapping no source states. The underlying facts are cited; the link between them is mine | new here |
| **[SPEC]** | **Speculative upgrade.** Plausible, level-appropriate, and *untested* — no source says it works | new here |

`> **GAP**` blockquotes mark something the plan asked for that the evidence base
does not supply. Gaps are left visible and are **never** filled with an upgrade.

### 0.2 No band promises

No lesson in this file "gets a learner to band X". `02` §9 establishes that **no
half-band descriptors exist**, that no official criterion-to-band arithmetic was
found, and that **no examiner-commented band-9 script was ever obtained** for
Writing (`05` §5) or Speaking (`06` §5) — everything said about the top of the
scale derives from descriptor wording, not from an exemplar.

Where a band number appears below, it **locates a feature on the descriptor
grid** ("chunking is named in the band-6 Pronunciation column") and never
predicts a learner's score. `02` §10.2 is binding: tools and lessons report
criterion-level observations, never a number.

### 0.3 Label by CEFR, not by IELTS band

`07` §8.4 is binding: lesson labels are CEFR-anchored. The IELTS↔CEFR crosswalk
is derivable from 5.5 upward and **bottoms out at band 4.0 = the B1 threshold**;
**no official source assigns any band score to A2 or A1 at all** (`07` §6.2a)
**[S]**. Where A1 or A2 must be certified the system does not extend the band
scale downward — it *switches instrument*, to IELTS Life Skills, which is
"not scored … you will receive either a Pass or a Fail result", so there is no
score to convert. **Most grade-8 learners sit at A2. There is therefore no band
to label them with, and none can be derived.** A lesson may say "trains toward
CEFR B1 Grammatical accuracy", and may say "IELTS band 4.0 in a skill is the
published B1 threshold"; it may **not** say "trains toward IELTS band 4.5", and
it may **not** say "IELTS band 3 = A2".

*Corrected 2026-08-08. This section previously recorded the floor as "a gap below
5.0". That was the floor of one Cambridge conversion table, not the floor of the
official alignment. The rule this file follows is unchanged; it is now justified
**positively** rather than by absence of evidence.*

## 1. A correction carried in from `01` §11.3

The research plan (Phase 5) asserts that "U9 instructions and U7 notice train
Task 1 General genres." **That claim was tested during research and failed.** It
is recorded here once so it is not re-derived, and it is not reproduced anywhere
else in this file.

GT Writing Task 1 admits **exactly one genre**: a letter to a named or
role-identified recipient, opened with a salutation, in one of three registers,
covering three supplied bullet points, at 150+ words (`01` §11.1–11.2) **[S]**.
Notices and instructions are neither letters nor addressed to a recipient and
take no salutation. They appear in General Training only as **Reading** texts —
Section 1 ("notices, advertisements, timetables") and Section 3, which is
"descriptive and instructive" (`01` §11) **[S]**.

The replacement, from `01` §11.3:

| Course lesson | Defensible claim |
| --- | --- |
| **U7 — notice, ~100–120w** | Trains **audience-appropriate register and concision**, plus familiarity with the notice as a *text type read* in GT Reading Section 1. No direct Academic Writing transfer. |
| **U9 — instructions, 80–100w** | Trains **sequencing, imperative and procedural language, and step ordering**, which transfers to **Academic Writing Task 1 process-diagram description** (`01` §5; `05` §2.6). |

The U9 → **Academic** Task 1 process link is the genuinely valuable one, and the
original claim obscured it. Since this course targets Academic (`01` §1), a GT
Task 1 justification was the wrong bridge to build in the first place.

## 2. What actually transfers — five findings that reshape the map

Read these before the per-unit table, or the table will read as flat.

1. **Discourse gates the top; sentence-level language does not buy a band.** An
   official Task 1 script credited with "a wide range of sophisticated lexis"
   and "the majority of sentences are error-free" — band-8 descriptor language —
   was held at 7: "In spite of the high level of language proficiency, the flaws
   in format and organisation limit the rating for this response to Band 7"
   (`02` §7 item 5) **[Q]**. The paragraph-level moves this course already
   teaches are therefore not the soft part of IELTS preparation; they are the
   part that caps strong language users.
2. **Complexity is not the ladder.** Syntactic complexity measures peak at band
   7 and *fall* at band 8 (`07` §4.2, Banerjee et al., 275 scripts) **[S/NS]**;
   Brown found band-8 spoken utterances less complex than band-7 (`02` §4.3)
   **[T2]**. The descriptors' repeated word is *flexibility*, not *complexity*
   (`07` §4.1) **[D]**. Adding subordination past U7 is not supported.
3. **Accuracy on three named structures tracks the whole scale.** Determiners
   are the largest error category at every band 5.5–7.5 (18.7% → 4.7%, `07`
   §4.4) **[S]**; third-person `-s` and passives climb Target-Like Use from
   ~0.4 to 1.0 across bands 3–8, and 3SG `-s` was L1-independent in its
   trajectory (`07` §4.3) **[S/NS]**. Two of those three sit inside the grade-8
   syllabus. One does not — see §5.1.
4. **Suprasegmentals discriminate; segmentals are a floor — and for *these*
   learners the floor is the binding constraint.** Discriminant loadings:
   chunking .745, intonation .735, word stress .668, vowel/consonant errors .551
   (`07` §5.2, Isaacs et al., N=80) **[S]**. The descriptor architecture agrees:
   segmentals are named at bands 4 and 6 and disappear at 8 and 9 (`02` §5.2)
   **[D]**. But Isaacs discriminated *bands 5–8* — a ceiling question — while the
   Vietnamese-L1 evidence measures features still failing **at C1** — a floor
   question — and grade-8 learners are at the floor. The two rankings therefore
   disagree, and where they do the floor governs (`07` §5.5.8, §7 item 8;
   §4.2 below). **Rank 1 in the Vietnamese ordering is syllable-final consonants,
   which no unit in the twelve teaches** (§5.4).
5. **The 80–100-word paragraph is a Task 2 *body* paragraph, not an essay**
   (`05` §1). Labelling it otherwise is the single easiest way to make a false
   claim about this course.

### Unit 1 — Leisure time
*Grammar: verbs of liking/disliking + V-ing vs to-V · Phonology: /ʊ/ vs /uː/ ·
Reading: blog post · Speaking: group Q&A, **report back** · Writing: email 80–100w*

**Already trains.** The theme is a Speaking Part 1 topic in the official wording
— the examiner "asks you general questions on some familiar topics, such as
home, family, work, studies or **interests**" (`06` §4) **[Q]**. The report-back
task is a proto-Part-2 long turn (§4.4 below). Liking/disliking is on the
official list of speech functions the test structure reliably elicits —
"expressing a preference", "expressing opinions" (`06` §6.3) **[T2]**. The email
is personal-experience content, which Task 2 explicitly licenses: "examples may
be drawn from a candidate's own experience" (`05` §3.1) **[C]** 3-0. The vowel
contrast is a long/short pair, and long/short vowel contrasts sit inside
Jenkins' Lingua Franca Core (`07` §5.4) **[S]**.

| Upgrade | What changes in the lesson | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **"One turn, one subject."** Before the report-back, the student names in one phrase what the turn is *for*, then drops anything that does not serve it. | An instruction line and a 10-second planning beat on a task that already exists. | Sourced. `06` §2 lists "relevance of spoken sentences to the general purpose of a turn" as an official Coherence indicator **[C]** 3-0; `06` §5 shows it deciding a real rating — Tina (Vietnam), Part 2, **Band 5**: "she **describes several interests rather than one interest**, makes her hard to follow at times and leads to some loss of coherence" **[Q]**. |

**Level guard.** No new language. The discipline is identical to a paragraph's
topic sentence, which the course teaches from U2 onward.

> Note the coincidence worth exploiting: the officially commented candidate whose
> long turn failed for having no single subject was talking about **her
> interests** — the U1 topic, in the U1 task shape.

### Unit 2 — Life in the countryside
*Grammar: comparative adverbs · Phonology: /ə/ vs /ɪ/ · Speaking: describe a
village · Writing: paragraph 80–100w, likes/dislikes*

**Already trains.** The 80–100w paragraph is a Task 2 body paragraph (`05` §1).
Comparative adverbs feed the one language system the Task 1 rubric asks for on
**every** visual genre: the instruction "make comparisons where relevant"
appears verbatim on all three official sample tasks including the process
diagram (`05` §2.2) **[C]** 2-1 — with the honest qualification `05` records,
that the vote was not unanimous and that no map task appears in the official
sample set at all. Describing a place is Part-1/Part-2 territory.

| Upgrade | What changes in the lesson | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Topic-sentence checkpoint.** Before drafting, write the one sentence a reader who reads nothing else should get; after drafting, check each remaining sentence supports it. | One pre-writing line and one post-writing check on the existing paragraph task. | Sourced. `05` §2.5: "'Overview' is a summarising skill, not a paragraph slot… *given five facts, write the one sentence that a reader who reads nothing else should get* — which is also the topic-sentence skill our paragraph genres already need." Paragraphing sits inside Coherence & Cohesion and is a named limiter at 5.5 and 6.5 (`02` §7 item 11) **[Q]**. |
| **One comparison, highlighted — not a list of differences.** The comparative-adverb exercise asks for the *biggest* difference between town and village, stated once, rather than three parallel comparisons. | Reworded exercise prompt. | Sourced at criterion level. Task Achievement targets "comparing or contrasting… by adequately highlighting the identifiable trends, principal changes or differences… **rather than mechanical description reporting detail**" (`02` §2.1, `05` §2.3) **[Q]**; "mechanical" recurs as the named limiter at bands 5 and 6 (`02` §7 item 8). That this transfers from a grade-8 comparative drill to Task 1 is **[INF]**. |

**Level guard.** Both upgrades are instructions about an existing text, not new
grammar or lexis.

**A note on /ə/.** The schwa is the vowel of unstressed English syllables, so a
schwa lesson is the earliest foothold the syllabus has on stress-timing — the
feature the band-6 Pronunciation descriptor names as the limiter on rhythm
("rhythm may be affected by a **lack of stress-timing**", `02` §5.2) **[D]**.
**[INF]** — no source in the knowledge base makes this link, and `07` §5.4
records that Jenkins' LFC recommends *not* teaching weak forms, while also
finding the LFC does not transfer to IELTS. Treat it as a reason to keep the /ə/
lesson, not as a warrant for building weak-form drills.

### Unit 3 — Teenagers
*Grammar: simple vs compound sentences, coordinating conjunctions · Phonology:
/ʊə/ vs /ɔɪ/ · Speaking: pair/group Q&A, **report back** · Writing: paragraph,
causes of stress and solutions*

**Already trains.** Compound sentences move a learner off the band-4 floor —
band 4 GRA is defined by "**Subordinate clauses are rare and simple sentences
predominate**", one of the few clauses the [2023] grid prints in bold (`02`
§3.5, §6.1) **[D]**. Band 4 Fluency & Coherence names "Can link simple sentences
but often with repetitious use of connectives" (`02` §4.1) **[D]** — which is
precisely what a coordinating-conjunction lesson risks producing if left
unguarded. Report-back = proto-Part-2 (§4.4).

| Upgrade | What changes in the lesson | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **"One marker per stage, none inside a stage."** Install the rule at the moment coordinating conjunctions are introduced, before the habit forms. | A constraint on the existing conjunction exercise and the paragraph task. | Sourced. `05` §4: the criteria explicitly *want* discourse markers, while examiner comments repeatedly flag overuse — "connectives are overused or inappropriate" (Band 6), "cohesive devices are overused" (Band 6), "some mechanical use of linkers" (Band 5.5), "some overuse of sequencers" (Band 7.5) **[Q]**. `05` §4's own recommendation is the wording used here. `02` §7 item 10 adds the nuance that overuse alone is survivable at 7.5, so this is a habit-forming measure, not a rule about a ceiling. |

**Level guard.** Fewer linkers is easier than more. This upgrade *reduces* what
the learner has to produce.

**Do not over-claim the genre.** A causes-and-solutions paragraph looks like the
prep industry's "problem–solution" Task 2 archetype. `05` §3.2 records a **GAP**:
no Tier-1 source enumerating Task 2 question archetypes was located, and only
two archetypes are attested by official prompts (agree/disagree, and
disadvantages-outweigh-advantages). Claim only the criterion-level fact — ideas
must be "extended and supported" (`02` §3.1) **[D]** — and do not call this
"problem–solution Task 2 practice".

### Unit 4 — Ethnic groups of Viet Nam
*Grammar: Yes/No and Wh- questions; countable/uncountable nouns · Phonology:
/k/ vs /g/ · Speaking: discuss stilt-house features; **interview peers** ·
Reading: ethnic groups, stilt houses · Writing: paragraph, own contributions to
family*

**Already trains.** The peer interview mirrors the Part 1 question–answer
mechanic; "providing personal information" and "providing non-personal
information" head the official list of functions the test elicits (`06` §6.3)
**[T2]**. Countable/uncountable is the selection condition for article and
quantifier choice, and determiners are the **largest error category at every
band 5.5–7.5** (`07` §4.4) **[S]** — though Müller & Han do not decompose
determiner errors by countability, so the link from this lesson to that finding
is **[INF]**. The reading is factual/descriptive, the text profile IELTS
Reading's local-literal items sit on (`04` §7.3) **[T2]**.

| Upgrade | What changes in the lesson | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **"Answer, then develop" — a two-beat rule for every interview answer.** One sentence that answers the question; one sentence that extends it. | A scoring rule on the existing peer-interview task. | Sourced. Seedhouse et al. (2014): candidates "need to provide an answer to the question **and** develop the topic inherent in the question", yet "the ability to answer questions is **not specifically included**" in the grading criteria (`06` §6.3) **[T2]**. `06`'s own teaching implication is the two-beat habit. Because no criterion names it, present it as a discourse demand, not as a scored behaviour. |
| **Synonym search against the clock.** After the reading, give a question phrase and ask learners to find its paraphrase in one named paragraph as fast as possible. | An added 3-minute activity on the existing text. | Sourced. `04` §8.3: successful readers fixated on the lexical link between question and text; on one item weak readers "fixated on this target word almost not at all"; Bax's own implication is to work on "the ability to identify **lexical matches of various kinds**" **[T2]**. `04` §8.3's teaching implication names exactly this exercise and calls it auto-scorable and level-appropriate. Carry `04` §8.4's limits: only two task types were studied, and 7 of 11 items showed no significant group difference. |

**Level guard.** The synonym task uses the unit's own text and lexis; difficulty
lives in the item, not in the passage (`04` §10 teaching implication).

### Unit 5 — Our customs and traditions
*Grammar: **articles, incl. zero article** · Phonology: /n/ vs /ŋ/ · Reading: a
village festival day · Writing: email of advice, 80–100w*

**Already trains.** This is, on the evidence base, **the single
highest-leverage grammar lesson in the syllabus**. Determiners are the largest
error category at every band from 5.5 to 7.5 and improve the most across that
range — 18.7% → 14.2% → 12.8% → 8.0% → 4.7% (`07` §4.4, `02` §3.5, Müller & Han,
500 samples, 144,671 words) **[S]**. `07` §8.2's verdict is quoted rather than
paraphrased: "U5 (articles incl. zero article) is the syllabus's highest-leverage
grammar lesson; **treat it as a spine, not a one-off**." The email of advice
additionally trains register selection against a stated recipient.

| Upgrade | What changes in the lesson | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Make U5 a spine.** Append a five-item article check to the writing task of every subsequent unit (U6–U12): five article decisions the learner made, marked as supplied-or-not in obligatory contexts. | A recurring checklist line on tasks that already exist. No new lesson. | Sourced. `07` §8.2 states the spine recommendation and the metric: track "target-like use in obligatory contexts for named structures", not a complexity index or a score. |
| **Report it as a fraction, not a mark.** "Articles supplied in 8/10 obligatory contexts." | Changes the feedback format only. | Sourced. `07` §8.2 **[S/NS]** (Banerjee's TLU method); `02` §10.2 forbids band output. `07` §4.4 adds the constraint that matters most here: improvement is **not linear**, and the CEFR Companion Volume independently reports that "inaccuracy increases at around B1 as the learner is beginning to use language more independently and creatively" **[S]**. A rising error rate at this level is the signature of progress, and the feedback format must not call it regression. |

**Level guard.** Articles are already this unit's target. The upgrade changes
where the practice *recurs*, not what is taught.

**On /n/ vs /ŋ/ — and why this is *not* the coda lesson it looks like.**
*(Rewritten 2026-08-08; `07` §5.5 is now closed.)* Final-nasal contrasts are
where a Vietnamese-L1 pronunciation priority looks as though it should sit, and
the evidence says it does not. Vietnamese licenses exactly eight coda segments —
"/p t k/ …, three nasals **/m n ŋ/**, and two approximants /j w/" (Kirby 2011;
`07` §5.5.1) **[S]**. **Both members of U5's contrast are already legal
Vietnamese codas**, so the lesson works inside the L1 inventory rather than
outside it.

What lies outside it is every final **fricative and affricate** — no Vietnamese
dialect permits one — and every final **cluster**. That is where the measured
difficulty is: 28.4% omission of final /s~z/ and 46.7% in coda clusters, in
learners at C1 (Disney & Le 2024, N = 16; `07` §5.5.2) **[S]**. **No unit in the
twelve targets it.** Teach U5's nasal contrast on the general phonetic case; do
not label it "the Vietnamese final-consonant lesson", because it is not one.
§5.4 carries the gap.

### Unit 6 — Lifestyles
*Grammar: future simple; first conditional · Phonology: /br/ vs /pr/ · Reading:
traditional lifestyle in Alaska · Writing: paragraph, pros **or** cons of online
learning*

**Already trains.** First of three balanced-argument-then-position writing tasks
(§4.1). The cluster contrast is productive word-initially, which `07` §5.4 notes
satisfies both the LFC account (which insists on "No omission of consonants in
word initial clusters") and the IELTS-relevant prosodic evidence **[S]**.

**And it is now directly Vietnamese-evidenced.** *(Added 2026-08-08 on `07`
§5.5.3, which did not exist when this section was written.)* Tran & Nguyen (2022),
N = 36 Vietnamese undergraduates, 1,080 tokens per cluster type, two raters at
r = .98, found that in **two-consonant onsets the dominant error is not deletion
but feature change on the plosive — 195 of 252 errors, 77.4%, against deletion at
22.2%** — and attribute it to voicing and aspiration: "the learners had a
tendency to change the voicing feature of voiceless consonants into the voiced
ones" **[S]**. **/br/ vs /pr/ is exactly that contrast.** `07` §8.3's VN-2 is
explicit: teach U6 and U7 "as **voicing-and-aspiration** contrasts, not as
'clusters'". That is a reframing of the existing drill, not a new one — so it is
recorded here rather than as an upgrade row.

**Carry the coverage caveat.** The study's two-consonant set was /br kr pr st tr/.
**/br/ and /pr/ were both directly tested**; U7's /bl/ and /kl/ were **not**
(U7 is supported by extension from /kr/), and of U8's pair **/st/ was tested and
/sp/ was not** (`07` §5.5.3). N = 36 at one university, read-aloud and prompted
tasks over Zoom, onset clusters only — the paper's own limitation statement calls
the sample "quite small, which significantly limits the generalizability of the
findings" **[S]**.

| Upgrade | What changes in the lesson | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Name the schema here, once: open → stand → close.** Teach it as one move for a *body paragraph*, and reuse the same name in U8 and U11. | A named frame on the existing paragraph task. | Sourced. The Task Response criterion assesses "how clearly the candidate opens the discourse, **establishes their position** and formulates conclusions" (`05` §3.1) **[Q]** — position clarity is a named criterion element, not style advice. `02` §3.1: at every band 6–9 a position must be presented; its absence is a band 4–5 feature **[D]**. See §4.1 for the two constraints this schema must carry. |
| **One concession sentence.** Add a single "Some people say X, but I think Y" pair — two simple sentences, not a subordinate clause. | One added sentence in the model and the rubric. | **[INF]** on level fit; the target behaviour is sourced. `05` §5: the Band 7.5 discussion script is credited because "An alternative position is also considered but rejected" **[Q]**. U6 has not yet taught subordination (that arrives in U7), so the concession must be built from two coordinated simple sentences at this point in the year. |

**Level guard.** The concession is two A2 sentences. The schema is a label on a
structure the task already requires.

**Do not sell the modality.** `07` §4.6 records a **GAP**: no band-level evidence
was found for conditionals or modality. Yan & Staples found "all modal verbs"
correlated **0.03 (n.s.)** with band level — "the 'use modals to sound advanced'
advice has no support in this dataset" (`06` §6.2) **[T2]**. Teach the first
conditional for meaning; do not present it as a band lever.

### Unit 7 — Environmental protection
*Grammar: **complex sentences with adverbial time clauses** · Phonology: /bl/ vs
/kl/ · Listening: causes and effects of water pollution · Writing: notice,
~100–120w*

**Already trains.** Subordination crosses the band-4 threshold: band 4 GRA is
defined by "Subordinate clauses are rare and simple sentences predominate"
(`02` §3.5, `07` §4.6) **[D]**. The notice trains audience-appropriate register
and concision, and familiarity with a GT Reading Section 1 text type (§1). It is
the syllabus's only formal/public genre and its only task over 100 words.

| Upgrade | What changes in the lesson | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cause-and-result connectives on the water-pollution listening.** After the listening, write two sentences linking one named cause to one named effect using *because / as a result / this causes*. | Two sentences added to an existing listening→writing bridge whose content is already causes and effects. | Sourced. British Council's Task 1 teaching pack — pitched at **B1+**, just above where grade 8 sits — targets three systems for process description: purpose (`to`/`so that`/`in order to`), **cause and result** (*because, due to, results in, causes, as a result, consequently*), and relative clauses (`05` §2.6) **[Q]**. `05` §2.6's own implication: these systems are "inside or adjacent to the grade-8 grammar map (U7 complex sentences with adverbial clauses)". |
| **Register switch on the notice.** Write the same three facts twice — once as the notice, once as a message to a friend — and name what changed. | A second five-minute draft of an existing text. | **[SPEC]**. Register is what `01` §11.3 credits U7 with training, and Lexical Resource names "style (formal/informal)" as an indicator (`06` §3) **[Q]**. But **no source in the knowledge base tests a paired-register exercise**, and `07` §4.6 flags flexibility drills ("same content, multiple structures") as a *recommendation* grounded in descriptor wording, not in an intervention study. Label it speculative in the lesson. |

**Level guard.** Both work on text the unit already produces.

**On /bl/ vs /kl/.** Same finding and same framing as U6 — feature change on the
plosive is 77.4% of two-consonant onset errors, so this is a
voicing-and-aspiration drill, not a "cluster" drill (`07` §5.5.3, §8.3 VN-2)
**[S]**. **But /bl/ and /kl/ were not among the five pairs Tran & Nguyen tested**
(/br kr pr st tr/), so U7's support is by **extension from /kr/**, not direct.
Say so in the lesson rather than borrowing U6's warrant wholesale.

**Stop here on subordination.** `07` §8.2 is explicit: "Do not sell
subordination as the route up… Stop after U7's complex sentences establish the
band-5 threshold. Adding density beyond that is not supported" **[S/NS]**. U7 is
correctly placed and should **not** be extended.

### Unit 8 — Shopping
*Grammar: **adverbs of frequency; present simple** for future events · Phonology:
/sp/ vs /st/ · Speaking: pairs Q&A on a local market, **report back** · Writing:
paragraph, pros **or** cons of a shopping type*

**Already trains.** More than the syllabus's own framing suggests. A
frequency-adverb lesson built on the present simple generates dense
**third-person singular obligatory contexts** — and 3SG `-s` is, per `07`:

- one of the two structures whose Target-Like Use climbs across the *entire*
  band range 3→8 (0.34 → 0.53 → 0.67 → 0.81 → 0.84 → 1.0), and the one Banerjee
  et al. single out because "it was not affected by the learner's L1"
  (`07` §4.3) **[S/NS]**;
- the morpheme Vietnamese learners omit most: **32.26% of written obligatory
  contexts omitted** in Dong & Nguyen's corpus (N=32 third-year English majors),
  against 18.87% for plural `-s`, χ² = 12.693, p < .001, with *omission* rather
  than misuse being essentially the entire error (`07` §4.5) **[S]**.

`07` §4.5's conclusion is quoted rather than softened: "For a Vietnamese-market
course this is arguably the highest-value single grammar target in the document."

| Upgrade | What changes in the lesson | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Re-score the frequency drill as a 3SG `-s` obligatory-context drill, and weight the spoken version above the written one.** Same sentences ("She always shops online"); the mark is supplied-or-omitted in obligatory contexts, and the drill is run aloud and recorded before it is run in writing. | A scoring change and a modality change on an exercise that already exists. | Sourced. `07` §8.2 recommends exactly this, including the modality weighting: "spoken practice weighted above written (32.26% written omission vs a reported ~78.4% oral)" **[S]**. **Carry the caveat:** the ~78.4% oral figure is Dong & Nguyen *citing* Nguyen & Newton (2020), not their own data (`07` §4.5). |
| **"One turn, one subject" on the report-back**, as in U1. | Instruction line. | Sourced — see U1. |

**Level guard.** No new grammar, no new lexis, no new task. Only the mark scheme
and the order of modalities change.

**Two limits on the phonology side of this unit.** *(Added 2026-08-08 on `07`
§5.5.2–5.5.3.)* First, of U8's own contrast **/st/ was directly tested by Tran &
Nguyen and /sp/ was not**; and in *three*-consonant onsets the error profile
inverts — deletion becomes 78.2% and feature change falls to 16.6%, targeting the
pre-initial /s/ "[which] does not appear in any consonant clusters in the
Vietnamese language" **[S]**. Second, and more consequentially: the 3SG `-s`
drill above attacks the **morphological** half of a two-part problem. Disney & Le
(2024) show the phonological cause — coda /s~z/ elision, 28.4% at C1 — and the
morphological cause are **additive and independently attackable**, with the
interaction not significant (OR = 0.88, p = 0.519), and state that for any single
token "it would be impossible to ascertain a phonological or morphological cause"
**[S]**. `07` §8.3's VN-1 therefore requires **two** interventions, not one — and
the syllabus has no unit for the pronunciation half (§5.4). Do not let this
unit's drill be presented as covering both, and do not let a diagnostic tool
claim to tell the two causes apart on a given omission.

> **The syllabus undersells this unit.** `curriculum/syllabus.md` lists U8's
> grammar as "Adverbs of frequency; present simple for future events" and its
> "Notes on progression" nominate U11 and U12 as the units that "deserve the most
> practice material". On the evidence in `07`, U8 carries the best-evidenced
> accuracy discriminator in the document *and* the top Vietnamese-L1 error, and
> U5 carries the largest error category at every band. See §4.3.

## 4. Cross-cutting strands

Four findings cut across units rather than sitting in one, plus two upgrades
that apply to all twelve.

### 4.1 Balanced argument then position — one schema, tested

The syllabus notes that "The recurring rhetorical move is **balanced argument
then position**: U6, U8, and U11 all ask for advantages/disadvantages or a
for/against stance. Worth teaching that structure once, early, and reusing it."
The research plan repeats the instinct. **Tested against `02` and `05`, the
instinct survives — with three constraints that change how it must be taught.**

**What supports it.**

- Position clarity is a **named criterion element**, not style advice: Task
  Response assesses "how clearly the candidate opens the discourse, **establishes
  their position** and formulates conclusions" (`05` §3.1) **[Q]**. `05` §3.1's
  own implication is the three-move schema used here: *open → stand → close*.
- A position is required at **every band from 6 to 9**; its absence is a band 4–5
  feature — "A position is discernible, but the reader has to read carefully to
  find it" (band 4) (`02` §3.1) **[D]**.
- Discourse-level failures cap scripts whose sentence-level language is already
  at band 8 (`02` §7 item 5, §7.1) **[Q]**. The schema is not the soft part.

**Constraint 1 — it is a body-paragraph schema, not an essay schema.** The
80–100-word paragraph is a Task 2 *body paragraph* (`05` §1). Task 2 is 250+
words in ~40 minutes (`01` §5) **[V]** 3-0. Any lesson that calls the U6/U8/U11
task "IELTS Task 2 practice" is making a claim the format cannot support.

**Constraint 2 — a reusable schema taught three times is one step from a
memorised template, which is the explicitly penalised category.** `02` §7 items
1–4 and 21: a totally memorised answer is band 0; memorised phrases and formulaic
chunks used inappropriately are a band-4 Lexical Resource feature; copied rubric
must be discounted; memorised utterances are excluded from rateable language.
`02` §10.3 makes it a build rule: "Never train template or memorised language."
The schema must therefore be taught as a **question the writer answers** ("what
is my position, and where does the reader first see it?") and never as a sentence
frame to fill. This is the sharpest thing testing the plan's instinct produced.

**Constraint 3 — do not build a position *ladder*.** `05` §3.3 records that a
proposed delta claim about position quality across bands 7–9 was tested and
**not sustained** **[X]**. The band-6→7 delta in `02` §3.1 is quotable ("A
position is presented that is directly relevant" → "A clear and developed
position is presented") **[D]**; anything finer is not.

**Where the schema actually recurs.** Five places, not three: U6 (paragraph,
pros or cons), U8 (paragraph, pros or cons), U11 (persuasive paragraph) — plus
**U10 speaking** (pairs, pros and cons of online calls) and **U3 writing**
(causes and solutions, which needs a stance on which solution is best). The
extension from the syllabus's three to these five is **[INF]** — a reading of
the task descriptions in `curriculum/syllabus.md`, not a sourced finding.
Teaching the schema once in U6 and naming it in the other four costs nothing.

### 4.2 The phonology strand — right order, wrong end of the year

**The order is validated.** `02` §5.2 and `07` §5.1 both conclude the syllabus's
progression matches the descriptor architecture exactly: segmental contrasts
(U1–U8) are what the band-4→6 Pronunciation descriptors talk about; word stress
(U9), sentence stress (U11) and intonation (U12) are what the band-6→8
descriptors talk about. `02` §5.2 calls it "the single strongest existing
alignment in the course". `07` §8.3's recommendation is "Keep the U1–U12 order".

**The tension.** The discriminant loadings put the *last* quarter of the strand
highest:

| Feature | Loading on the discriminating function | Where it lands in the year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speech chunking | **.745** | U12 only, and only by inference (§3, U12) |
| Intonation | **.735** | U12 |
| Word stress | .668 | U9, U10 |
| Vowel & consonant errors | .551 | U1–U8 |

(`07` §5.2, Isaacs et al., N=80, 8 examiners) **[S]**. Two further facts sharpen
it: prosodic training **transfers to spontaneous speech while segmental training
does not** (Derwing & Rossiter 2003, via Dauer, `07` §5.4) **[S]**; and the
syllabus's own progression note says "Later units need audio, not just print."

**What follows — three consequences, stated rather than left implicit.**

1. **Build the U9–U12 audio first.** The highest-value quarter of the phonology
   strand is also the quarter that cannot exist without recorded material, and it
   sits at the end of the year where build effort typically runs out. Reversing
   the build order costs nothing pedagogically and de-risks the part of the strand
   the evidence rates highest. **[INF]** — a production recommendation, not a
   finding.
2. **Seed chunking from U1 as a task habit, not a lesson.** The report-back tasks
   in U1, U3, U8 and U10 can carry "pause at each stage" from the first unit,
   because `06` §2 credits stage-marking pauses under Coherence — an official
   indicator, not a pronunciation add-on **[C]** 3-0. This puts the .745 feature
   into the course from week one **without reordering the syllabus** and without
   requiring audio. The formal treatment stays at U12.
3. **A learner who coasts after U8 has met only the .551 feature.** That is the
   honest description of the current shape, and it is the argument for (1) and
   (2) rather than for resequencing the national curriculum's phonology targets,
   which this course does not control.

**But Isaacs' loadings are not the only ranking, and for this population they are
not the governing one.** *(Added 2026-08-08, on `07` §5.5.8, which did not exist
when this section was written.)* The Vietnamese-specific evidence produces a
**different order**, and the two do not need reconciling because **they answer
different questions**:

| | Isaacs et al. (`07` §5.2) | The Vietnamese-L1 evidence (`07` §5.5) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Population | 80 test-takers, 8 accredited examiners | 16–36 Vietnamese learners per study, several under 20 |
| Band range | **Discriminated bands 5–8** | Features still failing **at C1, at IELTS 6.5, in fourth-year English majors** |
| Question asked | What separates a good performance from a very good one — a **ceiling** question | What stops a Vietnamese speaker being understood at all — a **floor** question |
| Top-ranked phonological feature | Chunking .745, intonation .735 | **Coda obstruents, above all final /s~z/** |
| Where segmentals rank | **Lowest** (.551) | Ranks 1, 2 and 3 of eight |

**Grade-8 learners are at the floor** — `07` §6.3 places them at A2 heading to
B1 — **so where the two rankings conflict, the floor evidence governs.** `07` §7
item 8 states it as a headline finding: "For Vietnamese learners specifically,
the highest-value pronunciation target is the one Isaacs ranks lowest."

This does **not** reverse consequences (1)–(3) above, and it is important not to
over-read it. The U1–U12 *order* is still validated, on descriptor grounds
(`07` §8.3: "Keep the U1–U12 order"). U9–U12 audio is still the right build
priority, because item 4 of `07` §5.5.8's ordering — deaccenting and question
tunes — is **the one place the two rankings agree, and both are strong**
(Isaacs' intonation .735; Nguyễn & Đào 2018, N = 30, learners "failed to
deaccent", rise-overuse on Wh- and tag questions) **[S]**. What changes is the
reading of the *segmental* end: U1–U8 is not merely "the low-loading quarter we
must get through". For this population it is where the binding constraint sits —
and the part of it that matters most, coda consonants, is the part the syllabus
never reaches (§5.4).

Two limits on the Vietnamese half of that table, carried from `07` §5.5: every
source in it is **[S]**, singly-sourced and unverified by any panel; and the
ordering itself rests on *difficulty and persistence* data plus one N = 1
intelligibility result, not on a Vietnamese intelligibility study (`07` §5.5.7
**GAP**).

**Three cautions that must travel with all of this.** Isaacs' classification
accuracy was only **47%** for Pronunciation, with band 6 misclassified **80%** of
the time (`07` §5.2) **[S]** — the upper bands are not cleanly separable by any
measured feature set, and `07` §8.3 forbids tools that claim otherwise. The two
*strongest* loadings on the Pronunciation function were not phonological at all
(grammatical accuracy .945, lexical richness .863), which the report treats as
descriptor-transparency contamination **[S]**. And `06` §3 records that the
proposition "pronunciation is scored suprasegmentals-first" was tested and **not
sustained** **[X]** — the ordering argument here rests on `02` §5.2's descriptor
reading and `07` §5.2's loadings, not on that claim.

### 4.3 The grammar ladder — which units actually carry the IELTS value

Three findings from `07` reshape the syllabus's own priority ordering.

| Finding | Source | Consequence for the unit map |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Syntactic complexity does **not** predict band; clauses/T-unit and dependent-clauses/clause both **peak at band 7 and fall at band 8** | `07` §4.2, Banerjee et al., 275 scripts **[S/NS]**; Brown, bands 5–8 **[T2]** (`02` §4.3) | U7 establishes subordination and that is enough. Do not extend it. U11–U12's reported speech is not "more complex therefore more valuable". |
| **Determiners** are the top error type at every band 5.5–7.5 and improve most across the range | `07` §4.4, Müller & Han, 500 samples **[S]** | **U5 is the syllabus's highest-leverage grammar lesson.** One unit of articles is not proportionate to that; hence the spine upgrade. |
| **3SG `-s`** is both a strong band discriminator across 3→8 *and* L1-independent, *and* the morpheme Vietnamese learners most omit (32.26% written) | `07` §4.3 **[S/NS]**, §4.5 **[S]** | **U8 carries the highest-value single grammar target for a Vietnamese-market course**, inside a lesson the syllabus presents as light. |

**Where the syllabus's own ordering undersells a unit — stated plainly.**

`curriculum/syllabus.md`'s "Notes on progression" says: "Units 11 and 12 are the
heaviest and deserve the most practice material." Measured against this evidence
base, that priority is **not IELTS-supported**:

- Reported speech (U11, U12) sits inside `07` §4.6's **GAP**: no band-level
  evidence was found for conditionals, modality, reported speech, cleft or
  inversion, and `07` warns that any claim like "inversion is a band-8 structure"
  "would be invented."
- Meanwhile U5 (articles) attacks the largest error category at every band, and
  U8 (present simple / frequency) attacks the best-evidenced accuracy
  discriminator plus the top Vietnamese-L1 omission.

The honest formulation, and the one to carry into any build decision: **this is
not a claim that reported speech is low-value teaching.** It is the national
curriculum's target, it is genuinely difficult, and difficulty is a legitimate
reason to build practice material. The claim is narrower — *for IELTS
specifically*, nothing in this knowledge base supports ranking U11–U12's grammar
above U5's and U8's, and U11–U12's real IELTS payload is **phonological**
(sentence stress, intonation) rather than grammatical.

**What the ladder says to train instead of complexity:** flexibility. The word
that appears at every band from 6 up in both Writing and Speaking GRA is
*flexibility*, not *complexity* (`07` §4.1) **[D]**, and `07` §8.2's exercise
recommendation is "express the same proposition three ways". At grade 8 that is a
three-line exercise, not a new syllabus strand.

### 4.4 Report-back tasks as proto-Part-2 long turns

Four units end a speaking task with report-back: **U1** (group Q&A on own
leisure), **U3** (pair/group Q&A on club details), **U8** (pairs Q&A on a local
market or centre), **U10** (pairs, pros and cons of online calls). These are the
closest thing the syllabus has to a Part 2 long turn, whose official skills
statement is "your ability to **speak at length** on a given topic, using
appropriate language and **organising your ideas logically**" (`06` §4) **[Q]**.

**The finding that should govern all four** is the Band 5 Vietnamese candidate in
`06` §5. Tina's long turn did not fail for lack of language — the examiner
credits her with keeping going and with good items ("widen my knowledge", "to
overcome or try to get over the problem"). It was capped because the turn had no
single subject: "she **describes several interests rather than one interest**,
makes her hard to follow at times and leads to some loss of coherence" **[Q]**.
That is the official Coherence indicator — relevance of every spoken sentence to
the purpose of the turn (`06` §2) **[C]** 3-0 — visible in a real rating, on a
Vietnamese candidate, on the U1 topic.

Three rules follow, all A2/B1-safe, all free:

| Rule | Warrant |
| --- | --- |
| **One turn, one subject.** Name the subject before speaking; drop anything that does not serve it. | `06` §2 **[C]** 3-0; `06` §5 **[Q]**. `06` §5's own implication: "a rule a 14-year-old can apply and self-check, and it is the difference the examiner names." |
| **Mark your stages out loud** — a pause plus a marker at each new stage. | `06` §2 **[C]** 3-0: pausing and discourse markers are listed as coherence *resources*, on the credit side. |
| **Answer, then develop.** | `06` §6.3 **[T2]**, with the caveat that no criterion names it. |

**Two things not to teach here.** Padding to fill time is not neutral — under the
relevance indicator it is off-purpose material inside a scored turn (`06` §2).
And `06` §4 records a **GAP**: nothing verified is known about how the Part 2
preparation minute is best used, so no note-taking technique should be taught as
effective.

**And a ceiling to be honest about.** The official speech-function list the test
elicits runs "providing personal information; expressing a preference; …
comparing; expressing opinions; summarising; explaining; … suggesting;
contrasting; justifying opinions; narrating and paraphrasing; **speculating;
analysing**" (`06` §6.3) **[T2]**. Roughly two-thirds is inside grade-8 range.
The abstract end — speculating, analysing, justifying — is Part 3's frontier and,
in `06` §6.3's words, "the honest ceiling of a grade-8 course." It belongs to §6,
not to an upgrade.

### 4.5 Two upgrades that apply to all twelve units

| Upgrade | What changes | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pre-teach each unit's topic lexis *before* its reading and listening, as a first-class step.** | Sequencing of an existing vocabulary set. | Sourced, and quantified. Nation: treating highly repeated topic words as known means "only 5,000 words would be needed to reach 98% coverage, **a saving of a 1,000 word-level**" (`07` §2.2) **[S]**. A themed 12-unit course is already doing this by accident; `07` §2.2 calls it "one of the cleanest arguments in the whole knowledge base for per-unit lexis sets taught before the reading". |
| **Attach a confidence rating to each listening answer, and show the learner their calibration alongside their score.** | One extra column on every listening exercise. | Sourced. Phakiti (376 students): "74% (Section 3) to 86% (Section 1) of the test-takers were miscalibrated"; on very difficult questions they were 25% overconfident (*d* = 0.62) and up to 93% miscalibrated (`03` §6.6) **[T2]**. His own recommendation is explicit feedback on whether a learner is realistic, overconfident or underconfident. `03` §6.6 calls this "the most directly buildable finding in the document… the only trainable behaviour here with an effect size attached." |

## 5. Gaps grade 8 cannot fix — deferred, not upgraded

These are named rather than forced into an upgrade. Each is handed to §6.

### 5.1 Passives — the largest evidence-backed grammar gap

Passives are **absent from the grade-8 syllabus** and are, with 3SG `-s`, one of
the two structures whose Target-Like Use climbs across the entire band range:
0.41 → 0.43 → 0.66 → 0.71 → 0.77 → 1.0 across bands 3–8 (`07` §4.3, Banerjee et
al.) **[S/NS]**. `07` §8.2's instruction is direct: "Passives are absent from the
grade-8 syllabus and are the second-best accuracy discriminator. **Add them as
the first post-grade-8 structure.**"

They are also the register of the genre U9 half-bridges to: British Council's
Task 1 process model text "is dense with agentless passives ('an electric signal
**is sent** via electrical wires to a pump')" (`05` §2.6) **[Q]**.

**Handled honestly:** U9's imperative/procedural language is a real and
level-appropriate route into process description (`01` §11.3), and it is **not a
substitute for the passive**. An imperative ("Turn off the electricity") and an
agentless passive ("The electricity is turned off") are different structures with
different registers, and nothing in this knowledge base suggests the first
teaches the second. No upgrade is offered. This is the first item in §6.

The whole-essay task is a different object from the body paragraph the course trains (`05` §1). Teaching it here would be the level break the brief forbids. |
| **Task 1 data description** (line/bar/pie/table charts) | Requires trend lexis, and `05` §2.6 records a **GAP**: no Tier-1 inventory of trend lexis for data charts was located, and `05` instructs "Do not fill this from memory." Nothing can be built here yet even if level allowed it. |

If GT ever became a target, `01` §11.3 specifies what the lesson would need: three-bullet prompt, fixed salutation, register selected from personal / semi-formal / formal. |

### 5.4 Syllable-final consonants — the syllabus's largest evidence-backed phonology gap

*Rewritten 2026-08-08. This section previously read "Vietnamese-L1 pronunciation
— pending, not absent" and treated `07` §5.5 as a blocking gap. **§5.5 is now
closed**, by a targeted pass that retrieved seven qualifying sources. Everything
in it is **[S]** — quoted from primary documents in the research cache, none
through the adversarial panel — and the sample sizes are uniformly small. The
markers below are inherited at that strength and are not upgraded.*

**The finding this file has to carry.** `07` §5.5.8 ranks Vietnamese-specific
pronunciation targets for this learner population, and **rank 1 is syllable-final
consonants, above all coda /s~z/ — which no unit in the twelve teaches.**

The structural fact: Hanoi Vietnamese "licenses eight segments in coda position:
three unreleased voiceless obstruents /p t k/ …, three nasals /m n ŋ/, and two
approximants /j w/" (Kirby 2011, *JIPA* 41(3); `07` §5.5.1) **[S]**. No fricative
or affricate may close a Vietnamese syllable in **any** dialect, and no coda
cluster exists at all. English final /s z f v θ ð ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ b d g/ are therefore
all outside the L1 coda inventory.

The measured consequence: Disney & Le (2024), N = 16 Hanoi learners **at or above
C1**, 3,858 tokens from 240 minutes of naturalistic monologue — **28.4% omission
of final /s~z/ overall, and 46.7% when the /s~z/ sits in a coda cluster** against
19.7% after a vowel (`07` §5.5.2) **[S]**. The authors' own framing is that these
are the *most* advanced learners, chosen deliberately: "If even these people
exhibit extensive elision … then for less advanced learners, the vast majority,
the situation must be even more challenging." **Grade-8 learners are the less
advanced majority.**

**Measured against the syllabus, this is a hole, not a weak spot:**

| Units | Phonology target | Does it touch a coda obstruent? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| U1–U4 | Vowel contrasts | No |
| **U5** | **/n/ vs /ŋ/** | **Nominally yes — but both are already legal Vietnamese codas** (Kirby's /m n ŋ/), so the contrast sits inside the L1 inventory and is not the difficulty the evidence names |
| U6–U8 | Onset clusters | No — onset only |
| U9–U12 | Word stress, sentence stress, intonation | No |

`07` §5.5.8's verdict is quoted rather than paraphrased: "**The best-evidenced
Vietnamese difficulty in this document has no lesson.**" `07` §8.3 makes it the
first of its four shippable Vietnamese targets (VN-1) and says the same thing:
"Highest-value Vietnamese target in the document, and **the syllabus has no unit
for it.**"

**Why no upgrade is offered here.** Every other entry in §3 is implementable
inside an existing lesson. This one is not: there is no unit whose phonology slot
it could occupy without displacing a national-curriculum target this course does
not control. Adding a coda-consonant unit is a syllabus change, and syllabus
changes belong to §6, not to §3. Two things §3 *can* honestly do, and both are
recorded in place: name the limit of U5's nasal contrast (§3, U5), and note that
U8's 3SG `-s` drill attacks only the morphological half of a two-part problem
(§3, U8) — `07` §5.5.2 establishes that the phonological and morphological causes
are **additive and independently attackable** (interaction n.s., p = 0.519), so
one intervention cannot cover both.

**What this file may now claim, and what it still may not.** `07` §8.3's
Vietnamese block is four rows — **three positive targets (VN-1 to VN-3) and one
prohibition list (VN-4)** — and its instruction is "Ship the four targets below;
**ship nothing else Vietnamese-specific**." Carried here:

| | Claimable | Where it lands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **VN-1** | Coda obstruents, above all final /s~z/; drill in **content** words, not function words | **No unit.** This section; deferred to §6 |
| **VN-2** | Onset-cluster **voicing and aspiration** — feature change is 77.4% of two-consonant onset errors, deletion only 22.2% | U6, U7 (§3) |
| **VN-3** | Deaccenting and question tunes — the one place the Vietnamese evidence and Isaacs' loadings agree | U11, U12 (§4.2) |
| **VN-4** | **What not to claim:** no Vietnamese-specific **vowel** guidance (no qualifying source, `07` §5.5.6); no **region-specific** North/South guidance (`07` §5.5.5); no justification of word-stress teaching by Vietnamese **tone transfer** (contested at the root — Brunelle 2017, N = 18); and no prediction of errors by contrasting the two inventories, which is the inference Disney & Le's own conclusion warns against | Binding on all twelve units |

**The syllable-timing tension is resolved, not deferred.** This section previously
recorded a standing conflict: `02` §7 item 18 named syllable-timed rhythm as a
band-7 limiter and appended the parenthetical "Highly relevant to Vietnamese L1,
a syllable-timed language" — a typological claim with no phonology source.
**That parenthetical has been removed from `02`** (see the note under `02` §7),
because `07` §5.5.4 records a standing **GAP**: no rhythm-metric characterisation
of Vietnamese-accented English exists, Grabe & Low (2002) excludes Vietnamese,
and the only rhythm-metric study of Vietnamese itself hedges its own
classification. The *examiner* finding about syllable-timing is well sourced and
stands; the L1 typology behind it does not, and neither file asserts it now.

Two corrections of fact while this was re-checked: the two commented performances
in which syllable-timing is named are **Xin (China)** and **Alexandra** (L1 not
stated in the source) — this section previously said "German and Chinese", which
misreads `06` §5; Hendrik (Germany) is limited by filler overuse and segmental
mispronunciation, not by rhythm. And `06` §5's corpus is **four** commentaries,
which is now what `02` says too (`09` §8.3 item 1).

**Three sub-questions remain genuinely open**, and no lesson may be built on any
of them:

> **GAP** — `07` §5.5.7: **no intelligibility *ranking* of Vietnamese features
> with an adequate sample.** The ordering above is built from *difficulty and
> persistence* evidence plus a single N = 1 intelligibility result (Zielinski
> 2008, three L2 speakers, one of them Vietnamese). It is not a measurement of
> what damages comprehension most for this population, and must be labelled that
> way wherever it is used.

> **GAP** — `07` §5.5.6: **no Vietnamese-L1 evidence on the vowel contrasts
> U1–U4 teaches** (/ʊ/–/uː/, /ə/–/ɪ/). The one directly relevant item located is
> conference proceedings, below `07`'s source policy, and concerns KIT/FLEECE —
> not a syllabus contrast. **Do not close this by contrastive reasoning.** U1–U4
> are taught on the general phonetic case.

> **GAP** — `07` §5.5.5: **no quantified North/South comparison of *English*
> production.** The two segmental studies are Northern (Hanoi) and the prosodic
> study is Southern, so the two halves of §5.5 are not drawn from the same
> population. The one defensible cross-regional statement is that **no Vietnamese
> dialect permits a final fricative**. Build no region-specific guidance.

## 6. The post-grade-8 arc

What a follow-on course must cover so that today's decisions do not dead-end.
Labelled by **CEFR**, per `07` §8.4 — never by IELTS band.

### 6.1 The labelling rule, restated because it constrains everything below

The IELTS↔CEFR crosswalk is derivable from Cambridge's published material at 5.5
and above (`07` §6.2) **[S]**, is **conflicted at 6.5** (the Cambridge Scale puts
6.5 at 176, inside B2; Cambridge's prose pairs 6.5 with C1 — so the honest label
there is "B2/C1"), and **bottoms out at band 4.0 = the B1 threshold**, sourced to
the UK Home Office SELT list, Cambridge's CEFR diagram and ielts.org's own prose
(`07` §6.2a) **[S]**. **Below that there is nothing to derive**: IELTS for UKVI
has no A1 row and no A2 row, and A1/A2 are certified by IELTS Life Skills, which
is unscored Pass/Fail and has no published conversion to the 9-band scale.
Cambridge's own methodological warning travels with all of it: "Exact
equivalences cannot always be demonstrated, only broad comparability" **[S]**.

Since grade-8 learners sit at A2, below the published floor, **no stage of this
arc may be labelled with an IELTS band.** That is now a positive finding rather
than an absence of evidence: there is no band to use, not merely a band we could
not look up. The CEFR side is fully sourced — the Companion Volume supplies
A2/B1/B2/C1 descriptors for general linguistic range, vocabulary range and
control, grammatical accuracy and phonological control (`07` §5.3, §6.3)
**[S]** — and the Oxford 3000/5000 tags every item A1–C1 (`07` §2.3) **[V]** 3-0.

### 6.2 The arc

| Stage | Vocabulary | Grammar | Phonology | Discourse / skills |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Now — A2 → B1** (where grade 8 sits) | Oxford 3000 backbone, A2/B1-tagged; per-unit topic sets pre-taught. ### 6.3 Four constraints the follow-on course inherits

1. **Progress is not monotonic at A2→B1.** Müller & Han found "a period of mixed
   regression and improvement… for scores 6.5 and 7.0 where scores jump around
   unexpectedly", and the CEFR Companion Volume independently reports that
   "inaccuracy increases at around B1 as the learner is beginning to use language
   more independently and creatively" (`07` §4.4) **[S]**. A progress tool that
   reports "your error rate went up, you got worse" will be **wrong precisely at
   the transition these learners are making**.
2. **Emergence, not mastery, is the evidence of progress.** CEFR Companion
   Volume: "the primary evidence for second language acquisition (that is,
   progress) is the emergence of new forms and not their mastery… When learners
   are tackling more complex tasks, their control of their language naturally
   suffers, and this is a healthy process" (`07` §6.3) **[S]**.
3. **Vocabulary must be trained in both modalities.** Orthographic and
   phonological vocabulary correlate only .46 with each other and predict
   different IELTS skills — written vocabulary predicts Reading and Writing,
   heard vocabulary predicts Speaking and Listening (`07` §2.4, Milton et al.,
   N=30) **[S/NS]**. A trainer that only shows words in print trains half the
   construct. N=30 is small and `07` says so.
4. **The rarity-versus-formulaicity question is open and must stay open.**
   `07` §3 lays out an experimental causal finding that low-frequency words raise
   examiner ratings (Naismith & Juffs, 47 examiners) **[V]** 3-0 against
   observational findings that high-band lexical resource is formulaic and built
   from high-frequency words (Read & Nation, 88 speaking tests; Banerjee, 275
   scripts) **[S/NS]**. `07` §8.1's instruction — do not optimise for either —
   carries forward unchanged. What **both** sides support is teaching items
   **inside collocations**; that is the part the follow-on course can build on
   safely.
