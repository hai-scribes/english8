## 1. The scoring architecture

> "There are nine bands and four criteria: Fluency and Coherence; Lexical
> Resource; Grammatical Range and Accuracy; and Pronunciation."
> — [IELTS Speaking Key Assessment Criteria](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-speaking-key-assessment-criteria.pdf) **[C]** 3-0

This is the binding architecture for **all three Parts**. There is no separate
criterion for Part 2 long-turn content and none for Part 3 reasoning quality:
the same four scales are applied to whatever the candidate produces across the
whole 11–14 minutes. Anything a course teaches as "Part 3 technique" has to cash
out in one of those four scales or it is not scored.

The public ielts.org description of the four criteria, in test-taker language,
adds one gloss worth keeping: Lexical Resource "also includes the ability to
express yourself using **alternative vocabulary when you don't know a particular
word**", and Pronunciation "assesses your ability to speak in a way which can be
understood **without too much effort**"
([IELTS Academic format — Speaking](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking)) **[Q]**.

> **Teaching implication.** Four scales, equal footing, no content scale — so a
> learner who prepares "good ideas" for Speaking is preparing something that is
> not directly scored. What is scored is the language the ideas are carried in.

## 2. Coherence — the one Speaking criterion this pass established in detail

> "clear marking (**with appropriate use of pausing, and spoken discourse
> markers and fillers**) of stages in a discussion, narration or argument ▪
> **relevance of spoken sentences to the general purpose of a turn** ▪ use of
> cohesive devices within and between spoken sentences (e.g. logical connectors,
> pronouns and conjunctions)."
> — [Speaking Key Assessment Criteria](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-speaking-key-assessment-criteria.pdf) **[C]** 3-0

The full list of coherence indicators is four items, the first being "logical
sequencing of 'spoken sentences'" **[Q]**. Two consequences run against common
prep advice:

1. **Pausing and fillers are listed as coherence *resources*, not defects.** The
   official criterion names them as a means of marking stages in a discussion,
   narration or argument. Whatever else fillers do (see §3, where the fluency
   wording sits), under Coherence they are on the credit side of the ledger when
   used to mark structure.
2. **Every spoken sentence must be relevant to the purpose of the turn.** This
   is a direct constraint on the widespread tactic of padding a Part 2 long turn
   with whatever comes to mind in order to fill two minutes. Padding is not
   neutral under this indicator; it is off-purpose material inside a scored turn.

The criteria document also defines its unit of analysis, which matters for any
rubric we write:

> "A 'spoken sentence' is the unit of speech which most closely corresponds to a
> written sentence. It is usually the same as a simple or complex written
> sentence, but may also include verbless structures, sometimes involving
> ellipsis, which perform a sentence-like function but lack elements which would
> be found in acceptable writing. Such units will usually be further
> distinguished by a pause at the end, which may be very brief, and 'final'
> intonation, typically a pitch fall." **[Q]**

> **Teaching implication.** Two teachable, self-checkable moves fall out of
> this, both usable at A2/B1: (a) *mark your stages out loud* — a pause plus a
> marker at each new stage of a description is credited behaviour, not filler;
> (b) *one turn, one purpose* — before speaking, name what this turn is for, and
> drop anything that does not serve it. Our report-back speaking tasks (U1, U3,
> U8, U10) are the natural home for both.

## 3. The other three criteria — primary text, no gloss

The indicator lists below are quoted verbatim from the Tier-1
[Speaking Key Assessment Criteria](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-speaking-key-assessment-criteria.pdf)
**[Q]**. Interpretations built on them in this pass — that fluency has exactly
two indicators and that fast speech is uncredited; that paraphrase is credited
"regardless of hesitation"; that pronunciation is scored suprasegmentals-first
and that accent per se is never penalised — were each tested and **not
sustained** **[X]**. The text is therefore reproduced without those readings.
Do not restore them from memory; a second pass should re-test them.

**Fluency and Coherence** — "the ability to talk with normal levels of
continuity, rate and effort, and to link ideas and language together to form
coherent, connected speech." Key indicators of fluency:

- "speech rate: ideally, not too slow (hard to keep links between words/propositions in mind)"
- "speech continuity: ideally, flow of speech will not be excessively interrupted by false starts, backtracking, functionless repetitions of words and phrases, and/or pausing during which the test taker searches for words."

**Lexical Resource** — "the range of vocabulary at the test taker's disposal,
which will influence the range of topics which they can discuss, and the
precision with which meanings are expressed and attitudes conveyed." Key
indicators:

- "variety of words used"
- adequacy and appropriacy of vocabulary in relation to: "referential meaning (the correct labelling of things and concepts)"; "style (formal/informal)"; "collocation (including idiomatic expressions)"; "indicating the speaker's attitude to content (whether favourable, neutral or unfavourable)"
- "ability to use paraphrase (getting round a vocabulary gap by using other words), with or without noticeable hesitation."

**Grammatical Range and Accuracy** — key indicators of **range**: "the length of
spoken sentences"; "appropriate use of subordinate clauses within clauses and
phrases"; "complexity of the verb phrase (correct use of auxiliaries in
continuous/perfect aspect, modality and passive voice)"; "complexity of other
phrases (use of pre- and post-modification…)"; "range of sentence structures,
especially to move elements around for information focus." Key indicators of
**accuracy**: "error density"; "the communicative effect of error (its effect on
intelligibility and precision or expression)."

**Pronunciation** — "the accurate and sustained use of a range of phonological
features to convey meaningful messages." Key indicators:

- "the ability to divide speech into meaningful utterances or chunks within spoken sentences"
- "the appropriate use of rhythm and stress timing, and the linking of sounds, using features such as elision to produce connected speech"
- "the use of stress (e.g. emphatic/contrastive) and intonation to enhance meaning"
- "the production of sounds at the word and phoneme level (e.g. word stress, vowel and consonant production), and the degree of effort required of the listener to understand these"
- "the overall effect of accent on intelligibility."

> **Teaching implication.** The pronunciation indicator list runs
> chunking → rhythm/stress-timing/linking → stress and intonation for meaning →
> segments → accent effect. Our phonology strand runs the opposite way
> (segmental contrasts U1–U8 → word stress U9 → sentence stress U11 →
> intonation U12). That is not a mismatch to fix but a hand-off to plan: the
> suprasegmental half of the strand is where the Speaking criterion puts its
> weight, and it currently lands last, in units that need audio.

## 4. The three Parts

From the official test-format page
([IELTS Academic format — Speaking](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking)) **[Q]**.
Timings and structure also belong to `01-exam-structure.md`; what matters here
is the **skills statement** attached to each Part, because those sentences say
what the Part is *for*.

| Part | Length | What's involved (official wording) | "What skills are tested" (official wording) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 — Introduction and interview | 4–5 min | Examiner checks identity, then "asks you general questions on some familiar topics, such as home, family, work, studies or interests." | "your ability to give opinions and information on everyday topics and common experiences or situations by answering a range of questions." |
| 2 — Long turn | 3–4 min incl. preparation | "The examiner gives you a task card… The card tells you what points you should include in your talk and instructs you to explain one aspect of the topic. You have **one minute to prepare**… you should be able to think of appropriate things to say, and have time to structure your talk so that you keep talking for **2 minutes**." | "your ability to **speak at length** on a given topic, using appropriate language and **organising your ideas logically**. You will need to think about your own experiences." |
| 3 — Discussion | 4–5 min | "you and the examiner discuss issues related to the topic in Part 2 in a more general and abstract way and, where appropriate, in greater depth." | "your ability to explain your opinions and to **analyse, discuss and speculate** about issues." |

The Part 3 skills sentence is the closest thing to an official statement of what
Part 3 rewards: **analysis, discussion, speculation** — plus the shift to "more
general and abstract". Note also that the task card "instructs you to explain
one aspect of the topic": the long turn is not a list of four bullet points, it
has a designated explanatory move.

An adjacent Tier-1 anchor from the public Speaking Band Descriptors, worth
recording because it is the only place the descriptors speak about topic
handling: the phrase **"topic development"** appears only at the top two bands —
Band 9 "Topic development is fully coherent and appropriately extended", Band 8
"Topic development is coherent, appropriate and relevant"; bands 7 and below
speak instead of keeping going and producing long turns ("Able to keep going and
readily produce long turns without noticeable effort", Band 7)
([Speaking Band Descriptors, public version](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-speaking-band-descriptors.pdf)) **[Q]**.
The band-by-band analysis belongs to `02-band-descriptors.md`; the structural
point here mirrors the Task 1 overview finding in `05-writing.md` §2.4 — *the
named requirement changes as you go up the scale*.

> **GAP** — not established in this research pass: any verified evidence on how
> the **one-minute preparation time** is best used, or what note-taking during it
> does to performance. The official article
> [How to use the preparation time in IELTS Speaking Part 2](https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/how-to-use-the-preparation-time-in-ielts-speaking-part-2)
> was fetched into the corpus but none of its claims survived to verification.

> **GAP** — not established in this research pass: a taxonomy of **Part 3
> question types**. The IDP long-turn article
> ([here](https://ielts.idp.com/prepare/article-ielts-speaking-test-long-turn-part-2))
> and the ielts.org
> [Demystifying the IELTS Speaking test](https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/demystifying-the-ielts-speaking-test)
> article are in the corpus; no claim from either was confirmed.

| Alexandra | **Band 7** | "There is some hesitation, but it is **mainly content-related** as she seeks to clarify her ideas before expressing them. **Coherence is not affected by these slight pauses**… She has a tendency to use **syllable-timing**, which prevents her sustaining appropriate rhythm over longer utterances… This test taker only just achieves Band 7, owing to a weaker performance in grammar." (IELTS Online Tutorial, "Speaking test: Examiner comments for Alexandra"; document present in the research corpus, canonical URL not captured this pass) |

The Tina comment is the most directly useful item in this file for a Vietnamese
grade-8 course. The long turn did not fail for lack of language — she "manages
to talk at some length" and produces good items ("widen my knowledge", "to
overcome or try to get over the problem"). It was capped because **the turn had
no single subject**: she described several interests instead of one, and
coherence went with it. That is precisely the Coherence indicator from §2 —
relevance of every spoken sentence to the purpose of the turn — showing up in a
real rating.

Two patterns recur across the set and are worth noting as observations about
this small corpus, not as rules: **syllable-timing** is named as a rhythm
limiter in two of the four comments, and in three of the four the *decisive*
weakness is grammar or pronunciation rather than ideas.

> **Teaching implication.** "One turn, one subject" is a rule a 14-year-old can
> apply and self-check, and it is the difference the examiner names. Add it to
> the report-back tasks now; it costs nothing and it is the same discipline as a
> paragraph's topic sentence.

> **GAP** — not established in this research pass: any officially commented
> **band 8 or 9** Speaking performance. Everything about the top of the Speaking
> scale in this repo derives from descriptor wording, not from an exemplar.

## 6. Tier-2 evidence: what actually varies across bands

These are IELTS-funded research findings about *tendencies in candidate
performance*. They are legitimate for designing practice and self-assessment
targets; they are **not** scoring rules and must never be quoted as such.

### 6.1 No single feature drives a band

- "The analysis has not identified any individual speaking feature that can be seen to distinguish between the score bands. Rather, it has proposed that in any given IST, a **cluster of assessable speaking features** can be seen to lead toward a given score." — [Seedhouse, Harris, Naeb & Üstünel (2014)](https://ielts.org/cdn/Research/relationship-between-speaking-features-and-band-descriptors-seedhouse-et-al-2014.pdf), 60 tests across bands 5–8 **[T2]**
- "no one measure drives the rating; rather a range of performance features contribute to the overall impression of the candidate's proficiency." — [Brown (2006)](https://ielts.org/cdn/Research/candidate-discourse-in-revised-ielts-speaking-test-brown-2006.pdf), 20 interviews **[T2]**
- Both studies report a non-monotonic result for grammatical complexity: "band 8 utterances are less complex than those of band 7" (Seedhouse et al. 2014, confirming Brown 2006) **[T2]**. Complexity is not a ladder you climb indefinitely.

In Brown's ANOVAs the only fluency-side measure reaching significance across
levels was **total amount of speech** (p = .005); ratio of pause time, pauses to
words, speech rate, and average turn length did not reach significance in that
sample. On the lexical side, **none** of the frequency measures (K1%, K2%,
Academic Word List %, off-list %, type/token ratio, word length) reached
significance. On grammar, **proportion of error-free utterances** (p = .004) and
**ratio of specific basic errors to words** (p = .002) did **[T2]**.

### 6.2 What correlates with band level in the largest recent corpus

[Yan & Staples (2023)](https://ielts.org/cdn/Research/investigating-cognitive-and-social-aspects-of-ielts-speaking-performances-across-proficiency-levels-yan-et-al-2023.pdf),
280 benchmark performances, bands 5–8, Pearson correlations with score level **[T2]**:

| Feature | r | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speech rate | **0.67** | Higher band, faster |
| Type–token ratio | **0.54** | Higher band, more lexical variety |
| Morphological errors | **−0.51** | Higher band, fewer |
| Mean duration of silent pauses | −0.44 | Higher band, shorter pauses |
| High functional-load **vowel** errors | −0.43 | Higher band, fewer |
| "Space" (proportion of prominent words to total) | −0.42 | Higher band, *fewer* words made prominent |
| Mean length of run | 0.40 | Higher band, longer unbroken stretches |
| Stance adverbials — certainty | 0.27 | Higher band, more |
| Level tone | −0.24 | Higher band, less |
| First-person pronouns | −0.27 | Higher band, fewer |
| Second-person pronouns | 0.20 | Higher band, more |
| All modal verbs | 0.03 (n.s.) | No relationship |
| Premodifying nouns | −0.02 (n.s.) | No relationship |

Three of these deserve emphasis because they cut against intuition. **Prominence
is negative**: lower-band speakers made ~39% of words prominent, band 8 ~31% —
stressing everything is a low-band signature, and selective stress is the
high-band one. **Level tone is negative** while falling tone is mildly positive
(0.17): flat, unresolved intonation contours go with lower bands. And **modal
verbs show no relationship at all** — the "use modals to sound advanced" advice
has no support in this dataset.

Kang, Egbert & Miao (2023) independently found "speech rate, grammatical
complexity, the use of L1 words, intonation level-tone choice, and segmental
errors were significant predictors of IELTS speaking proficiency scores with
R² = 40–60%", and — again non-monotonically — "as proficiency improved, IELTS
test-takers spoke faster but their grammatical structure became **less** complex"
([Kang et al. 2023](https://ielts.org/cdn/Research/distinguishing-ielts-proficiency-levels-progression-over-time-kang-et-al-june-2023.pdf)) **[T2]**.

> **Teaching implication.** Two trainable, measurable targets emerge that a
> grade-8 learner can work on with a phone recorder: **longer runs between
> pauses** (mean length of run) and **selective prominence** — stress the words
> that carry the new information, unstress the rest. Both connect to the U11–U12
> sentence-stress and intonation work. Note that *speech rate* is the strongest
> correlate but the worst teaching target: telling a learner to speak faster
> optimises the proxy, not the ability.

### 6.3 The test is a constrained speech-exchange system

The examiner is working from a script. Instructions to examiners: "The IELTS
Speaking Test involves the use of an examiner frame which is a script that
**must be followed**… Stick to the rubrics – do not deviate in any way… If asked
to repeat rubrics, do not rephrase in any way." Control is tightest in Parts 1
and 2; "In Part 3, the frame is less controlled so that the examiner's language
can be accommodated to the level of the candidate being examined"
(quoted in [Seedhouse & Egbert 2006](https://cdn.ielts.org/Research/interactional-organisation-of-ielts-speaking-test-seedhouse-et-al-2006.pdf)) **[T2]**.

Consequences supported by that report **[T2]**:

- If a Part 1 question is misunderstood, the examiner may repeat it but **cannot reformulate it**; the repertoire of interventions is repetition of the rubric (Parts 1–2), "Can you tell me anything more about that?" (Part 2), and re-wording or substituting a question (Part 3).
- "in interviews with high test scores, fewer examples of repair are observable" — a correlation the authors report, not a causal claim.
- Examiner deviations from the frame, when they occur, have "little significant impact on the language produced by candidates" ([O'Sullivan & Lu 2006](https://ielts.org/cdn/Research/impact-on-candidate-language-of-examiner-deviation-from-set-interlocutor-frame-osullivan-et-al-2006.pdf), 60 recordings) **[T2]**.

Seedhouse et al. (2014) draw the pedagogically sharpest conclusion: candidates
"need to provide an answer to the question **and** develop the topic inherent in
the question", yet "the ability to answer questions is **not specifically
included**" in the grading criteria — which is why they recommend adding it. They
propose a "discourse involvement hypothesis": candidates orient to the
moment-to-moment demands of the question–answer pair rather than to the rating
scales, which is why band descriptors and observed talk match so loosely **[T2]**.

The official examiner-training materials, via the same report, list the speech
functions the test structure reliably elicits: "providing personal information;
expressing a preference; providing non-personal information; comparing;
expressing opinions; summarising; explaining; conversation repair; suggesting;
contrasting; justifying opinions; narrating and paraphrasing; speculating;
analysing" (Taylor 2001a, quoted in Seedhouse & Egbert 2006) **[T2]**.

> **Teaching implication.** That function list is a ready-made syllabus of
> speaking functions, and roughly two-thirds of it (preference, comparison,
> opinion, explanation, suggestion, contrast, narration) is already inside
> grade-8 range. The abstract end — speculating, analysing, justifying — is the
> Part 3 frontier and the honest ceiling of a grade-8 course.

> **Teaching implication.** "Answer the question, *then* develop it" is a
> two-beat habit worth drilling from Part-1-style questions upward. It is what
> the discourse structure demands even though no criterion names it.

## 7. Gaps — what a second pass must establish

- **Fluency indicators.** The verbatim list is in §3; the interpretive questions (is *rate* one-sided? is *repetition* penalised only when functionless? is word-search pausing distinguished from content pausing at criterion level?) are unresolved **[X]**. The Alexandra comment in §5 is suggestive but is one rated performance, not a rule.

This matters directly to our phonology strand and should be re-run against the descriptors and the pronunciation-scale research reports.
- **Lexical Resource.** Whether paraphrase-with-hesitation is credited, and how precision trades against rarity, is unresolved **[X]**.
- **Part 2 preparation minute** — no verified evidence (§4).
- **Part 3 question taxonomy** — no verified evidence (§4).
- **Band 8–9 exemplars** — none located (§5).
- **Interactional competence** — not currently a criterion; Seedhouse et al. recommend adding "the ability to answer questions". Any rubric we build should record that this dimension is *unscored* rather than quietly scoring it.
- **Computer-delivered Speaking** — not examined in this pass at all.
