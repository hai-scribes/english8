# IELTS Writing — Task Types, Criteria, and Known Failure Modes

**What this is.** Phase 3a of the IELTS knowledge base (see
`../ielts-research-plan.md`): a source-verified description of what the IELTS
Writing test asks for, how it is officially assessed, and what the published
examiner commentary shows actually decides a band. It is a curriculum
reference, not a prep guide — every entry exists so that lessons, rubrics and
tools built in this repo can trace a claim to a document.

**Research date.** 2026-08-08. Descriptors cited are the version marked
*Updated May 2023*; sample tasks are the 2023 official set.

**Source policy.** Tier 1 (binding): ielts.org, British Council, IDP,
Cambridge. Tier 2 (evidence): IELTS Research Reports and applied-linguistics
literature — used for tendencies, never for rules. Tier 3 (prep-industry lore)
is never a source here; where it appears it is the *object* of a claim, not the
warrant for one.

**Verification legend.** This pass generated 119 candidate claims from 24
sources; 25 went through adversarial verification (16 confirmed, 9 not
sustained). Every assertion below carries its evidential status:

| Tag | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **[C]** | Confirmed — passed adversarial verification this pass; vote shown, e.g. `C 3-0` |
| **[Q]** | Tier-1 verbatim — quoted directly from a primary document in the corpus; the quote is the claim, with no interpretation layered on it |
| **[T2]** | Tier-2 evidence — a research finding about tendencies in candidate performance; not a rule of the test |
| **[X]** | Tested, not sustained — an interpretation adversarial verification refused. **Neither it nor its negation is stated as fact here**; it is logged so it is not silently re-derived later |

## 1. The scoring architecture

| Fact | Status | Source wording |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two tasks; each assessed independently; Task 2 weighs more | **[C]** 3-0 | "Both the Academic and General Training Writing Modules consist of two tasks, Task 1 and Task 2. Each task is assessed independently. The assessment of Task 2 carries more weight in marking than Task 1." ([Key Assessment Criteria](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-key-assessment-criteria.pdf)) |
| Task 1: ~20 min, minimum 150 words. Task 2: ~40 min, minimum 250 words | **[C]** 3-0 | "They need to write 150 words in about 20 minutes. … They need to write 250 words in about 40 minutes." ([Sample Tasks 2023](https://ielts.org/cdn/Sample-tests/ielts-academic-writing-sample-tasks-2023.pdf)); task rubrics print "Write at least 150 words" / "Write at least 250 words" |
| Criteria: Task 1 = Task Achievement + CC + LR + GRA; Task 2 = Task **Response** + CC + LR + GRA | **[C]** 3-0 | Both criteria lists set out verbatim in the [Key Assessment Criteria](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-key-assessment-criteria.pdf) |
| Scores may be whole or half bands | **[Q]** | "Scores may be reported as whole bands or half-bands." ([Sample Tasks 2023](https://ielts.org/cdn/Sample-tests/ielts-academic-writing-sample-tasks-2023.pdf)) |

The word-count minimum is not a free-floating rule: it is written *inside* the
Task Achievement / Task Response criterion itself — "This criterion assesses
how fully, appropriately, accurately and relevantly the response fulfils the
requirements set out in the task, **using a minimum of 150 words**" **[C]** 2-1.
That placement is what §3.4 below turns on.

> **Teaching implication.** The 20/40-minute split and the Task 2 weighting are
> the only official warrant for how much practice time a course should give each
> genre. Our syllabus's 80–100-word paragraph is a Task 2 *body paragraph*, not a
> whole task — label it that way rather than as "IELTS writing practice".

## 2. Task 1 Academic — what the task officially is

### 2.1 An information-transfer task, and the limits that follow

> "Task 1 Academic is a writing task which has a defined input and a largely
> predictable output. It is basically an information-transfer task, which
> relates narrowly to the factual content of a diagram, graph, table, chart,
> map or other visual input, **not to speculative explanations that lie outside
> the given data**."
> — [Key Assessment Criteria](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-key-assessment-criteria.pdf) **[C]** 3-0; the same sentence appears in the [Sample Tasks 2023](https://ielts.org/cdn/Sample-tests/ielts-academic-writing-sample-tasks-2023.pdf) **[C]** 3-0 and the [computer-delivered example responses](https://ielts.org/cdn/computer-delivered-sample-tests-academic-writing/ielts-academic-writing-example-responses-to-parts-1-and-2-with-band-scores-and-examiner-comments.pdf) **[C]** 2-1

This is the strongest single finding for Task 1 teaching. "Do not explain *why*
the trend happened" is usually taught as a style convention; it is in fact a
statement about the **scope of the task** in the official criteria document, and
therefore a Task Achievement matter. The same sentence also carries the official
genre inventory: *diagram, graph, table, chart, map, or other visual input*.

IDP, as test co-owner, enumerates the live visual genres more concretely: line
graphs, pie charts, bar graphs, tables of data, combinations of visuals,
processes (manmade or natural), and maps of either an outdoor space or a floor
space ([IDP](https://ielts.idp.com/prepare/article-ielts-academic-writing-task-1-how-to-analyse-graphs)) **[C]** 2-1.

> **Teaching implication.** Grade-8 learners describing a chart will reach
> instinctively for causes ("because people are busier now"). The corrective is
> not "that's bad style" but "that information is not in the picture" — a
> checkable, teachable test the learner can apply alone.

### 2.2 The rubric wording is invariant across visual genres

The prep-industry belief that process and map tasks carry a different
instruction — one that drops the comparison clause — **is not supported by the
official sample set**. The identical sentence appears verbatim on all three
sample Task 1 prompts, including the process diagram:

| Sample task | Visual | Instruction line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1A | Bar chart (men/women in further education) | "Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant." |
| 1B | Line graph (radio/TV audiences) | "Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant." |
| 1C | **Process diagram** (brick manufacture) | "Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant." |

Source: [Academic Writing Sample Tasks 2023](https://ielts.org/cdn/Sample-tests/ielts-academic-writing-sample-tasks-2023.pdf) **[C]** 2-1.

Two honest qualifications. First, the verification vote here was 2-1, not
unanimous; the recorded vote is all we have — the grounds of the dissent were
not captured, so treat the quotable, identical wording as the solid part and the
generalisation beyond these three tasks as the soft part. Second, wording
*variants* do exist in official teaching materials: British
Council's own Task 1 worksheet prints "make comparisons where **appropriate**"
on a practice hydraulic-lift diagram
([BC teaching resource](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/sites/default/files/academic_writing_task_1_grammatical_structures_for_describing_diagrams.pdf))
**[Q]**. That is a teaching resource, not a live task, but it means the precise
claim to carry forward is: *the comparison clause is present on process tasks in
the official sample set*, not *the rubric string is byte-identical everywhere*.

Precision about what was found: the research located **no official basis** for
the belief that process/map rubrics omit comparison, and positive evidence
against it for processes. That is not the same as proving no such variant exists
in any live paper — and note that **no map task appears in the official sample
set at all**, so maps are untested here.

> **Teaching implication.** If comparison is demanded even by a process task,
> then comparison language is a Task 1 universal, not a chart-only skill. Our
> Unit 9 instructions genre (before/during/after a flood) is sequence-only; a
> cheap upgrade is to add one comparative move ("the strongest of the three
> stages"), which trains the clause the rubric always asks for.

### 2.3 What Task Achievement actually assesses in Task 1

Verbatim from the [Key Assessment Criteria](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-key-assessment-criteria.pdf) **[Q]** — the TA criterion assesses
the ability to summarise the information provided in the diagram by:

- "selecting key features of the information."
- "providing sufficient detail to illustrate these features."
- "reporting the information, figures and trends accurately."
- "comparing or contrasting the information by adequately highlighting the identifiable trends, principal changes or differences in the data and other inputs (rather than mechanical description reporting detail)."
- "presenting the response in an appropriate format."

Note the parenthesis in the fourth bullet: **mechanical description is named as
the thing comparison is supposed to replace**. That single word — *mechanical* —
recurs in examiner comments as the reason a competent-looking script stalls (see
§5).

For completeness, General Training Task 1 (letters) is assessed on: explaining
the letter's purpose, fully addressing the three bullet points, extending those
three functions, using an appropriate letter format, and consistently using an
appropriate tone **[Q]**. This document targets Academic; GT is recorded here
only at criterion level.

### 2.4 The overview is band-gated — and stops being named at the top

In the public [Writing Band Descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf)
(Task 1, Task Achievement, Academic clauses):

| Band | Academic Task Achievement wording | Overview named? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 9 | "All the requirements of the task are fully and appropriately satisfied. There may be extremely rare lapses in content." | No |
| 8 | "Key features are skilfully selected, and clearly presented, highlighted and illustrated." | No |
| 7 | "It presents a **clear overview**, the data are appropriately categorised, and main trends or differences are identified." | Yes |
| 6 | "Key features which are selected are covered and adequately highlighted. **A relevant overview is attempted.** Information is appropriately selected and supported using figures/data." | Yes |
| 5 | "Key features which are selected are not adequately covered. The recounting of detail is mainly mechanical. There may be no data to support the description." | No |
| 4 | "Key features/bullet points which are presented may be irrelevant, repetitive, inaccurate or inappropriate." | No |

Bands 6 and 7 **[C]** 3-0; bands 8 and 9 **[C]** 3-0; bands 4–5 **[Q]**.

Two consequences, both counter to how the overview is usually taught:

1. **Band 6 needs only an *attempt*; band 7 needs a *clear* overview with
   categorised data.** The overview is the hinge between 6 and 7 in Task
   Achievement, not a pass/fail gate at every level.
2. **Above band 7 the descriptor stops naming it.** At 8 and 9 the discriminator
   becomes *selection and illustration quality* — "skilfully selected, and
   clearly presented, highlighted and illustrated". Teaching "write an overview"
   as the route to band 8 mistakes a band-7 requirement for a band-9 one.

A corpus observation worth recording, carefully: the word "overview" does not
appear anywhere in the [Key Assessment Criteria](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-key-assessment-criteria.pdf)
document, and appears in the public band descriptors only at bands 6 and 7
(direct inspection of both documents) **[Q]**. The tempting inference — *therefore
a separate overview paragraph is not really required* — **was tested this pass and
not sustained** **[X]**, and the examiner evidence in §2.5 points the other way.
Record the distribution; do not draw the conclusion.

### 2.5 Examiners do enforce the overview — and at the top, enforce its *quality*

Three official examiner comments, one claim **[C]** 3-0
([Sample Tasks 2023](https://ielts.org/cdn/Sample-tests/ielts-academic-writing-sample-tasks-2023.pdf)):

| Script | Band | Examiner wording |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1B Script A (line graph) | 6 | "In order to gain a higher mark for content, the candidate would be expected to summarise the most important features of the graph in an overview, for example, 'audiences for radio peak in the morning, and for TV, in the evening'." |
| 1C Script A (process) | 5 | "this script fails to present an overview and some of the key features are not adequately covered." |
| 1C Script C (process) | 8.5 | "There is an overview in the first paragraph indicating that there are 'seven consecutive steps' however for the highest score, a **fuller** overview would be needed, to summarise those key stages, for example; extracting the clay, then shaping, drying and delivering the bricks." |

The 8.5 comment is the most instructive line in the whole corpus: an overview
was present, and it still capped the score, because naming that a process has
seven steps is not *summarising* the seven steps. At the top of the scale the
overview must carry content, not announce structure.

A related interpretation — that examiners accept an overview placed in the
*final* paragraph, contradicting the "overview must be paragraph 2" rule — was
tested and **not sustained** (vote 1-2) **[X]**. What is quotable is the Band 6
comment on the computer-delivered sample: "There is a relevant overview in the
final paragraph, with a summary of the main changes"
([example responses](https://ielts.org/cdn/computer-delivered-sample-tests-academic-writing/ielts-academic-writing-example-responses-to-parts-1-and-2-with-band-scores-and-examiner-comments.pdf))
**[Q]**. Take the quote; do not build a placement rule on it in either direction.

> **Teaching implication.** "Overview" is a summarising skill, not a paragraph
> slot. The transferable grade-8 drill is: *given five facts, write the one
> sentence that a reader who reads nothing else should get* — which is also the
> topic-sentence skill our paragraph genres already need.

### 2.6 Language systems for Task 1 diagrams

The only Tier-1 language-system resource captured this pass is British
Council's Task 1 teaching pack for **diagrams/processes**, which is explicitly
pitched at **level B1+** — i.e. just above where grade 8 sits
([BC](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/sites/default/files/academic_writing_task_1_grammatical_structures_for_describing_diagrams.pdf))
**[Q]**. It targets three systems:

| System | Forms taught |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose | `to` + infinitive; `so that` + full clause; `in order to` + infinitive; `so as to` + infinitive |
| Cause and result | *because, because of, due to, on account of, results in, causes, gives rise to, as a result, consequently* |
| Extra information | Defining vs non-defining relative clauses (`which`/`that`/`where`, comma rules, no `that` in non-defining) |

Its own model text is dense with agentless passives ("an electric signal **is
sent** via electrical wires to a pump"), which is the expected register for
process description.

> **GAP** — not established in this research pass: an official, Tier-1
> inventory of **trend lexis for data charts** (rise/fall/plateau verbs and
> their adverbs, degree modification, approximation language). The BC resource
> covers processes only. Do not fill this from memory; a later pass should
> target the remaining British Council Academic Writing Task 1 teaching packs.

> **GAP** — not established in this research pass: any official **map task**
> sample with examiner commentary. Maps are confirmed as a live genre (IDP,
> §2.1) but no scored exemplar was located.

> **Teaching implication.** Purpose clauses, cause/result connectives and
> relative clauses are all inside or adjacent to the grade-8 grammar map
> (U7 complex sentences with adverbial clauses). A process-description task is
> therefore the *cheapest* Task 1 genre to introduce early — it needs no trend
> lexis at all.

## 3. Task 2 — what the task officially is

### 3.1 The criterion definition

> "For Task 2 of both AC and GT Writing tests, candidates are required to
> **formulate and develop a position** in relation to a given prompt in the form
> of a question or statement, using a minimum of 250 words. Ideas should be
> supported by evidence, and **examples may be drawn from a candidate's own
> experience**."
> — [Key Assessment Criteria](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-key-assessment-criteria.pdf) **[C]** 3-0

The Task Response criterion assesses (verbatim) **[Q]**:

- "how fully the candidate responds to the task."
- "how adequately the main ideas are extended and supported."
- "how relevant the candidate's ideas are to the task."
- "how clearly the candidate opens the discourse, establishes their position and formulates conclusions."
- "how appropriate the format of the response is to the task."

Two things follow that are usually treated as advice rather than rubric.
**Position clarity and consistency are named criterion elements** — "establishes
their position" — not stylistic preferences. And **personal-experience examples
are explicitly licensed**; the widespread instruction to avoid them has no basis
in this document.

> **Teaching implication.** Our syllabus already runs balanced-argument-then-
> position three times (U6, U8, U11). The rubric phrase "opens the discourse,
> establishes their position and formulates conclusions" is a three-move schema
> a grade-8 learner can hold: *open → stand → close*. Teach it once as one named
> schema and reuse it, rather than as three separate unit-local writing tasks.

### 3.2 The two official Task 2 prompts in the corpus

| Sample | Prompt type as printed | Instruction line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2A | "To what extent do you agree or disagree with this opinion?" | "Give reasons for your answer and include any relevant examples from your own knowledge or experience. Write at least 250 words." |
| 2B | "Do the disadvantages of international tourism outweigh the advantages?" | (same) |

([Sample Tasks 2023](https://ielts.org/cdn/Sample-tests/ielts-academic-writing-sample-tasks-2023.pdf)) **[Q]**

> **GAP** — not established in this research pass: the standard five-way
> archetype taxonomy (opinion / discussion / advantage–disadvantage /
> problem–solution / double question). It is prep-industry taxonomy; no Tier-1
> source enumerating Task 2 question archetypes was located or verified this
> pass. Two archetypes are attested by the official prompts above. Do not
> present the five-way list as official until a Tier-1 source is found.

### 3.3 The Task Response ladder

The band-by-band Task Response wording belongs to
`02-band-descriptors.md`; a proposed delta claim about position quality across
bands 7–9 was tested this pass and **not sustained** **[X]**, so no ladder
summary is asserted here. What is quotable from the
[public descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf)
**[Q]**, as raw anchors only:

- Band 9 — "A clear and fully developed position is presented which directly answers the question/s." / "Ideas are relevant, fully extended and well supported."
- Band 8 — "A clear and well-developed position is presented in response to the question/s."
- Band 7 — "A clear and developed position is presented." / "Main ideas are extended and supported but there may be a tendency to over-generalise or there may be a lack of focus and precision in supporting ideas/material."
- Band 6 — "A position is presented that is directly relevant to the prompt, although the conclusions drawn may be unclear, unjustified or repetitive."
- Band 5 — "The writer expresses a position, but the development is not always clear."
- Band 4 — "A position is discernible, but the reader has to read carefully to find it."

### 3.4 Underlength is not a fixed numeric penalty

This is the second headline finding, and it contradicts the most-repeated
number in the prep industry.

**What the official criteria document names as penalties — the complete list:**

> "Candidates should note that scripts may be penalised if they are a) partly or
> wholly plagiarised, b) not written as full, connected text (e.g. using bullet
> points in any part of the response, or note form, is not appropriate, etc.)."
> — [Key Assessment Criteria](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-key-assessment-criteria.pdf) **[C]** 3-0

Two penalties: plagiarism, and not writing connected prose. No numeric
underlength deduction, and no off-topic penalty rule, appears in that document.

**What an examiner did with an underlength script:**

> "A clear position is presented from the outset, supported by relevant ideas.
> These would require further development to achieve a higher score. It is also
> useful to note, this response does not meet the minimum word count for Task 2
> of 250 words, so **there is room for further development**."
> — Band **5.5** ([computer-delivered example responses](https://ielts.org/cdn/computer-delivered-sample-tests-academic-writing/ielts-academic-writing-example-responses-to-parts-1-and-2-with-band-scores-and-examiner-comments.pdf)) **[C]** 2-1

An under-250-word response still scored 5.5, and the shortfall is framed as
*forgone development opportunity*, not as a deduction applied.

**Where length does appear in the descriptors** — precisely, at Band 3 only, and
in two criteria, not one ([public descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf)) **[Q]**:

- Lexical Resource, Band 3 (both tasks): "The resource is inadequate (which may be due to the response being significantly underlength)."
- Grammatical Range & Accuracy, Band 3 (both tasks): "Length may be insufficient to provide evidence of control of sentence forms."
- Band 1, all four criteria: "Responses of 20 words or fewer are rated at Band 1."

The mechanism the documents describe is therefore **indirect**: too short means
too little evidence for the rater, and — via the TA/TR definitions in §1 — an
incompletely fulfilled task. A broader formulation of this ("length affects the
score *only* indirectly, and solely via Lexical Resource") was tested and **not
sustained** **[X]** — correctly, since GRA names it too.

Tier-2 corroboration that length matters without being a penalty: in a study of
41 L1-Korean test takers, "essay length and lexical sophistication contributed
significantly to the writing proficiency scores (R² = 35–46%)"
([Kang, Egbert & Miao 2023](https://ielts.org/cdn/Research/distinguishing-ielts-proficiency-levels-progression-over-time-kang-et-al-june-2023.pdf))
**[T2]**. A correlation in a 41-person sample is not a scoring rule; it is
consistent with the "forgone development" reading.

Precision about what was found: the research located **no official basis** for a
fixed underlength deduction. That is not the same as establishing that examiners
never apply one.

> **Teaching implication.** For a grade-8 course this reframes word count
> entirely: length is not a hoop, it is *how much evidence of your English the
> reader gets to see*. That framing works at 80–100 words as well as at 250, and
> it is honest.

### 3.5 Copied rubric earns nothing

> "The first five lines of this response are directly copied rubric; **no credit
> is given for copied rubric**." — Band 5.5 comment
> ([Sample Tasks 2023](https://ielts.org/cdn/Sample-tests/ielts-academic-writing-sample-tasks-2023.pdf)) **[Q]**

> "(It is a shame that the first paragraph, and beginning of the second are
> mainly copied from the rubric.) … The repetition of language from the rubric,
> while integrated, **reveals a lack of ability to paraphrase**." — Band 6.5 **[Q]**

The descriptors back this at the bottom of the scale: Band 1 Task Response —
"Any copied rubric must be discounted." **[Q]**

> **Teaching implication.** Paraphrasing the prompt is not decoration; copied
> text is deducted from the count of what the learner produced. A one-line
> starter routine ("say the question in your own words before you answer it")
> trains a directly scored behaviour.

## 4. Coherence & Cohesion, Lexical Resource, GRA — the shared criteria

Verbatim criterion definitions, both tasks
([Key Assessment Criteria](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-key-assessment-criteria.pdf)) **[Q]**:

| Criterion | Definition | Assessed sub-points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Coherence & Cohesion** | "the overall organisation and logical development of the message… Coherence refers to the linking of ideas through logical sequencing, while cohesion refers to the varied and appropriate use of cohesive devices" | logical organisation/progression; paragraphing for topic organisation; logical sequencing within and across paragraphs; flexible reference and substitution; discourse markers to mark stages, e.g. "[First of all \| In conclusion]", and to signal relations, e.g. "[as a result \| similarly]" |
| **Lexical Resource** | "the range of vocabulary the candidate has used and the accuracy and appropriacy of that use in terms of the specific task" | range of general words (synonyms to avoid repetition); adequacy/appropriacy (topic-specific items, indicators of writer's attitude); **precision** of word choice; control of collocation, idiom, sophisticated phrasing; density and communicative effect of **spelling** errors; same for **word-formation** errors |
| **Grammatical Range & Accuracy** | "the range and accurate use of the candidate's grammatical resource via the candidate's writing at sentence level" | range and appropriacy of simple/compound/complex sentences; accuracy of each; density and communicative effect of grammatical errors; accurate and appropriate **punctuation** |

Three things a curriculum should notice:

1. **Spelling and word formation are inside Lexical Resource**, and are judged by
   *density and communicative effect*, not by count. The Band 5.5 script's rating
   was held down explicitly by this: "frequent spelling errors that can cause some
   difficulties for the reader, thus keeping the rating down for the lexical
   criterion" **[Q]**.
2. **Punctuation is inside GRA**, not a separate tidiness concern. Band 5 Task 1
   comment: "there are some punctuation errors that cause confusion" **[Q]**.
3. **Paragraphing is inside Coherence & Cohesion** and is named repeatedly in
   examiner comments as the ceiling on otherwise strong scripts.

On discourse markers, the criteria explicitly *want* them ("appropriate use of
discourse markers to clearly mark the stages in a response") while examiner
comments repeatedly flag **overuse**: "connectives are overused or inappropriate"
(Band 6), "cohesive devices are overused" (Band 6), "some mechanical use of
linkers in places" (Band 5.5), "some overuse of sequencers in paragraph 2
[Firstly | So | Also | Yet]" (Band 7.5) **[Q]**. A stronger interpretation of the
7.5 comment — that at bands 8–9 dense signposting is treated as a *defect* while
hedging is credited as a "higher-level feature" — was tested and **not
sustained** **[X]**. The quotes stand; the generalisation does not.

> **Teaching implication.** "Use linking words" is the single most
> over-transferred piece of school-writing advice, and the examiner corpus shows
> it turning into a ceiling. The safer instruction for our paragraph genres is
> *one marker per stage, none inside a stage*.

Patterns visible across the set, stated as observations about this corpus rather
than as rules of the test:

- The 7→8.5 boundary in Task 1 is about **overview quality and selection**, not
  about more vocabulary.
- **Format failure caps Task Achievement independently of language quality** —
  the process Script B is described as having "a high level of language
  proficiency" and still lands at 7 because of a letter-style opening.
- At every band from 4 to 6.5 in Task 2, **development, progression or
  paragraphing** is among the named limiters — usually alongside a language
  criterion (vocabulary control at 5.5, grammatical control at 6.5), never
  instead of organisation.
- The highest officially commented Academic script in the corpus is **8.5**.

> **GAP** — not established in this research pass: any official examiner-
> commented **band-9** Writing script. Everything said about band 9 in this file
> derives from descriptor wording, not from an exemplar.
