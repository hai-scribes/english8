## 1. The one thing to read if you read nothing else

The plan's working assumption was that band-9 Lexical Resource means *precision
and collocation, not rare words*. **That question was open when this file was
written and was resolved on 2026-08-08 (§3): it was a false opposition.** Rarity
of a *single word* and rarity of a *word combination* are different constructs.
Word-level rarity is real, causal and small, and stops discriminating near the
top. Combination-level rarity — association strength, MI — is strong, monotonic,
and is the only complexity family still separating levels at the ceiling. A
formulaic sequence *is* a rare item, measured on combinations. §8.1's ranking
function is therefore **no longer blocked**, and §3 also corrects **two errors of
fact** this file previously carried.

A second targeted pass on the same date tested whether **syllable-final consonant
work** should enter the syllabus, on the user's condition that it be *proved
helpful for reaching the top of the scale*. **It was not proved** (§5.5.7a–c). No
study relates coda omission to any score, and the trainability evidence points
the wrong way. The difficulty finding stands; the payoff claim never existed.

Everything else in this document rests on firmer ground.

## 2. Vocabulary: how much, and of what

### 2.1 Coverage thresholds — the size targets

Nation's BNC-list coverage analysis is the spine of every vocabulary decision in
this project. It is Tier 2, peer-reviewed, and confirmed 3-0.

> "If 98% coverage of a text is needed for unassisted comprehension, then a
> 8,000 to 9,000 word-family vocabulary is needed for comprehension of written
> text and a vocabulary of 6,000 to 7,000 for spoken text."
> ([Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)) **[V 3-0]**

The 98% figure is not a convention — it is derived:

> "With a text coverage of 95% (1 unknown word in 20), a few more gained
> adequate comprehension, but they were still a small minority. At 100%
> coverage, most gained adequate comprehension. When a regression model was
> applied to the data, a reasonable fit was found. It was calculated that 98%
> text coverage (1 unknown word in 50) would be needed for most learners to gain
> adequate comprehension."
> ([Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf), reporting Hu & Nation 2000) **[V 3-0]**

The practical consequence is the **coverage curve flattens brutally**:

> "The most common 2,000 words in the BNC account for about 83% of the running
> words. The most frequent 4,000 words from the BNC plus proper nouns account
> for around 95% of the running words, and to get to the 98% coverage level a
> vocabulary of at least 8,000 words plus proper nouns is needed."
> ([Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)) **[V 3-0]**

| Vocabulary size (BNC word families) | Written-text coverage | Reading experience |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2,000 | ~83% | ~1 unknown word in 6 — unreadable |
| 4,000 + proper nouns | ~95% | ~1 unknown word in 20 — comprehension for a small minority |
| 8,000+ + proper nouns | 98% | ~1 unknown word in 50 — the unassisted-comprehension threshold |

Spoken text is cheaper. Nation's two spoken samples (Wellington talkback/
interview and friendly conversation) give: **[S]**

| Word list | Talk-back, interview | Conversation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2,000 | 89.41% | 89.35% |
| 3,000 + proper nouns | 96.52% | 96.03% |
| 6,000 + proper nouns | 98.26% | 97.67% |
| 7,000 + proper nouns | 98.62% | 97.95% |

> "Clearly, spoken language makes slightly greater use of the high-frequency
> words of the language than written language does. Against this we need to
> consider that greater text coverage than 98% may be needed to cope effectively
> with the transitory nature of spoken language."
> ([Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)) **[S]**

For IELTS Listening specifically:

> "The results showed that, 3,000 most frequent word families in the British
> National Corpus/Corpus of Contemporary American English (BNC/COCA) word list
> were necessary to safely gain 95% coverage of all four sections in the test."
> ([Lexical coverage of the IELTS Listening test, *SAGE Open* 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/21582440221079934)) **[V 2-1]**

> **GAP** — not established in this research pass: what vocabulary size reaches
> **98%** coverage of IELTS Listening. The panel put a 5,000-family claim to a
> vote and it was not sustained (0-3), and the source page was behind a
> bot-check so no verbatim text could be retrieved. Do not quote a 98% Listening
> figure. Nation's general spoken figure (6,000–7,000) is the nearest defensible
> proxy and should be labelled as such.

### 2.2 The topic-lexis discount — and why it matters for a 12-unit course

Nation quantifies something this project can exploit directly. Recurring
topic words behave like known words after their first few occurrences:

> "Thus by considering these highly repeated words as known, only 5,000 words
> would be needed to reach 98% coverage, a saving of a 1,000 word-level."
> ([Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf), on the *Shrek* corpus) **[S]**

A course organised into themed units — leisure, countryside, ethnic groups,
customs, environment, shopping, disasters, communication, technology, space — is
already doing this. Front-loading a unit's topic set is not decoration; it is
worth roughly a thousand word families of coverage on that unit's texts. This is
one of the cleanest arguments in the whole knowledge base for **per-unit lexis
sets taught before the reading**, and it is a Tier-2 quantified finding, not
folklore.

### 2.3 Which lists, in which order

**Oxford 3000 / 5000 — the usable CEFR-graded ladder.**

> "The Oxford 3000 is a list of the 3,000 core words that every learner of
> English needs to know. The words have been chosen based on their frequency in
> the Oxford English Corpus and relevance to learners of English. Every word is
> aligned to the CEFR, guiding learners on the words they should know at A1-B2
> level."
> ([Oxford Learner's Dictionaries](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/about/wordlists/oxford3000-5000)) **[V 3-0]**

> "As well as the Oxford 3000 core word list, it includes an additional 2,000
> words that are aligned to the CEFR, guiding advanced learners at B2-C1 level
> on the most useful high-level words to learn to expand their vocabulary."
> ([Oxford Learner's Dictionaries](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/about/wordlists/oxford3000-5000)) **[V 3-0]**

That is a two-stage ladder with a **per-item CEFR tag** — the only list in this
research pass that gives an A2→B1→B2→C1 sequence at word granularity. For a
grade-8 learner it is the correct backbone.

How the lists were built (panel did not sustain this, but the page says it
verbatim):

> "The words in the Oxford 3000 and 5000 have been selected based on two
> criteria: the frequency of the words in the Oxford English Corpus, a database
> of over 2 billion words from different subject areas and contexts which covers
> British, American and world English. [and] the relevance of the words to
> English language learners, measured by their frequency in a specially created
> corpus of Secondary and Adult English courses published by Oxford University
> Press."
> ([Oxford Learner's Dictionaries](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/about/wordlists/oxford3000-5000)) **[S/NS]**

This matters: Oxford 3000/5000 are **frequency-plus-pedagogic-relevance** lists,
not pure frequency rankings like the BNC/COCA families Nation counts. They are
not interchangeable with word-family coverage lists and should never be summed
with them.

**The AWL is not a separate stage.** This is the single most actionable
sequencing finding of the phase:

> "Table 1 indicates that 451 of the 570 AWL word families (79%) are represented
> in the top 4,000 lemmas of COCA, with 236 (Bands 1 and 2) of the 570 (41%)
> actually being in the top 2,000 lemmas of COCA. … provide strong evidence that
> (i) the AWL is largely a subset of the high-frequency words of English and
> should therefore not be thought of as an appendage to the GSL, and (ii) the
> GSL, as a whole, is no longer an accurate reflection of high-frequency
> English."
> ([Gardner & Davies](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_52.pdf)) **[V 3-0]**

**Do not build a "finish the general list, then start the AWL" pipeline.** Four
in five AWL families are already inside the first 4,000 general lemmas; two in
five are inside the first 2,000. Academic vocabulary is a *label on words the
learner is meeting anyway*, not a later tier. In a trainer, AWL membership
should be an **attribute** of an item, not a stage gate.

**AWL vs AVL.** Gardner & Davies report their Academic Vocabulary List roughly
doubles the AWL's academic coverage at equal list size (panel did not sustain
this; the figures are verbatim in the paper): AVL 570 families cover **13.8%** of
COCA academic and **13.7%** of BNC academic, against AWL 570 at **7.2%** and
**6.9%**. The BNC replication matters because the BNC was not used to build the
AVL. The full AVL target is stated as "expanded AVL (3,000 lemmas, 2,000 word
families)". **[S/NS]**

Against that, a published commentary argues the comparison is not clean —
different counting units (lemma vs word family), different corpora, different
exclusion methods — and concludes "the AVL is far from complete and cannot
replace the AWL", with the broader verdict that "both the AWL and the AVL seem
to be far from complete."
([Gholaminejad & Anani Sarab, *TEFLIN Journal* 32(1)](http://dx.doi.org/10.15639/teflinjournal.v32i1/167-182)) **[S]**

**Recommendation for this project:** treat AWL/AVL membership as a *tag*, not a
syllabus. The lists disagree about which words are academic; they agree that
academic words are mostly high-frequency words. Sequence by CEFR level (Oxford)
and by unit topic; annotate with academic-list membership for later filtering.

### 2.4 Vocabulary size and CEFR level

The panel did not sustain the Milton claims (the EuroSLA monograph PDF appears
to have been unreachable during verification), but the source is held in the
cache and states them verbatim. Reported here with the author's own caveats,
which are substantial.

Meara & Milton's XLex data, from learners taking and passing Cambridge exams at
each CEFR level — **the unit is lemmas sampled from the most frequent 5,000, not
word families**: **[S/NS]**

| CEFR | Cambridge exam | XLex score (5,000 max) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | Starters, Movers, Flyers | <1,500 |
| A2 | Kernel English Test | 1,500–2,500 |
| B1 | Preliminary English Test | 2,750–3,250 |
| B2 | First Certificate in English | 3,250–3,750 |
| C1 | Cambridge Advanced English | 3,750–4,500 |
| C2 | Cambridge Proficiency in English | 4,500–5,000 |

Milton's own reading of it:

> "Something like 2000 words, of the most frequent 5000, might be needed to
> attain A2 level in all the languages tests … It appears that approximately
> 3000 words, of the most frequent 5000 words, might be needed to progress
> beyond the elementary A1 and A2 levels and achieve any kind of independence …
> The advanced levels of performance on the CEFR, C1 and C2 level, are
> associated as Nation (2006) suggested with almost complete recognition of the
> most frequent 5000 words."
> ([Milton, *EuroSLA Monographs* 1](http://www.eurosla.org/monographs/EM01/211-232Milton.pdf)) **[S/NS]**

**Caveats the source itself supplies, and which must travel with the table:**
"These figures are broad generalisations"; the equivalent figures differ by
target language (French learners show smaller vocabularies at each level, Greek
larger); the multi-language study drew on "comparatively small samples"; and
60–70% of variance in CEFR level is explained by vocabulary size, meaning
30–40% is not. Milton is explicit that "vocabulary size and communicative
performance are separable qualities."

Milton et al. (2010), N = 30, Spearman correlations between vocabulary size and
IELTS sub-scores: **[S/NS]**

| | Reading | Listening | Writing | Speaking | Overall |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| XLex (orthographic) | .70\*\* | .48\*\* | .76\*\* | .35 | .68\*\* |
| ALex (phonological) | .22 | .67\*\* | .44\* | .71\*\* | .55\*\* |

\*\* p < .01, \* p < .05. Regression: ~58% of variance in overall IELTS,
~60% in Writing, 48% in Reading explained by orthographic vocabulary size; 61%
of variance in whether a learner scores Speaking ≥ 5 explained by phonological
vocabulary size.

**N = 30 is small.** But the *pattern* — written vocabulary predicts reading and
writing, heard vocabulary predicts speaking and listening, and the two correlate
only .46 with each other — is a design constraint worth respecting: a
vocabulary trainer that only ever shows words in print trains half the construct.

## 3. Rarity or collocation? — resolved by separating the two measures

*Rewritten 2026-08-08 by a second, targeted gap-closure pass. This section
previously recorded the question as **unresolved** and carried **two errors of
fact plus one minor one**; all three are corrected in place and flagged where
they occur. Everything added by that pass is **[S]** — retrieved and quoted from
the primary document, **not** panel-verified. Pre-existing **[V]** and **[S/NS]**
markers are unchanged and are not upgraded.*

### 3.0 The resolution — word rarity and combination rarity are different constructs

**The two "sides" were measuring different things.** Rarity of a *single word*
and rarity of a *word combination* are separate constructs, and once they are
separated the evidence stops disagreeing. **[S]**

| | **Word-level rarity** (frequency band of a lemma) | **Combination-level rarity** (association strength, MI) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Relation to proficiency | Weak, **non-monotonic**, unreliable as an index | **Strong and monotonic** |
| At the top of the scale | Stops discriminating | **Effects are largest at the top** |
| Causal evidence | **Yes** — inserting rarer lemmas raises expert ratings | **None.** Correlational and rater-perception only |
| Verdict | Real but small; **not** a ranking key | The only complexity family still discriminating at the ceiling |

A high-MI collocation is typically **moderately frequent words in a rare,
exclusive pairing** — *immortal souls*, not *hard work*. That is why "rare words"
and "formulaic language" looked like rival answers: **the formulaic answer *is* a
rarity answer, measured on combinations instead of on words.** Granger & Bestgen
put the learner-side signature of it plainly: intermediate learners

> "overuse high frequency collocations (such as *hard work*) and underuse
> lower-frequency, but strongly associated, collocations (such as *immortal
> souls*)"
> ([Granger & Bestgen 2014, *IRAL* 52(3)](https://doi.org/10.1515/iral-2014-0011)) **[S]**

The consequence for tooling is in §8.1, which is **no longer blocked**: rank by
the association strength of the collocations an item participates in, not by the
item's own frequency band.

### 3.1 Side A — word-level rarity causally raises ratings, by a small amount

Naismith & Juffs (2025), *Studies in Second Language Acquisition* 47, 336–360.
Experimental: 30 versions of 3 argumentative essays, all normalised to 250 words,
single prompt, rated by **47 current or former IELTS examiners**, with rater
severity controlled via many-facet Rasch measurement before regression.

> **Correction, 2026-08-08.** This section previously described the three base
> texts as "CEFR B1, B2, C1". They are **real published IELTS Task 2 scripts
> rated band 4, 6.5 and 8**, which the authors then map onto B1/B2/C1. The
> distinction matters: the design **does** include a genuine band-8 script, so
> the study is not confined to mid-band material. **[S]**

> "The findings revealed that the use of lower-frequency words significantly and
> positively impacted the experts' ratings."
> ([Naismith & Juffs 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/impact-of-collocational-proficiency-features-on-expert-ratings-of-l2-english-learners-writing/66E56E465A7FDE3C1D506B3AF5F76253)) **[V 3-0]**

> "When used as part of collocations, such words then provided a small yet
> significant additional boost to ratings." **[V 3-0]**

This is **causal**, not correlational: frequency was manipulated, not observed.
That is the strongest single piece of evidence in the phase.

But read the magnitudes. In the regression on Lexical Resource fair scores: **[S]**

| Predictor | Estimate | p |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Task Response fair score | .883 | < .001 |
| Grammatical Range & Accuracy fair score | .879 | < .001 |
| Frequency [low] | .032 | < .001 |
| Item type [collocation vs not] | –.022 *(sum contrast — see correction below)* | < .001 |
| **Collocational accuracy [low–high]** | **–.005** | **.293 (n.s.)** |

Tukey contrasts on frequency: low vs high .050 (p < .001), mid vs high .032
(p = .018), low vs mid .018 (n.s.). So the word-level rarity effect is **real,
causal, and about one-twentieth the size** of the other analytic criteria's
association with the Lexical Resource score in the same model.

> **⚠ Error corrected, 2026-08-08 — this file previously recorded the opposite of
> the finding.** The `–.022` above is a **sum-contrast coefficient, not a
> between-condition contrast**, and this section formerly read it as showing that
> collocational packaging *lowered* ratings. The paper's own post-hoc says the
> reverse: "Tukey's … col vs. non-col: Estimate **.036**, SE .006, t ratio 5.744,
> **p = .018**", and "This effect was small but significant, resulting in
> **higher** LR ratings when the lower-frequency lemmas were part of a
> collocation." The authors' abstract concludes: "low-frequency words within
> collocations are particularly salient to raters and deserving of pedagogic
> focus."
> ([Naismith & Juffs 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/impact-of-collocational-proficiency-features-on-expert-ratings-of-l2-english-learners-writing/66E56E465A7FDE3C1D506B3AF5F76253)) **[S]**
> Anything downstream that cited a negative collocation effect from this file was
> citing a sign error. Nothing in this knowledge base now asserts one.

Naismith & Juffs also tested whether the rarity effect **attenuates** near the
top of the range. It does not: the CEFR × frequency interactions were
non-significant (B1–C1 × freq **−.014, p = .162**; B2–C1 × freq **−.010,
p = .240**). **[S]**

### 3.3 The combination-level evidence — what actually resolves it

Two studies carry this, and both are **[S]**.

**Saito (2020), *Language Learning***
([PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10083748/1/LL2020.pdf)). **N = 85** Japanese
speakers, **10 raters**, spontaneous **speech**. Decisive because both measures
were computed on the *same* speakers, so the comparison is not confounded by
sample: **[S]**

| Measure | Correlation with rated proficiency (two rating sets) |
| --- | --- |
| **Bigram MI** (association strength) | **.734 / .755** |
| Trigram MI | .676 / .641 |
| **Bigram t-score** (raw co-occurrence frequency) | **−.333 / −.322** — *frequent* collocations predict **worse** ratings |
| **Single-word frequency** | **n.s. — the only non-significant variable in the study** |

Regression: **Advanced Collocation Use accounted for 35.3% / 42.8%** of rating
variance; **One-Word Use for 4.8% / 5.9%**. With text length partialled out,
collocation still held **17.6% / 25.5%**. **[S]**

**Paquot (2019), *Second Language Research* 35(1)**
([record](https://research.dial.uclouvain.be/handle/2078.5/178318)). **98 texts:
B2 = 25, C1 = 62, C2 = 11** — French-L1 academic **writing**. MI computed over
Stanford typed dependencies, so "collocation" is defined syntactically rather
than by adjacency: **[S]**

| Dependency | η² | Mean MI B2 → C1 → C2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| amod (adjective–noun) | .106 | — |
| advmod (adverb–verb) | .118 | — |
| **dobj (verb–object)** | **.154**; **C2–C1 = 0.40, p = .005** | **1.79 → 1.97 → 2.38** |

And the negative half, which is the part that matters most: **[S]**

> "no measure of lexical complexity distinguishes with statistical significance
> between proficiency levels"

Neither did syntactic complexity. Phraseological **diversity** was **n.s. and
declining**, and **membership of an academic collocation list was n.s.** So in a
study where *nothing else* separated B2/C1/C2, **association strength did** — and
its largest step is **C1 → C2**.

**Corroboration, both [S]:**

- **Kim, Crossley & Kyle (2018), *Modern Language Journal* 102(1):** bigram and
  trigram features accounted for **16.1%** and **31.0%** of proficiency variance;
  content-word frequency added **+8.5% incremental** — i.e. word-level frequency
  is *not* zero, it is *second*.
- **Crossley, Salsbury & McNamara (2015):** collocation accuracy explained
  **84%** of variance in writing and **89%** in speaking. **Read with the caveat
  attached: predictor and outcome are both human ratings, so an unknown share of
  those figures is rater consistency, not language.** **[S]**

| **Measure** (word vs combination) | **This is the moderator.** §3.0 |
| **Modality** (speaking vs writing) | **Not a moderator. Retired 2026-08-08.** This file previously ran a "Modality" row splitting the two sides; it does not survive. The strongest MI finding (Saito, r = .734/.755) is **speaking**; Crossley et al. found collocation accuracy explained *more* variance in speaking (89%) than in writing (84%); Paquot found the construct valid in writing. **[S]** |
| **Band range** | **Partial moderator — and this file's previous conclusion was backwards for combinations.** "Rarity buys movement through the middle bands and stops paying at the ceiling" is right for **word-level** rarity and **wrong for combination-level**: Paquot's MI effects are *largest at the top* (C2–C1 dobj = 0.40, p = .005), and Naismith & Juffs found no CEFR × frequency attenuation. This corroborates `02` §3.4: **range** is already "wide" at band 8 and so cannot separate 8 from 9; **control** does — and MI is a control measure. **[S]** |
| **Accuracy** | **Not resolved.** See §3.5. |
| **Design** (experimental vs observational) | Still real, and still asymmetric in the unhelpful direction: the *word-level* finding is causal; **every combination-level finding is correlational.** See the first GAP in §3.6. |

### 3.5 Collocational accuracy — still open, and now a three-way split

Naismith & Juffs tested the accuracy question directly and found nothing:
frequency × accuracy **.009, p = .150 (n.s.)**; main effect of accuracy
**−.005, p = .293 (n.s.)**. The panel put the accuracy claim to a vote and it
errored (0-2), so it is unconfirmed either way. The authors attribute both nulls
to their design:

> "These rater data further support the hypothesis that the lack of significance
> for collocational accuracy in this study can likely be attributed to
> experimental design." **[S]**

Specifically: only **six collocation errors per 250-word text**, and uniformly
light error gravity — "the meaning remains clear in all the collocation errors,
for example, *positive school*". **[S]** They excluded error gravity as a factor
and used one prompt and three base texts. Their raters' *comments* told the
opposite story from their statistics: collocation errors were "frequently noted",
and writers at all three levels were described as "risk takers", i.e. higher
sophistication with lower accuracy.

**A comparable manipulation found the opposite.** Fritz & Ruegg (2013),
*Assessing Writing* 18(2), report **accuracy F(2,68) = 4.262, p = .013
(significant)** and **sophistication F(2,68) = 1.68, p = .194 (n.s.)** — the
mirror image of Naismith & Juffs. **This is second-hand: it reaches this file via
Naismith & Juffs' discussion, and the primary was not retrieved.** **[S]**

So the position is a three-way split — one null, one positive, and one pair of
correlations (Crossley et al.) whose predictor and outcome are the same kind of
object — **and none of the three is decisive.**

### 3.6 What is still not established

> **GAP** — not established: whether **teaching** high-MI collocations raises a
> rated band. Every supporting finding in §3.3 is either correlational (Saito,
> Paquot, Kim et al.) or a rater-perception experiment on texts **no learner ever
> studied** (Naismith & Juffs). **No intervention study was found.** The ranking
> function specified in §8.1 is therefore a bet on a well-evidenced *correlate*,
> not on a demonstrated cause, and must be labelled that way wherever it is used.

> **GAP** — not established: whether collocational **accuracy** affects band
> outcomes. The evidence is a three-way split (§3.5), none of it decisive, and
> **no study has manipulated error gravity** — the one variable both sides blame.
> **Do not build an accuracy-scoring module** (`09` **F1**).

> **GAP** — not established: **any lexical feature that separates band 8 from
> band 9.** The nearest proxy in this literature is Paquot's C2-versus-C1 dobj
> contrast, which rests on **n = 11 C2 texts** in **French-L1 academic writing** —
> a different population, a different genre, and a sample too small to carry a
> ceiling claim. Nothing here licenses a "band 9 vocabulary" statement.

> **GAP** — not established: **what MI values are appropriate for A2/B1
> learners.** Every MI finding above comes from a **B2-and-above** population
> (Paquot B2–C2; Saito's speakers rated on advanced collocation use; Naismith &
> Juffs' band 4–8 scripts rated by examiners). Applying an MI threshold to
> grade-8 learners is **an extrapolation, not a finding.**

**What follows for tooling.** §8.1's ranking function is **no longer blocked** —
the measure question is resolved and the specification is written there. What
remains forbidden is narrower and specific: no ranking by **raw frequency band**,
no ranking by **CEFR level**, no ranking by **word-list membership**, no ranking
by **diversity**, and **no accuracy module at all**.

## 4. Grammar

### 4.1 What the descriptors actually ask for

Speaking, Grammatical Range & Accuracy, from the official band descriptors
([British Council / IELTS](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-speaking-band-descriptors.pdf)) **[S]**:

| Band | Grammatical Range & Accuracy (Speaking) |
| --- | --- |
| 9 | "Structures are precise and accurate at all times, apart from 'mistakes' characteristic of native speaker speech." |
| 8 | "Wide range of structures, flexibly used. The majority of sentences are error free." |
| 7 | "A range of structures flexibly used. Error-free sentences are frequent. Both simple and complex sentences are used effectively despite some errors. A few basic errors persist." |
| 6 | "Produces a mix of short and complex sentence forms and a variety of structures with limited flexibility. Though errors frequently occur in complex structures, these rarely impede communication." |
| 5 | "Basic sentence forms are fairly well controlled for accuracy. Complex structures are attempted but these are limited in range, nearly always contain errors and may lead to the need for reformulation." |
| 4 | "Can produce basic sentence forms and some short utterances are error-free. Subordinate clauses are rare and, overall, turns are short, structures are repetitive and errors are frequent." |

Writing Task 2 ([IELTS Writing Band Descriptors, updated May 2023](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf)) **[S]**:

| Band | Grammatical Range & Accuracy (Task 2) |
| --- | --- |
| 9 | "A wide range of structures is used with full flexibility and control." |
| 8 | "A wide range of structures is flexibly and accurately used. The majority of sentences are error-free." |
| 7 | "A variety of complex structures is used with some flexibility and accuracy … error-free sentences are frequent." |
| 6 | "A mix of simple and complex sentence forms is used but flexibility is limited. Examples of more complex structures are not marked by the same level of accuracy as in simple structures." |

**The word that appears at every level is *flexibility*, not *complexity*.** The
ladder the descriptors describe runs: *subordinate clauses are rare* (4) →
*complex structures attempted, nearly always contain errors* (5) → *mix of short
and complex, limited flexibility* (6) → *variety of complex structures, flexibly
used* (7) → *wide range, flexibly and accurately* (8/9). Complexity is a
precondition; **flexibility and accuracy are the discriminators.**

### 4.2 The research says syntactic complexity does not predict band

This kills a common curriculum assumption and it deserves to be stated flatly.
Banerjee et al. (2007), 275 scripts, bands 3–8, two L1 groups. The panel did not
sustain this claim; the report's abstract states it verbatim:

> "The results suggest all except the syntactic complexity measures investigated
> here are informative of increasing proficiency level."
> ([Banerjee et al. 2007](https://ielts.org/researchers/our-research/research-reports/documenting-features-of-written-language-production-typical-at-different-ielts-band-score-levels)) **[S/NS]**

> "Mean CL/T-unit appears to be a better discriminator of level at lower band
> levels, but it is not clear that either measure is a good discriminator from
> level 5 up." **[S/NS]**

> "Our findings for complexity measures were somewhat disappointing, in that by
> themselves none of the measures investigated seemed to provide a good
> predictor of IELTS band level." **[S/NS]**

Their data (both L1 groups combined): **[S/NS]**

| | Band 3 | Band 4 | Band 5 | Band 6 | Band 7 | Band 8 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mean clauses / T-unit | 1.43 | 3.01 | 3.68 | 3.59 | 4.22 | **3.38** |
| Mean dependent clauses / clause | 0.25 | 0.64 | 0.78 | 0.86 | 0.95 | **0.81** |

Note both measures **peak at band 7 and fall at band 8**. Subordination density
is not a band-9 target.

Kang et al. (2023) found something in the same direction for speaking: in the
regression on overall Speaking score, "grammatical complexity" was the second
strongest predictor but with a **negative** β = –0.25 (p = .004), and β = –0.32
(p = .0006) for Lexical Resource. **[S]**

> **Interpretation caveat, flagged rather than glossed.** Kang's speaking
> complexity composite mixes measures pointing in opposite directions: C-unit
> complexity is defined as "number of C-units divided by number of clauses" and
> verb phrase ratio as "number of C-units divided by number of verb phrases"
> (both *inverse* to subordination density), while dependent clause ratio runs
> the conventional way. **[S]** The composite's sign is therefore not safely
> interpretable as "more subordination lowers scores". Report the negative β,
> do not build on it.

In Kang's **writing** data, grammatical complexity (Biber-style phrasal features:
prepositions, attributive adjectives, nominalisations, pre-modifying nouns,
passives) correlated only r = .17–.29 with the scoring criteria, while **essay
length** correlated r = .52–.61 — the strongest single variable in the study.
Curiously, grammatical complexity correlated *more* with Task Response (.29) and
Lexical Resource (.26) than with Grammatical Range & Accuracy (.17). **[S]**

### 4.3 What does predict band: accuracy on specific structures

Banerjee et al., same study, grammatical accuracy measured as Target-Like Use
(correct suppliance in obligatory contexts):

> "Our findings are compatible with the predictions in the L2 development
> literature: accuracy on plural and copula was higher than accuracy on SV
> agreement and passives across levels and L1 groups. SV agreement and passives
> appear as the best measures of increased proficiency across the whole band
> range investigated here, and we believe they deserve further investigation,
> especially third person singular 's' marking, as it was not affected by the
> learner's L1."
> ([Banerjee et al. 2007](https://ielts.org/researchers/our-research/research-reports/documenting-features-of-written-language-production-typical-at-different-ielts-band-score-levels)) **[S/NS]**

Target-Like Use, L1 Chinese, Tasks 1 and 2 combined: **[S/NS]**

| | Band 3 | Band 4 | Band 5 | Band 6 | Band 7 | Band 8 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Copula (am/is/are) | 0.79 | 0.786 | 0.895 | 0.935 | 0.966 | 0.857 |
| **S-V agreement (3PS -s)** | **0.34** | **0.526** | **0.669** | **0.81** | **0.838** | **1.0** |
| **Passives** | **0.41** | **0.427** | **0.659** | **0.714** | **0.767** | **1.0** |

*(The band-8 row of the source table has one fewer value than columns, so the
band-8 assignments above are the most likely reading of an ambiguous extraction;
the bands 3–7 trajectory is unambiguous and is what the argument rests on.)*

Copula accuracy saturates by band 5. Third-person -s and passives climb the whole
way. **These are the two structures that track the band scale from 3 to 8, and
one of them is L1-independent.**

### 4.4 Error rates, keyed to band

Müller & Han (2022), *IELTS Research Reports* 1/22. 500 IELTS Writing samples
(100 per half-band, 5.5–7.5), 144,671 words, 12,269 containing grammatical
errors, tagged for 8 parts of speech and 33 subtypes. **[S]**

Mean error rate by band (% of uses of that type that were erroneous):

| IELTS band | 5.5 | 6.0 | 6.5 | 7.0 | 7.5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Determiners** | 18.7% | 14.2% | 12.8% | 8.0% | 4.7% |
| Prepositions | 16.2% | 10.0% | 8.5% | 7.7% | 5.2% |
| Nouns | 13.2% | 9.9% | 7.7% | 8.4% | 7.3% |
| Verbs | 14.7% | 10.5% | 8.8% | 6.1% | 5.0% |
| Pronouns | 18.0% | 8.3% | 6.7% | 6.0% | 6.0% |
| Conjunctions | 14.3% | 10.1% | 5.7% | 6.3% | 4.7% |
| Adverbs | 14.5% | 7.4% | 7.1% | 5.2% | 3.7% |
| Adjectives | 10.0% | 6.6% | 4.4% | 4.7% | 3.3% |
| **Average** | **14.8%** | **10.1%** | **8.3%** | **6.0%** | **4.9%** |

> "the worst rate was 3 errors per 20 words at IELTS 5.5, and the best rate was
> 1 error per 20 words at IELTS 7.5." **[S]**

Two things to take from this. First, **determiners are the single largest error
source at every band** and improve the most across the whole range — and
determiners/articles are exactly Unit 5's grammar target in the grade-8
syllabus. Second, improvement is **not linear**:

> "there is a period of mixed regression and improvement found for scores 6.5
> and 7.0 where scores jump around unexpectedly. This goes against an assumption
> that improvement is linear" **[S]**

The CEFR Companion Volume says the same thing independently:

> "research in English, French and German suggests that inaccuracy increases at
> around B1 as the learner is beginning to use language more independently and
> creatively. The fact that accuracy does not increase in a linear manner is
> reflected in the descriptors."
> ([CEFR Companion Volume 2020, Grammatical accuracy](https://rm.coe.int/common-european-framework-of-reference-for-languages-learning-teaching/16809ea0d4)) **[S]**

**This is a design constraint for progress metrics.** A tool that reports "your
error rate went up, you got worse" will be wrong precisely at the A2→B1
transition our learners are making. Rising error rate alongside rising range is
the expected signature of progress at this level.

Müller & Han also found first language matters: "First-language background was
found to have an effect, with significant variation found between grammatical
error rates within the same IELTS score for different first-language groups."
**[S]** No Vietnamese-specific figure is reported.

### 4.5 Vietnamese L1: the inflectional -s problem

Dong & Nguyen (2024), *Electronic Journal of Foreign Language Teaching* 21(1),
36–54. 32 Vietnamese third-year English majors, one 45-minute opinion essay each;
10 followed up with semi-structured interviews. **[S]**

| Morpheme | Obligatory contexts | Correct | Incorrect | **Omitted** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Plural -s | 546 | 442 (80.95%) | 1 (0.18%) | **103 (18.87%)** |
| 3rd-person singular -s | 155 | 105 (67.74%) | 0 | **50 (32.26%)** |

The difference in omission rate is significant (χ² = 12.693, p < .001). Omission,
not misuse, is the error: incorrect use was one instance across the whole corpus.
Within plural -s, the *-es* form was omitted most (45.45%), then *-ies* (32.5%),
then *-s* (17.17%), and the difference is significant (χ² = 142.864, p < .001).

The explanation offered:

> "Omission of 3SG–s and PN–s could be partly explained by Vietnamese L1 as an
> isolating language which does not mark inflection … Affixation is absent in
> the Vietnamese language, which poses a challenge for students to map such
> abstract features as 3SG–s and PN–s to surface forms."
> ([Dong & Nguyen 2024](https://e-flt.nus.edu.sg/v21n12024/dong.pdf)) **[S]**

And the mode effect, which is large:

> "the omission rate of 3SG–s in students' writing performance (32.26%) was much
> lower than that in oral speech by Vietnamese English-major university students
> (about 78.4%) as reported in Nguyen and Newton's (2020) study" **[S]**

**Put this beside Banerjee.** Third-person -s is the structure that discriminates
IELTS bands across the whole range *and is L1-independent in its band
trajectory*, and it is the structure Vietnamese learners omit in roughly a third
of written and (per the cited figure) three-quarters of spoken obligatory
contexts — even as English majors in year three. For a Vietnamese-market course
this is arguably the highest-value single grammar target in the document.

Note the caveats: N = 32, one task, one prompt, university English majors rather
than grade-8 learners, and the 78.4% oral figure is Dong & Nguyen citing Nguyen &
Newton (2020) rather than their own data.

### 4.6 The grammar ladder, and where grade 8 sits on it

The grade-8 syllabus (`curriculum/syllabus.md`) builds sentence complexity:
simple → compound (U3, coordinating conjunctions) → complex with subordination
(U7, adverbial time clauses) → reported speech (U11–U12). Mapped against what
this research supports:

| Syllabus stage | Descriptor band it unlocks | Evidence status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple sentences, U1–U2 | Below band 4 ceiling — "basic sentence forms" | Descriptor **[S]** |
| Compound (U3) | Band 4 "can link simple sentences … repetitious use of connectives" | Descriptor **[S]** |
| Complex / subordination (U7) | Band 5 threshold — band 4 is defined by "subordinate clauses are rare" | Descriptor **[S]** |
| Articles & zero article (U5) | Directly attacks the **largest** error category at every band 5.5–7.5 | Müller & Han **[S]** |
| Present simple, S-V agreement (U8 and throughout) | The best single accuracy discriminator, bands 3–8 | Banerjee **[S/NS]** |
| First conditional, future (U6) | Modality range; no band-specific evidence found | — |
| Reported speech (U11–U12) | No band-specific evidence found | — |
| Passives | **Not in the grade-8 syllabus.** Second-best accuracy discriminator, bands 3–8 | Banerjee **[S/NS]** |

**Above grade 8**, what the evidence supports sequencing next is *not* more
subordination. It is:

1. **Accuracy consolidation on 3rd-person -s and determiners** — the two
   structures with the strongest, longest band trajectories.
2. **Passives** — the syllabus's largest gap against the evidence. Banerjee's
   passive TLU climbs 0.41 → 1.0 across bands 3–8. The syllabus never targets
   it. Task 1 process descriptions need it (see `05-writing.md`).
3. **Flexibility drills** — same content, multiple structures — because
   *flexibility*, not *complexity*, is the word the descriptors use at 6→7→8.
4. Only then: cleft, inversion, and other emphasis structures.

> **GAP** — not established in this research pass: band-level evidence for
> conditionals, modality, reported speech, cleft, or inversion. The plan asked
> for structures "mapped to bands"; the descriptors name only "complex
> structures", "range", and "flexibility" generically, and no source in this
> pass mapped named structures above subordination to bands. Any claim that
> "inversion is a band-8 structure" would be invented.

## 5. Phonology

### 5.1 What IELTS scores

Pronunciation is one of four Speaking criteria and has no Writing counterpart.
The band descriptors ([British Council / IELTS](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-speaking-band-descriptors.pdf)) **[S]**:

| Band | Pronunciation |
| --- | --- |
| 9 | "Uses a full range of phonological features to convey precise and/or subtle meaning. Flexible use of features of connected speech is sustained throughout. Can be effortlessly understood throughout. Accent has no effect on intelligibility." |
| 8 | "Uses a wide range of phonological features … Can sustain appropriate rhythm. Flexible use of stress and intonation across long utterances, despite occasional lapses. … Accent has minimal effect on intelligibility." |
| 7 | "Displays all the positive features of band 6, and some, but not all, of the positive features of band 8." |
| 6 | "Uses a range of phonological features, but control is variable. Chunking is generally appropriate, but rhythm may be affected by a lack of stress-timing and/or a rapid speech rate. Some effective use of intonation and stress, but this is not sustained. Individual words or phonemes may be mispronounced but this causes only occasional lack of clarity." |
| 5 | "Displays all the positive features of band 4, and some, but not all, of the positive features of band 6." |
| 4 | "Uses some acceptable phonological features, but the range is limited. Produces some acceptable chunking, but there are frequent lapses in overall rhythm. Attempts to use intonation and stress, but control is limited. Individual words or phonemes are frequently mispronounced, causing lack of clarity." |

The named feature set is: **individual phonemes, chunking, rhythm/stress-timing,
stress, intonation, connected speech, intelligibility, accent effect.** Segmental
accuracy appears at bands 4 and 6 and then *disappears* — bands 8 and 9 talk only
about range, flexibility, connected speech, and effect on the listener. The
scale's own architecture says segmentals are a floor, suprasegmentals are the
ceiling.

**This validates the grade-8 progression.** The syllabus runs vowel contrasts
(U1–U4) → nasals and consonant clusters (U5–U8) → word stress, sentence stress,
intonation (U9–U12). That is the descriptor ladder's own order: get phonemes to
"not causing lack of clarity" first, then build the suprasegmental range that
bands 7–9 are made of.

*It validates the **order**, not the **coverage**. §5.5.8 records what the order
leaves out: no unit in the twelve targets syllable-final consonants, which is the
best-evidenced Vietnamese difficulty in this document.*

### 5.2 What actually discriminates the upper bands

Isaacs, Trofimovich, Yu & Muñoz Chereau (2015), *IELTS Research Reports* 2015/4.
Speech samples from **80 test-takers** on the long-turn task, rated by **8
accredited IELTS examiners** on eight semantic-differential measures; discriminant
analysis against pre-rated bands 5–8.

Structure matrix, Function 1 (the only significant discriminating function,
accounting for 90.2% of variance in Pronunciation band placement, canonical
R² = .25, Wilks' λ = .73, χ²(24) = 192.271, p < .0001): **[S]**

| Measure | Loading on Function 1 |
| --- | --- |
| Grammatical accuracy & sentence structure | **.945** |
| Lexical richness | **.863** |
| Speech chunking | .745 |
| Intonation | .735 |
| Word stress | .668 |
| Speech rate | .633 |
| Vowel & consonant errors | .551 |
| Comprehensibility | .462 |

Two findings, both awkward and both important:

1. **Within genuinely phonological features, the order is chunking > intonation >
   word stress > segmentals.** The suprasegmental end of our U9–U12 strand is
   where the upper-band discrimination lives; the segmental end (U1–U8) loads
   lowest. Segmentals are necessary and insufficient — exactly what the
   descriptors imply.
2. **The two strongest loadings on the *Pronunciation* scale are not phonological
   at all.** Grammatical accuracy and lexical richness load higher than every
   pronunciation feature. The report is a scale-validation study and treats this
   as a transparency problem in the descriptors, noting that "grammatical
   accuracy/sentence structure are beyond the remit of the IELTS Pronunciation
   scale". **[S]** Read it as a caution: examiner pronunciation judgements are
   contaminated by the rest of the performance.

The function separated bands 5–6 from bands 7–8 (group centroids: band 5 –.645,
band 6 –.392, band 7 .425, band 8 .770) but classification accuracy was only
**47%** for Pronunciation and 46.5% for Speaking, with band 6 misclassified 80%
of the time. **[S]** The upper bands of the Pronunciation scale are not cleanly
separable by any measured feature set. Do not build a tool that claims to.

A companion study (Yates, Zielinski & Pryor, *IELTS Research Reports*, Round 14)
reached a compatible conclusion from the examiner side: examiners were "most
confident about making global judgements of intelligibility and listener effort,
which were the features they considered to be the most important when awarding a
Pronunciation score", but "The distinction between Bands 6 and 7 seemed to be
particularly problematic, and there was a tendency to award a 6 to Band 7
speakers." **[S]**

### 5.3 The CEFR phonological ladder

The CEFR Companion Volume 2020 replaced the 2001 phonology scale entirely, and
the replacement is the best-structured phonology progression available to this
project. It splits into three sub-scales; the middle rungs are what a grade-8
course is climbing.
([CEFR Companion Volume 2020, Phonological control](https://rm.coe.int/common-european-framework-of-reference-for-languages-learning-teaching/16809ea0d4)) **[S]**

| CEFR | Sound articulation | Prosodic features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A2** | "Pronunciation is generally intelligible when communicating in simple everyday situations, provided the interlocutor makes an effort to understand specific sounds. Systematic mispronunciation of phonemes does not hinder intelligibility…" | "Can use the prosodic features of everyday words and phrases intelligibly, in spite of a strong influence on stress, intonation and/or rhythm from the other language(s) they speak. Prosodic features (e.g. word stress) are adequate for familiar everyday words and simple utterances." |
| **B1** | "Is generally intelligible throughout, despite regular mispronunciation of individual sounds and words they are less familiar with." | "Can convey their message in an intelligible way in spite of a strong influence on stress, intonation and/or rhythm from the other language(s) they speak." |
| **B2** | "Can articulate a high proportion of the sounds in the target language clearly in extended stretches of production; is intelligible throughout, despite a few systematic mispronunciations. Can generalise from their repertoire to predict the phonological features of most unfamiliar words (e.g. word stress) with reasonable accuracy." | "Can employ prosodic features (e.g. stress, intonation, rhythm) to support the message they intend to convey, though with some influence from the other languages they speak." |
| **C1** | "Can articulate virtually all the sounds of the target language with a high degree of control. They can usually self-correct if they noticeably mispronounce a sound." | "Can produce smooth, intelligible spoken discourse with only occasional lapses in control of stress, rhythm and/or intonation… Can vary intonation and place stress correctly in order to express precisely what they mean to say." |
| **C2** | "Can articulate virtually all the sounds of the target language with clarity and precision." | "Can exploit prosodic features (e.g. stress, rhythm and intonation) appropriately and effectively in order to convey finer shades of meaning (e.g. to differentiate and emphasise)." |

Note the CEFR's own framing: at A2 and B1, **L1 influence on prosody is expected
and explicitly permitted**; the ladder is about intelligibility, not accent
removal. The Companion Volume is blunt about why the scale was rewritten:

> "The focus on accent and on accuracy instead of on intelligibility has been
> detrimental to the development of the teaching of pronunciation." **[S]**

This aligns with band 9's "Accent has no effect on intelligibility" — which is a
statement about *effect*, not about accent elimination.

### 5.4 Segmentals vs suprasegmentals: an unsettled teaching debate

Jenkins' Lingua Franca Core proposes teaching a reduced segmental set and
dropping most prosody. Dauer's TESOL Quarterly review summarises the core: all
consonants except /θ, ð/; aspiration of initial /p, t, k/; **"No omission of
consonants in word initial clusters"** with medial/final cluster omission only on
inner-circle rules ("facts = fax, bands = bans"); long/short vowel contrasts;
vowel shortening before voiceless and lengthening before voiced consonants; and
in prosody only "Correct placement and production (lengthening) of nuclear stress
and contrastive stress" plus "Division of the speech stream into word groups."
Jenkins recommends **not** teaching weak forms, word stress, stress-timing, or
pitch movement.
([Dauer 2005, *TESOL Quarterly* 39(3), 543–550](http://www.czymskorupka.edu.pl/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Dauer-LinguaFrancaCore-2005.pdf)) **[S]**

**The LFC does not transfer to IELTS, and Dauer's own review explains why.** The
LFC is derived from non-native-to-non-native intelligibility: "the research
showing the importance of suprasegmentals in intelligibility has been based
entirely on NS listeners, who may process speech differently from NNSs." **[S]**
IELTS Speaking is rated by trained, predominantly native-speaker examiners.
Dauer cites the directly relevant counter-evidence: Anderson-Hsieh, Johnson &
Koehler (1992) "found overall prosody (as opposed to segmental or syllable
structure errors) had a [greater] influence on pronunciation ratings for
standardized spoken language tests", and Derwing & Rossiter (2003) that "prosodic
training transfers to extemporaneously produced speech, but that segmental
training, although resulting in improvement in reading aloud, does not." **[S]**

**Implication for U1–U12:** the syllabus's cluster work (U6 /br/–/pr/, U7
/bl/–/kl/, U8 /sp/–/st/) is defensible on both models — the LFC insists on no
omission in *word-initial* clusters, and all three contrasts are productive
word-initially, so teaching them in initial position satisfies both accounts.
The suprasegmental block (U9–U12) is defensible on the
IELTS-relevant evidence (Isaacs' chunking/intonation loadings, Anderson-Hsieh via
Dauer, the CEFR prosodic ladder) and is what the LFC would drop. **For an
exam-oriented course, keep U9–U12 and treat the LFC as an argument about
priorities under time pressure, not about what IELTS rewards.**

**A second reason to reject the LFC for this market, added 2026-08-08.** The LFC
explicitly *permits* medial and final cluster omission on inner-circle rules
("facts = fax, bands = bans") and reserves its no-omission rule for word-initial
clusters. That is precisely backwards for Vietnamese learners: §5.5 establishes
that **coda** obstruents are their largest and most persistent difficulty —
28.4% omission of final /s~z/ at C1, rising to 46.7% in coda clusters — while
onset clusters are damaged mostly by *voicing* errors rather than omission. A
model that licenses coda omission licenses the exact error this population
already makes by default, and it takes the third-person-/plural--s signal down
with it (§5.5.2). **Do not adopt the LFC's coda permission.**

### 5.5 Vietnamese L1 pronunciation

*Rewritten 2026-08-08. The gap this section previously recorded is **now largely
closed**, by a targeted pass that retrieved seven qualifying sources — including
the `ctujs.ctu.edu.vn` article 448 that the earlier pass could not open. Three
sub-questions remain open and are marked as sub-GAPs below.*

**Evidential status of everything in §5.5: [S].** Every claim here is quoted from
a primary document retrieved and held in the research cache; **none has been
through the 3-vote adversarial panel.** Treat this section as well-evidenced but
singly-sourced. Sample sizes are reported for every empirical claim because they
are uniformly small — the largest Vietnamese-L1 phonology study cited here has
36 participants, and several have fewer than 20.

**A standing caution that applies to the whole section.** The strongest source
here (Disney & Le 2024) is itself an argument *against* predicting Vietnamese
learners' errors by contrasting the two sound systems. Do not extend anything
below by contrastive reasoning of the form "Vietnamese lacks X, therefore X will
be hard". Where the evidence stops, it stops.

#### 5.5.1 What Vietnamese permits in a syllable coda

This is the structural fact the rest of the section rests on, and it is
well-established. From the *Journal of the International Phonetic Association*
Illustration of Hanoi Vietnamese:

> "Hanoi Vietnamese licenses eight segments in coda position: three unreleased
> voiceless obstruents /p t k/ ([p̚ t̚ k̚]), three nasals /m n ŋ/, and two
> approximants /j w/."
> ([Kirby 2011, *JIPA* 41(3), 381–392](https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/589949/S0025100311000181a.pdf)) **[S]**

> "A Vietnamese syllable consists of three obligatory elements: an onset, a tone
> and a vowel. The syllable may optionally contain an obstruent, nasal, or
> approximant coda."
> ([Kirby 2011](https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/589949/S0025100311000181a.pdf)) **[S]**

Two consequences follow directly, and only these two should be drawn:

1. **No fricative or affricate may close a Vietnamese syllable, and no voiced
   obstruent may.** Vietnamese *has* /f v s z x ɣ h/ — but onset-only. So English
   final /s z f v θ ð ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ b d g/ are all outside the L1 coda inventory,
   while final /p t k m n ŋ/ are inside it. Disney & Le state the corollary
   plainly: "Fricatives are not permitted word-finally." **[S]**
2. **A Vietnamese syllable has one onset consonant and no cluster** — the
   structure statement above admits an onset, a tone, a vowel and at most one
   coda segment. Nothing in Kirby's inventory is a cluster in either position.

Note the scope limit: Kirby's Illustration is explicitly of **Hanoi** (Northern)
Vietnamese. See §5.5.5.

#### 5.5.2 Word-final /s/ and /z/ — and the phonology/morphology question, answered

This is the sub-question the plan asked most precisely, and there is now a study
that answers it directly.

**Disney & Le (2024), *Languages* 9(10), 327.** Sixteen Vietnamese adult learners
of English in Hanoi. All had just completed a four-year dual-award BA validated
by a UK university and were training as English-language professionals; all "have
all achieved a minimum equivalent of C1 … in terms of the Common European
Framework of Reference for Languages" **[S]**. Data are naturalistic: fifteen-minute
individual graduation presentations, video-recorded for quality assurance rather
than for research. Consent was sought from the whole cohort (n = 38), 32
consented, and the 16 analysed are those whose audio was usable — "an essentially
random sample because the quality of the recording was random" **[S]**. After
cleaning: **25,000 words, 240 minutes, 3,858 tokens with a target final /s/ or
/z/.** Coding was perceptual, by one native-speaker coder, Praat-assisted, with a
re-check error rate < 1%.
([Disney & Le 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/languages9100327))

Overall omission, and the two main effects:

| Condition | Instances | Mean error (omission) rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **All /s~z/** | 3,858 | **28.4%** (range across speakers 11.5%–48.8%) |
| Lexical /s~z/ (e.g. *ice*) | 2,219 (57.5%) | 16.8% |
| **Morphological {-s}** (e.g. *eat-s*) | 1,639 (42.5%) | **45.1%** |
| Preceded by a vowel | 2,650 (68.7%) | 19.7% |
| **Preceded by a consonant** (i.e. in a coda cluster) | 1,208 (31.3%) | **46.7%** |

**[S]**

And the 2×2, which is the useful table for teaching:

| Combination | Mean error rate |
| --- | --- |
| Lexical /s~z/ after a vowel | 15.3% |
| Lexical /s~z/ after a consonant | 32.5% |
| Morphological {-s} after a vowel | 36.4% |
| **Morphological {-s} after a consonant** | **50.7%** |

**[S]**

A multilevel binary logistic regression (random intercept by participant,
F(3,3744) = 126, p < 0.001, 75.4% correctly classified) gave OR = 3.59 (95% CI
2.55–5.05, p < 0.001) for morphological vs lexical, and OR = 2.14 (95% CI
1.70–2.68, p < 0.001) for preceding consonant vs vowel. **[S]**

**The answer to the plan's question — are the phonological and morphological
problems separable? — is yes, and the study says so explicitly.** The interaction
was not significant (OR = 0.88, 95% CI 0.59–1.31, **p = 0.519**):

> "This suggests that the issues are stacked rather than interacting and that the
> combination of cluster + morpheme is not creating additional problems for
> learners. … This serves to highlight the difference between the general
> tendency for /s~z/ elision as a phonological process, in contrast to the
> omission of a morpheme as a grammatical construction-forming process. It also
> follows that it would be impossible to ascertain a phonological or
> morphological cause for a given case of C+ morphological {-s} elision."
> ([Disney & Le 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/languages9100327)) **[S]**

**Read that carefully, because it cuts two ways.** The two causes are *additive
and independently attackable* — roughly a 27–28 percentage-point penalty each,
which is why the teaching response must be two separate interventions and not
one. But for **any individual token** of *students* or *asks*, you cannot tell
which cause produced the omission. So a diagnostic tool must not claim to.

**This converges with §4.5 across modality, which is the strongest thing about
it.** Disney & Le's morphological {-s} splits by word class: **1,511 plural nouns
at a 41.7% error rate, and only 112 verbs at 58%** **[S]** — third-person -s worse
than plural -s, in speech. Dong & Nguyen (§4.5) found the same ordering in
writing (18.87% plural vs 32.26% third-person). Two independent studies, two
modalities, two samples, same direction. **Third-person -s is the single most
omitted inflection for Vietnamese learners in both channels**, and §4.5 already
established it is also the structure that discriminates IELTS bands across the
whole range.

Two further findings worth building on:

- **Frequency protects.** Correlation between overall /s~z/ frequency and accuracy
  is weak across speakers (r = 0.34), but *is* and *this* alone account for 24.8%
  of all /s~z/ tokens at only a 12% error rate; six high-frequency function words
  account for 31% of all tokens at 11%. **Removing those six raises the overall
  error rate from 28.4% to 41%.** **[S]** A drill built on high-frequency
  function words will flatter the learner; the real difficulty is in content
  words.
- **The problem is not word-final position, it is the coda.** "it is not WF /s~z/
  that is 'the problem' per se, but the issue concerns syllable coda /s~z/.
  Indeed, the other fricatives and the affricates are also frequently omitted."
  **[S]**

**Caveats that must travel with these numbers.** N = 16; all in Hanoi; all at or
above C1 — the authors chose them precisely because "If even these people exhibit
extensive elision of WF /s/ and /z/, then for less advanced learners, the vast
majority, the situation must be even more challenging" **[S]**, which is an
argument, not a measurement, and **our grade-8 learners are the less advanced
majority**. One formal monologue genre only. Voicing could not be determined
acoustically, so /s/ and /z/ are conflated. Single coder. And the authors' own
headline conclusion is a caution against L1-transfer explanations: "the observed
omission rates are not solely attributable to L1 transfer effects" **[S]**.

#### 5.5.3 Consonant clusters — and a direct hit on the U6–U8 strand

**Tran & Nguyen (2022), *Can Tho University Journal of Innovation and Sustainable
Development* 14(3), 32–39.** This is the paper the previous pass listed but could
not open. **36 Vietnamese EFL undergraduates** (the abstract says 39 and the
methods section says 36 — *the paper is internally inconsistent; 36 is the figure
used throughout the analysis*), 5 male / 31 female, aged 19–20, ≥7 years of
secondary English plus one university year, none of whom "have never been to any
of the countries where English is spoken as the first language" **[S]**. Three
sub-tests — word list, picture-prompted question-response, and read-aloud
sentences — delivered over Zoom, 1,080 tokens per cluster type, scored
independently by two examiners (Pearson r = 0.98, p < .001).
([Tran & Nguyen 2022](https://ctujs.ctu.edu.vn/index.php/ctujs/article/view/448), DOI 10.22144/ctu.jen.2022.040)

**The study covers onset clusters only.** Two-consonant: /br/, /kr/, /pr/, /st/,
/tr/. Three-consonant: /skr/, /skw/, /spl/, /spr/, /str/.

| | Correct | Mistakes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **CC** (two-consonant onsets) | 828 (76.67%) | **252 (23.33%)** |
| **CCC** (three-consonant onsets) | 906 (83.89%) | **174 (16.11%)** |

**[S]**

And the error *types*, which is where the teaching value is:

| Error type | CC onsets | CCC onsets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Feature change** (voicing/aspiration) | **195 (77.4%)** | 29 (16.6%) |
| **Deletion** | 56 (22.2%) | **136 (78.2%)** |
| Epenthesis (vowel insertion) | 1 (0.4%) | 9 (5.2%) |

**[S]**

Within deletion, the pattern is sharply directional. In CC clusters the **first**
consonant goes in 55 of 56 cases and the second in 1 — i.e. the plosive is
dropped and the liquid survives. In CCC clusters the casualties are the first
(77) and second (56) consonants, the third almost never (1), because:

> "the pre-initial /s/ does not appear in any consonant clusters in the
> Vietnamese language. Therefore, adding it to the already complex two-consonant
> clusters is a challenge for Vietnamese learners."
> ([Tran & Nguyen 2022](https://ctujs.ctu.edu.vn/index.php/ctujs/article/view/448)) **[S]**

**The single most syllabus-relevant finding in this whole section is that for
two-consonant onsets, deletion is *not* the main error — feature change is, at
77.4%.** The authors attribute it to aspiration and voicing:

> "the learners found it difficult in pronouncing CC clusters; and therefore,
> they tended to change voiceless plosives with an approximant in CC clusters,
> such as /t/, /p/, and /k/ in /tr/, /pl/, and /kr/ … the learners had a tendency
> to change the voicing feature of voiceless consonants into the voiced ones"
> ([Tran & Nguyen 2022](https://ctujs.ctu.edu.vn/index.php/ctujs/article/view/448)) **[S]**

**This validates the syllabus's cluster units precisely.** U6 (/br/ vs /pr/) and
U7 (/bl/ vs /kl/) are voicing contrasts on the onset plosive — exactly the
feature that carries 77.4% of Vietnamese CC onset errors. **A grade-8 unit that
drills /br/–/pr/ as a voicing-and-aspiration contrast is aimed at the dominant
observed error, not at a textbook convention.** Two honest limits: /br/ and /pr/
were both directly tested, but **/bl/ and /kl/ were not** (the CC set was /br kr
pr st tr/), so U7 is supported by extension from /kr/ and not directly; and of
U8's pair, **/st/ was tested but /sp/ was not**.

**Coda clusters are a different matter and are only indirectly evidenced.** The
CTU study does not test them. The one quantified figure available is Disney &
Le's: /s~z/ preceded by a consonant — i.e. in a coda cluster — was omitted at
**46.7%** against **19.7%** after a vowel, an odds ratio of 2.14. **[S]** That is
a real coda-cluster reduction measurement, but only for clusters ending in
/s~z/.

The literature also *disagrees with itself* about epenthesis, and the file should
record that rather than pick a side. Tran & Nguyen report 0.4% (CC) and 5.2%
(CCC), and note the conflict in the prior literature: "Sato (1984) did not find
epenthesis to be a common strategy used in the pronunciation of consonant
clusters while Benson (1986) found a considerable preference for insertion of
schwa after obstruents" **[S]**. Sato and Benson are second-hand citations here
and were not retrieved. **On the retrieved evidence, epenthesis is a minor
strategy for Vietnamese onset clusters and deletion/feature-change dominate.**

**Caveats.** N = 36 undergraduates at one university; read-aloud and prompted
tasks, not spontaneous speech; conducted over Zoom; onset clusters only; the
paper's own limitation statement says "the number of participants in the study
was quite small, which significantly limits the generalizability of the
findings" **[S]**. For coda clusters, the only Vietnamese-specific study located
is a **single-speaker case study** — Osburne (1996), *Applied Linguistics* 17(2),
164–181 — which is a source for the phenomenon's existence and nothing more.

#### 5.5.4 Tone-language transfer to intonation, stress and rhythm

This is where the evidence is thinnest relative to how much the plan wanted from
it, and where the honest answer is partial.

**Intonation — the one well-evidenced result.** Nguyễn & Đào (2018),
*Asian-Pacific Journal of Second and Foreign Language Education* 3(1), art. 2.
**N = 30: 10 native Australian English controls, and 20 Southern Vietnamese
learners — 10 beginners (students in Ho Chi Minh City) and 10 advanced (IELTS
≥ 6.5, resident in Australia 6 months to 2 years).** 25 sentences (5 statements,
5 Yes-No, 5 Or-, 4 Tag-, 6 Wh-questions) elicited as genuine information-seeking
questions; 750 utterances prosodically annotated for the final tune. Mixed-effects
model: Group n.s.; Tunes and Group × Tunes significant (p < 0.05).
([Nguyễn & Đào 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40862-018-0044-4), Gold OA)

Two findings matter for teaching:

> "neither of the L2 learner groups accommodate to this feature … this result is
> indicative of the transfer of L1 Vietnamese prosodic structure of a lexical
> tone language in which every syllable is specified for a tone and there was
> neither toneless syllable nor tonal reduction."
> ([Nguyễn & Đào 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40862-018-0044-4)) **[S]**

— that is, **Vietnamese learners fail to deaccent**, because in a language where
every syllable carries a tone there is no toneless syllable to model deaccenting
on. And:

> "Southern speakers of Vietnamese tend to raise F0 at the end of questions,
> particularly the final words/syllables. This happens to all speakers in their
> study." **[S]**

— producing rise-overuse on Wh-questions (beginners: R 61%) and on tag questions,
where beginners scattered across F-F, L-L, L-R and R-R against the native F-R
(advanced: 93%). The learner data also required a tune category, **level (L)**,
that the native inventory does not use. **[S]**

**This is the specific, trainable, Vietnamese-specific intonation finding the
plan asked for**, and it is a good fit for U11 (stress in statements vs
questions) and U12. Note the population: **all 20 learners were Southern
Vietnamese**, and the transfer mechanism is explicitly grounded in *Southern*
question prosody.

**Word stress — contested at the root, and the key numbers are unretrievable.**
The standard account is Nguyễn & Ingram's: that Vietnamese learners mark English
stress with pitch and intensity but are insensitive to duration, transferring
tonal cue-weighting. The thesis abstract states it verbatim — "Vietnamese
learners showed tonal transfer effects in their realization of the English stress
contrasts in their reliance mainly on pitch and intensity and insensitivity to
duration cues" ([Nguyễn 2004, PhD thesis, University of
Queensland](https://espace.library.uq.edu.au/view/UQ:107093)) **[S]** — but the
thesis is not open access and **no sample size for any of its seven experiments
could be retrieved**. The two journal versions (Nguyen & Ingram 2005, *TESOL
Quarterly* 39(2), 309–319; Nguyen, Ingram & Pensalfini 2008, *Journal of
Phonetics* 36(1), 158–190) exist and were confirmed bibliographically, but their
abstracts and bodies could not be retrieved from any route. **Do not quote
numbers for them.**

And the premise itself is disputed. Brunelle (2017), *JIPA* 47(3), 283–320 —
**N = 18 Southern Vietnamese speakers (8 + 10), aged 18–26, recorded in Ho Chi
Minh City**, three word lists in carrier sentences, mixed-effects models on
duration, intensity, f0, f0 range and formants — concludes:

> "Results suggest that there is little evidence for word stress in Southern
> Vietnamese and that reports of final stress can be reinterpreted as
> phrase-final lengthening."
> ([Brunelle 2017](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0025100316000402)) **[S]**

Brunelle names Nguyễn & Ingram among the authors he is arguing against. **So
"Vietnamese has word stress that learners transfer to English" is a contested
claim, not a settled one.** A course may still teach English word stress — the
IELTS descriptors and Isaacs justify that independently — but it must not justify
it by asserting a specific Vietnamese transfer mechanism.

**Rhythm and vowel reduction — real effects, weak sourcing.** Nguyen & Ingram
(2004), *Proceedings of SST-2004*, compared **11 Vietnamese speakers (advanced;
IELTS ≥ 6.5; mostly MA TESOL students who had been EFL teachers) against 12
native Australian English speakers** on connected-speech processes. Vietnamese
speakers produced significantly less vowel reduction (native mean 2.7 vs
Vietnamese 1.2 incidences, Z = 2.0, p = .04), and much less coalescence (89% of
native tokens vs under 20%, Z = 2.8, p = .0039), liaison and assimilatory
consonant elision (both ~4.5× lower, p ≈ .004); syllabic consonants did not
differ significantly (p = .5). Their summary: "in spite of an advanced level of
English proficiency, many Vietnamese speakers of English failed to produce the
connected speech and assimilation processes which characterize native speakers'
spontaneous natural speech." **[S]**

**Weight this carefully.** The paper's own page footer reads *"Accepted after
abstract only review"* — **it is a conference paper that was not fully peer
reviewed**, and it is therefore below this file's source policy for a
load-bearing claim. It is recorded here because it is the only retrieved
measurement of connected-speech processes in Vietnamese-accented English, it
points the same way as Nguyễn & Đào's deaccenting result, and its absence would
be a bigger distortion than its presence with this caveat attached. **Do not
build a metric on it.**

> **GAP** — still not established: **any rhythm-metric characterisation of
> Vietnamese-accented English.** Searches for nPVI / %V / ΔC / Varco measures
> applied to English produced by Vietnamese learners returned nothing
> peer-reviewed. Grabe & Low (2002), the standard rhythm-class study, was
> retrieved in full and **does not include Vietnamese** — its 18 languages, one
> speaker each, do not cover it. The only rhythm-metric study of Vietnamese
> itself (Nguyen 2017, *Australian Journal of Linguistics* 37(4)) uses **four
> speakers per language** and describes Vietnamese as a language "which is
> *claimed to* cluster with syllable-timed languages" — the hedge is the
> author's. **Do not state that Vietnamese is syllable-timed and English is
> stress-timed as an established premise for a rhythm lesson.**

> **GAP** — still not established: any peer-reviewed study of Vietnamese
> learners' English **nuclear stress or tonic placement**. Nguyễn & Đào's
> deaccenting finding is the nearest thing and is about final tunes, not tonic
> placement.

#### 5.5.5 Northern vs Southern — the learner population is not uniform, and the literature is not balanced

The dialects genuinely differ, and a peer-reviewed source states how:

> "while northern and central varieties have glottalized tones …, southern
> dialects make no use of voice quality in tonal contrasts … unlike Northern
> Vietnamese, which has six tones, southern dialects (and many central dialects)
> have merged the tones C1 (*hỏi*) and C2 (*ngã*) and now have a five-tone
> inventory."
> ([Brunelle 2017, *JIPA* 47(3)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0025100316000402)) **[S]**

Kirby (2010), *JASA* 127(6), 3749–3757, shows the difference reaches perception:
**30 listeners (10 Northern, 20 Southern)** in a speeded AX discrimination task on
Northern stimuli — "Southern listeners were more likely to confuse tones produced
with nonmodal voice quality, whereas Northern listeners found tones with similar
pitch excursions to be more confusable." **[S]** That is L1 tone perception, not
English.

**For English codas, the difference is asserted but thinly evidenced.** Disney &
Le put it as follows:

> "This regional variation has no impact on the current study because there are
> no final fricatives in any Vietnamese dialect, although people from the North,
> and Hanoi in particular, have been shown to be less likely to elide word-final
> consonants (Hwa-Froelich et al. 2002, p. 270)."
> ([Disney & Le 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/languages9100327)) **[S]**

The first half is solid and is the important half: **no Vietnamese dialect permits
a final fricative, so the coda-fricative problem is dialect-independent.** The
second half is a second-hand citation to Hwa-Froelich, Hodson & Edwards (2002),
*American Journal of Speech-Language Pathology* 11(3), 264–273 — which is a
clinical **tutorial**, not a controlled study, illustrated by "speech samples of
three speakers representing the northern, central, and southern dialects" **[S]**.
**A North/South difference in coda elision resting on one speaker per dialect in
a tutorial is not a basis for differentiating teaching.**

Which region each study actually sampled:

| Source | Region sampled |
| --- | --- |
| Kirby 2011 (coda inventory) | **Hanoi / Northern**, explicitly |
| Disney & Le 2024 (final /s~z/) | **Hanoi / Northern** |
| Tran & Nguyen 2022 (onset clusters) | Can Tho University; the paper says only "a university in Vietnam" — **Southern by author affiliation, which is an inference, not a stated fact** |
| Nguyễn & Đào 2018 (question intonation) | **Southern**, explicitly, all 20 learners |
| Brunelle 2017 (word stress, tone inventories) | **Southern**, explicitly |
| Nguyen & Ingram 2004 (connected speech) | **Not stated** |

> **GAP** — not established: any quantified Northern vs Southern comparison of
> **English** production. The two best segmental studies are Northern and the
> best prosodic study is Southern, so the two halves of §5.5 are not drawn from
> the same population. **Do not build region-specific guidance.** The one
> defensible cross-regional statement is Disney & Le's: no Vietnamese dialect
> permits a final fricative.

#### 5.5.6 Vowels — the sub-gap that did not close

> **GAP** — not established: Vietnamese-L1 difficulty on the specific vowel
> contrasts the syllabus teaches in **U1 (/ʊ/ vs /uː/)** and **U2 (/ə/ vs /ɪ/)**.
> Searched: the Vietnamese-L1 phonology literature via DuckDuckGo, OpenAlex,
> Semantic Scholar and Crossref for perception/production studies on English
> vowel contrasts by Vietnamese speakers. What came back was either not
> peer-reviewed, or contrastive-inventory comparison papers rather than learner
> studies, or unretrievable. The one directly relevant item located — Cunningham,
> "Quality, quantity and intelligibility of vowels in Vietnamese-accented
> English" — reports that a non-Vietnamese-speaking listener could not
> distinguish *bead / beat / bid / bit* from Vietnamese speakers "at better than
> chance", on **16 Hanoi women across three groups** plus a 2-speaker/6-listener
> perception study. **It is conference proceedings (FONETIK 2009), not a
> peer-reviewed journal, and therefore below this file's source policy — it is
> recorded as a lead, not cited as evidence.** Note also that it concerns
> KIT/FLEECE, which is *not* one of the syllabus's contrasts.
>
> **Do not close this gap by contrastive reasoning.** "Vietnamese has no /ʊ/,
> therefore U1 is hard" is exactly the inference Disney & Le's results warn
> against. U1–U4 should be taught on the general phonetic case, and the file
> should not claim Vietnamese-specific evidence for them.

#### 5.5.7 Intelligibility impact — the ranking question, partially answered

The plan asked which features most affect *being understood*, as against which
merely sound non-native, because that ranking should drive teaching priority. One
qualifying source addresses it, and it is small.

**Zielinski (2008), *System* 36(1), 69–84.** Three native English listeners
orthographically transcribed connected conversational speech from **three L2
speakers — one Korean, one Mandarin, and one Vietnamese** — and were observed and
questioned about what they relied on when they could not identify a word.

> "Regardless of the speaker they were listening to, when attempting to identify
> the speaker's intended words, all three listeners relied heavily and
> consistently on the speaker's syllable stress pattern (the number and pattern
> of strong and weak syllables), and more consistently on segments in strong
> syllables than those in weak syllables. Both non-standard syllable stress
> patterns and non-standard segments misled them into wrongly identifying a
> speaker's intended words, but for all three speakers, **it was non-standard
> segments in strong syllables that misled those most often.**"
> ([Zielinski 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2007.11.004)) **[S]**

**N = 1 Vietnamese speaker.** This is not a finding about Vietnamese learners; it
is a finding about three L2 speakers, one of whom was Vietnamese. It is included
because it is the only retrieved peer-reviewed study that ranks feature types by
*intelligibility* rather than by examiner rating, and because Zielinski is a
co-author of the Yates et al. IELTS Pronunciation-scale report already cited in
§5.2 — the two are the same research programme viewed from the listener side.

The other statement bearing on intelligibility is Disney & Le's, and it is an
argument rather than a measurement: "because omission of /s~z/ is so frequent in
speech and can have a significant effect on a hearer's comprehension, we would
argue that teachers do have a certain responsibility…" **[S]**. Tran & Nguyen, by
contrast, report the opposite for their own phenomenon, citing a study of a
different L1: "Although cluster simplification might not affect the
comprehensibility of L2 speakers (Bouchhioua, 2019), it partly contributed to
their classification as having a strong accent." **[S]**

> **GAP** — not established: an intelligibility ranking of Vietnamese-specific
> pronunciation features with an adequate sample. No study was found that takes a
> reasonable number of Vietnamese speakers, measures listener comprehension, and
> ranks the features by damage done. The ordering in §5.5.8 is therefore built
> from *difficulty and persistence* evidence plus one N=1 intelligibility result,
> not from a Vietnamese intelligibility study. **Label it as such wherever it is
> used.**

#### 5.5.7a Is there a measurable *cost* to coda omission? No evidence, either way

*Added 2026-08-08 by a second, targeted pass. The user's condition for adding
syllable-final consonant work to the syllabus was that it be **proved helpful for
reaching the top of the IELTS scale**. §5.5.7a–§5.5.7c are that test. It failed.
Everything in them is **[S]**; the difficulty findings above are untouched.*

**No study, for any L1, at any proficiency, relates coda *deletion* to an IELTS
band, a pronunciation score, a comprehensibility rating, or transcription
intelligibility.** Roughly twelve query formulations were run across OpenAlex
full text, Semantic Scholar, Crossref and general web search. Nothing was found.
**[S]**

**Why the functional-load literature does not fill the gap.** The functional-load
tradition — Catford (1987), Brown (1988), and its L2 testing applications
(Munro & Derwing 2006, cited here **second-hand**; Suzukida & Saito 2019,
N = 40 + 40) — ranks **phonemic substitutions**: how much a listener loses when
one contrast collapses into another. **A deletion is not a contrast, so it has no
functional-load rank at all.** Suzukida & Saito's full text contains **zero**
instances of "deletion", "omission", "coda", "word-final" or "cluster". **[S]**
The FL literature is therefore not silent on codas by oversight; codas are
outside what it measures.

**The one study that ranks errors by *position* points away from coda priority.**
Bent, Bradlow & Smith (2007), in *Language Learning & Language Teaching* 17:

> "errors in word-initial position are more detrimental to intelligibility than
> errors in other positions" **[S]**

and in the same study **vowel** accuracy — not consonant accuracy — correlated
with intelligibility. **Sample size and L1 were not obtainable**, so this is
**directional only** and must never be quoted as a magnitude. It is nonetheless
the empirical shape sitting behind the Lingua Franca Core's permission to omit
final clusters, already noted in §5.4 — a permission this file still declines to
adopt, but now for a *different and weaker* reason than it gave there.

> **GAP** — not established: **any cost of coda omission.** No study, any L1, any
> level, relates coda deletion to a band, a pronunciation score, a
> comprehensibility rating or a transcription-intelligibility measure. The
> difficulty of coda obstruents for Vietnamese learners is well evidenced
> (§5.5.1–§5.5.2); **what it costs the learner is not evidenced at all.** Do not
> assert a cost, and do not assert the absence of one.

#### 5.5.7b Is it trainable into spontaneous speech? The evidence points the wrong way

This is the decisive gate, because a syllabus item has to survive not just
"is it hard" but "does teaching it change what the learner does when not being
taught".

**Saito & Plonsky (2019), *Language Learning* 69(3)** — meta-analysis of **77
studies** of instructed L2 pronunciation:

> "The 95% CIs for all three subgroups involving spontaneous speech cross zero"
> **[S]**

> "the effectiveness of pronunciation teaching could be limited to L2 learners'
> acquisition of specific segmental and suprasegmental features at a controlled
> speech level" **[S]**

The between-group figures, which must travel with the claim: **[S]**

| Outcome | Controlled speech | **Spontaneous speech** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expert rating | d = **0.75** [0.42, 1.07] | d = **0.40 [−0.44, 1.26]** |
| Acoustic measure | d = **0.84** [0.45, 1.24] | d = **0.24 [−0.22, 0.71]** |

**Lee, Jang & Plonsky (2014), *Applied Linguistics* 36(3)**, an earlier
meta-analysis over **86 reports**, found the same asymmetry: larger effects for
"more controlled outcome measures". **[S]** Two independent meta-analyses, same
direction.

**The only Vietnamese intervention study with a spontaneous-speech outcome found
null.** Chau, Huensch, Hoang & Chau (2022), *TESL-EJ* 25(4): **N = 30**
Vietnamese adult EFL learners (elementary to low-intermediate), **32 hours over 8
weeks**, segmental versus suprasegmental instruction, pre/post **monologues**
rated by **11 native-speaker raters**.

> No effect of Time, **F(1,28) = 1.41, p = .25**; "**Strong to decisive evidence
> for the null hypothesis** … Time (BF₁₀ = .05), Group (BF₁₀ < .01), Time × Group
> (BF₁₀ = .02)" **[S]**

**Caveats that must travel with this and must never be dropped:** **no control
group**; **N = 30**; beginners, not the population this file's other Vietnamese
studies describe; a probable **ceiling effect** (mean intelligibility already
81.7% at pre-test); and the outcome is **global intelligibility, not coda
accuracy**. **It does not show that coda instruction fails.** It shows that a
comprehensive segmental course did not move a global spontaneous measure.

> **GAP** — not established: **whether coda-targeted instruction transfers to
> spontaneous speech.** No intervention study targeting syllable-final consonants
> with a control group and a spontaneous-speech outcome was found, for Vietnamese
> or for any L1. The general pronunciation-instruction meta-analyses find
> controlled-speech gains and confidence intervals crossing zero for spontaneous
> speech; the one Vietnamese trial is uncontrolled and null. **Neither the claim
> that coda instruction works nor the claim that it fails is asserted here.**

> **GAP** — not established: **whether ~14 is an appropriate age for coda
> instruction.** No study addresses whether early adolescence is a better or worse
> window for it. The critical-period literature (Long 1990; DeKeyser 2000;
> Muñoz 2008) concerns **age of onset and ultimate attainment in naturalistic
> acquisition**; it does **not** transfer to a classroom-syllabus decision and
> **must not be used to fill this gap.**

#### 5.5.7c The cross-criterion attribution question — a real, unexamined gap

The most plausible remaining argument for coda work is *cross-criterion*: that an
inaudible inflectional `-s` is heard by an examiner as a **grammar** error rather
than a pronunciation one, so the phonological problem is paid for on the
Grammatical Range & Accuracy scale. **No peer-reviewed study tests this.** About
twelve query formulations across OpenAlex full text, Semantic Scholar, Crossref
and web search returned nothing. **[S]** It is a real and publishable gap, not a
retrieval failure this file can route around.

**One strong indirect finding exists.** Kang & Yan (2018), *Journal of Language
Testing & Assessment* 1(1), on a Cambridge PET/FCE/CAE/CPE speaking corpus.
**Sample size is internally inconsistent in the paper — 106 candidates per the
abstract, 118 files per the method section; flag it wherever this is cited.** Ten
L1s, **no Vietnamese**; two coders, ICC ≥ .89. Three things follow: **[S]**

1. **Grammar was coded off orthographic transcripts verified against audio.** So
   an **inaudible plural becomes a grammar error by construction** — the coding
   scheme cannot do otherwise. This is evidence about **coding practice, not
   about examiner perception**, and the study never discusses the point. Do not
   present it as showing what examiners do.
2. **Singular/plural errors are the highest-loading feature of grammatical
   accuracy** in that study — loading **.55**, ahead of articles at **.44**.
3. "Speaking performance at the C1 and C2 levels were distinguished on three
   rating criteria **except pronunciation**", with consonant errors giving the
   Tukey pattern `1>234; 2>4` — i.e. **C1 and C2 do not differ on pronunciation
   at all.** Whatever separates the top two levels, segmental consonant accuracy
   is not it.

**The counter-direction is already in this knowledge base, and it is the one that
is demonstrated.** Isaacs et al. (2015) found grammatical accuracy loading
**.945 on the *Pronunciation* scale** (§5.2). Cross-criterion bleed is real —
but the demonstrated direction is **grammar contaminating pronunciation**, not
pronunciation contaminating grammar.

**And attribution may be undecidable in principle.** Disney & Le, on their own
data: "it would be impossible to ascertain a phonological or morphological cause
for a given case of C+ morphological {-s} elision." **[S]** (§5.5.2)

> **GAP** — not established, and **not examined by anyone**: whether listeners or
> examiners attribute an omitted inflectional ending to **grammar** rather than to
> **pronunciation**. No peer-reviewed study tests it. Kang & Yan's coding
> practice makes an inaudible plural a grammar error *by construction*, which is
> a fact about a research method, not about raters. Isaacs et al. demonstrate
> bleed in the **opposite** direction. Nothing in this knowledge base may assert
> that coda work buys a grammar-scale improvement.

#### 5.5.7d Mis-citation warnings, and the three studies that would settle this

**Four citations that look Vietnamese or look load-bearing and are not.** Record
these so they are not silently re-derived: **[S]**

| Citation | What it actually is |
| --- | --- |
| Hansen (2001), *Applied Linguistics* 22(3), on English syllable codas | **Mandarin** speakers, **N = 3**. **Not Vietnamese** |
| Hansen Edwards (2011), on /t, d/ deletion | **7 Mandarin** speakers. **Not Vietnamese** |
| Osburne (1996), *Applied Linguistics* 17(2) | **Is** Vietnamese, but **N = 1**, descriptive, with **no cost and no training data**. Already listed in this file's source table at that strength |
| Suzukida & Saito (2019), functional load | Ranks **phonemic substitutions**; contains no treatment of deletion, omission, codas, word-final position or clusters |

**The three studies that would settle the question.** Recorded so that a future
pass knows what to look for rather than re-running the same searches:

1. **An attribution experiment.** Resynthesise the `-s` within a single speaker's
   utterances (present / absent, everything else held constant), have raters score
   **Pronunciation and Grammar separately**, and measure which scale moves. This
   is the study that does not exist (§5.5.7c).
2. **A cost study.** Relate measured coda-omission rate to band, or to
   comprehensibility, in **Vietnamese B2–C1 speakers** — the population Disney &
   Le already sampled. This is the study that would close §5.5.7a.
3. **A coda-targeted intervention** with a **control group** and a
   **spontaneous-speech outcome**. This is the study that would close §5.5.7b,
   and the only one of the three that could satisfy the user's original condition.

- **No unit targets syllable-final consonants** — U1–U4 are vowels, U5 is /n/–/ŋ/

## 6. CEFR anchoring

The plan's requirement: "published IELTS↔CEFR alignments, so every lesson can
carry an honest 'this trains toward X' label." A guessed mapping would poison
every lesson downstream, so this section is deliberately conservative about
what it will and will not assert.

### 6.1 What the official sources say in prose

IELTS' own *Teacher's guide to IELTS* (official PDF, held in the research cache)
publishes a CEFR comparison diagram and frames it carefully:

> "The four skills of listening, reading, writing and speaking tested in IELTS
> and the four areas of reception, production, interaction and mediation
> described in the CEFR overlap rather than match, as do the nine IELTS bands
> and the six CEFR levels. However, our research shows this diagram provides
> useful comparisons." **[S]**

> "IELTS and the CEFR are designed for different purposes and view language
> proficiency from different perspectives." **[S]**

Cambridge English's *Comparing scores to IELTS* makes two explicit statements:

> "Candidates who have secured a C1 Advanced grade C, having scored between 180
> and 192 on the Cambridge English Scale, are at Level C1 of the CEFR and can be
> expected to be comparable in ability with candidates who have secured 6.5 or
> 7.0 in IELTS."
> ([Cambridge English](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/461626-cambridge-english-qualifications-comparing-scores-to-ielts.pdf)) **[S]**

> "Candidates who have secured scores of 160 to 179 on C1 Advanced are placed at
> Level B2 and may be expected to be comparable to candidates who have secured
> 5.5 or 6.0 in IELTS." **[S]**

And its methodological warning, which must travel with any crosswalk:

> "Exact equivalences cannot always be demonstrated, only broad comparability.
> … It is better not to rely on a single source of evidence but to build up a
> cumulative case based on a range of data." **[S]**

The alignment behind those statements is not casual: an equipercentile linking
study with counterbalanced design (IELTS candidates took C1 Advanced and vice
versa), plus a standard-setting study led by Chad Buckendahl with **19
panellists** using a modified Analytical Judgment method for Speaking/Writing and
the Yes/No Angoff variation for Listening/Reading. "Classification consistency
between the two exams on the three levels and across the four skills averaged
80%." **[S]**

### 6.2 The crosswalk this project may use

Cambridge's *Comparing scores to IELTS* also publishes an IELTS-band ↔ Cambridge
English Scale table, and states the CEFR boundaries on that Scale in prose
(B2 = 160–179; C1 = 180 and above). Combining the two — **an arithmetic
derivation from one Tier-1 document, not a statement that document makes** —
gives:

| IELTS band | Cambridge English Scale **[S]** | CEFR level (derived) | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5.0 | 154 | B1 (below the 160 B2 floor) | **derived** |
| 5.5 | 162 | B2 | **derived**, and matches Cambridge's prose |
| 6.0 | 169 | B2 | **derived**, and matches Cambridge's prose |
| 6.5 | 176 | B2 by the Scale; Cambridge's prose pairs 6.5 with C1 | **conflicted — see note** |
| 7.0 | 185 | C1 | **derived**, and matches Cambridge's prose |
| 7.5 | 191 | C1 | **derived** |

**The 6.5 conflict is real and should not be smoothed over.** The Scale table
puts 6.5 at 176, inside the B2 range; the prose pairs 6.5 with C1 Advanced grade
C. Band 6.5 sits on the B2/C1 border. Any lesson label at that level should say
"B2/C1", not pick one.

### 6.2a The floor of the official alignment is band 4.0 = B1 — and there is nothing below it

*Added 2026-08-08. This closes part (b) of the gap this section previously
recorded. The answer is a negative one, and it is the more useful answer: **no
official source maps any IELTS band score to CEFR A2 or A1.** All markers in this
subsection are **[S]** — quoted from primary official documents retrieved and
held, not put through the verification panel.*

The Cambridge conversion table above stops at 5.0. That is a property of *that
table*, not the floor of the official alignment. Three further Tier-1 sources
locate the actual floor, and all three put it in the same place: **IELTS band
4.0, at the bottom of B1.**

The cleanest evidence is textual rather than diagrammatic, and it comes from the
one official body with an operational need to certify at A1 and A2. The UK Home
Office's list of approved Secure English Language Tests assigns the 9-band test
to four CEFR levels and stops:

| CEFR | Type | Approved English language test | Minimum grade required |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | 2-facet | IELTS Life Skills – A1 Speaking & Listening | **Pass** |
| A2 | 2-facet | IELTS Life Skills – A2 Speaking and Listening | **Pass** |
| B1 | 2-facet | IELTS Life Skills – B1 Speaking & Listening | **Pass** |
| B1 | 4-facet | **IELTS for UKVI** | Listening: 4.0; Speaking: 4.0; Reading: 4.0; Writing: 4.0 |
| B2 | 4-facet | **IELTS for UKVI** | 5.5 in each of the four skills |
| C1 | 4-facet | **IELTS for UKVI** | 7.0 in each of the four skills |
| C2 | 4-facet | **IELTS for UKVI** | 8.5 in each of the four skills |

([UK Home Office, *List of approved Secure English Language Tests taken outside
the UK*](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/guidance-on-applying-for-uk-visa-approved-english-language-tests),
rows for the IELTS SELT Consortium, transcribed verbatim from the published
spreadsheet) **[S]**

**IELTS for UKVI has no A1 row and no A2 row.** Where A1 or A2 must be certified,
the system does not extend the band scale downward — it *switches instrument*, to
IELTS Life Skills, which is not banded at all:

> "Unlike our other tests, IELTS for UKVI Life Skills is not scored. Instead, you
> will receive either a Pass or a Fail result depending on how you performed."
> ([ielts.org](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-tests-for-uk-visas-and-immigration/ielts-life-skills-test)) **[S]**

There is no published conversion from Life Skills to the 9-band scale because
there is no score to convert. That is the structural reason the crosswalk has no
sub-B1 rung, and it is worth more to this project than an invented one would be.

Cambridge's current CEFR diagram agrees. Its IELTS column is floored at Cambridge
English Scale 140 — the A2/B1 boundary — with A2 Key, A1 Movers and Pre A1
Starters occupying the rows below and IELTS absent from all of them. The
document's own footnote explains the status of the mapping:

> "*IELTS and young learners are mapped to, but do not report on the Cambridge
> English Scale."
> ([Cambridge English, *CEFR diagram*](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/126130-cefr-diagram.pdf), file version `CEFR_diagram_update_0625`) **[S]**

And ielts.org's own page says, in prose, why a sub-B1 rung should not be expected
and why even the published rungs are approximate:

> "there will not be a one-to-one correspondence between IELTS scores and CEFR
> levels"

> "They should not be interpreted as reflecting strong claims about exact
> equivalence"

> "As IELTS preceded the CEFR, IELTS band scores have never aligned exactly with
> the CEFR transition points."
> ([ielts.org, IELTS and the CEFR](https://ielts.org/organisations/ielts-for-organisations/compare-ielts/ielts-and-the-cefr)) **[S]**

**Two consequences for this project.**

1. **The A2/B1 boundary on the IELTS scale is now anchored**, from Tier 1: band
   4.0 in each skill is the B1 threshold. §8.4's labelling rule can cite that.
2. **A2 has no IELTS band, officially, at all** — and A2 is where most grade-8
   learners sit. §8.4's rule therefore stands unchanged and is now *positively*
   justified rather than justified by absence of evidence: label by CEFR
   descriptor, because for the level our learners occupy there is no band to
   label with.

Note also what the Cambridge linking study did *not* cover. The same Cambridge
document states its method was to use "linear interpolation … to determine IELTS
raw marks that corresponded to CEFR Levels B2, C1 and C2 on each of the four
skills" **[S]** — the standard-setting work behind the crosswalk never targeted
B1 or below.

> **GAP** — still not established: (a) the exact CEFR cut points as text on
> ielts.org's own comparison figure — the figure is a raster image, and while its
> lowest band label is 4 and its A1/A2 rows are visibly empty, that reading is
> derived from the image rather than from published text, so it is recorded here
> as corroboration of the Home Office and Cambridge documents and not as an
> independent source; (b) whether British Council publishes its own band↔CEFR
> table — `takeielts.britishcouncil.org` was unreachable across repeated attempts
> in this pass, so its floor is unknown; (c) whether bands 8.0–9.0 map to C1 or
> C2, beyond ielts.org's FAQ statement that "Band scores of 8.5 and higher are
> recognised as C2. Band 8 is borderline" **[S]**.
>
> **What is no longer a gap:** whether an official sub-B1 alignment exists. It
> does not. Any A1/A2 band figures in circulation — including a table on an IDP
> *marketing* page giving A1 = 2.0–3.0 and A2 = 3.0–3.5 — are not supported by
> any official technical or validation document, contradict the ielts.org figure,
> and must not be used. **Do not label a lesson "IELTS band 3 = A2".** No such
> mapping is published, and this pass looked for it specifically.

One third-party usage, for information only and not as authority: Naismith &
Juffs describe their IELTS band 4, 6.5, and 8 base texts as corresponding "to
CEFR levels of B1, B2, and C1, respectively." **[S]** That is a researcher's
working assumption in a Tier-2 paper, consistent with the derivation above at 6.5
but placing band 8 at C1.

### 6.3 What we can label honestly, today

Because the official IELTS-side alignment **bottoms out at band 4.0 = the B1
threshold and assigns no band at all to A2 or A1** (§6.2a), **lesson labels
should be CEFR-anchored, not IELTS-band-anchored.** The CEFR side is fully sourced: the
Companion Volume gives A2/B1/B2 descriptors for General linguistic range,
Vocabulary range, Vocabulary control, Grammatical accuracy, and Phonological
control, and the Oxford 3000 tags every word A1–B2.

The relevant CEFR rungs for a grade-8 course
([CEFR Companion Volume 2020](https://rm.coe.int/common-european-framework-of-reference-for-languages-learning-teaching/16809ea0d4)) **[S]**:

| Scale | A2 | B1 (the target) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General linguistic range | "Has a repertoire of basic language which enables them to deal with everyday situations with predictable content, though they will generally have to compromise the message and search for words… Can use basic sentence patterns and communicate with memorised phrases…" | "Has a sufficient range of language to describe unpredictable situations, explain the main points in an idea or problem with reasonable precision and express thoughts on abstract or cultural topics…" |

Note the CEFR's framing of range, which reads as a direct instruction to this
project:

> "the primary evidence for second language acquisition (that is, progress) is
> the emergence of new forms and not their mastery … When learners are tackling
> more complex tasks, their control of their language naturally suffers, and
> this is a healthy process." **[S]**

## 7. What the descriptors and the research agree on

A short list, because it is what survives the contested parts:

1. **Flexibility beats complexity.** Descriptors say it at every band; Banerjee's
   complexity measures peak at band 7 and fall at band 8.
2. **Accuracy on a few specific structures tracks the whole band range** —
   third-person -s, passives, determiners.
3. **Coverage is the vocabulary constraint**, and the curve flattens after
   ~4,000 families; topic pre-teaching is worth ~1,000 families per text.
4. **Academic vocabulary is not a later stage.**
5. **Suprasegmentals discriminate the upper pronunciation bands; segmentals are
   a floor.** — *but see 8: for this learner population the floor is the
   binding constraint.*
6. **Progress is not monotonic** at the A2→B1 transition; error rates rise as
   range grows.
7. **The CEFR is the honest label; the IELTS band is not** — because the
   official alignment stops at band 4.0 = B1 and nothing is published for A2 or
   A1 (§6.2a).
8. **For Vietnamese learners specifically, the highest-value pronunciation
   target is the one Isaacs ranks lowest**: syllable-final obstruents, still
   omitted 28.4% of the time at C1. Isaacs measured what separates band 7 from
   band 8; grade-8 learners are nowhere near that question (§5.5.8).

#### 8.1a The ranking function — specification

*Added 2026-08-08. This replaces the "**Do not implement one yet**" block this
section carried until §3 was resolved. All of it is **[S]**. It is a
specification, not a licence to extend: anything not named here stays out.*

**Stage 1 — eligibility gate (a gate, not a ranking).** Nation's coverage figures
decide *which* items are admissible at all: **K1–K4 first**, in that order, on the
coverage argument already in §2.1 and row 3 above. Oxford 3000/5000 CEFR tags are
used **for labelling** the item, never for ordering it. An item outside the
eligible band is simply not in the pool; it is not ranked low.

**Stage 2 — ranking within the eligible set.** Score each eligible item by the
**association strength of the strongest collocation it participates in**:

```
value(item) = max over collocations c containing item of MI(c)
  subject to   n(c) > 5   and   t(c) > 2
  tie-break    dobj  >  amod  >  advmod
```

- **`MI > 3` is the conventional threshold** for treating a pair as a collocation
  at all (Hunston; Durrant & Schmitt; Granger & Bestgen; Paquot; Naismith &
  Juffs). **[S]**
- **`n > 5`, `MI > 3`, `t > 2`** together are **Naismith & Juffs' own COCA
  filter**, adopted whole rather than re-invented. **[S]**
- The **dependency tie-break is Paquot's**, and follows her effect sizes directly:
  dobj η² = .154 > advmod .118 > amod .106, with dobj also carrying the only
  significant C2–C1 step (0.40, p = .005). **[S]**

**Teach the item as a collocation, not as a word.** This is the single least
contested decision in the whole area (§8.1 row 8).

**Store as attributes, never as a ranking key.** These are what the item record
holds so that the policy can change without a re-import:

| Attribute | Note |
| --- | --- |
| lemma + POS | |
| audio form | Mandatory — §8.1 row 6, **F3** |
| frequency band K1–K16 | **Attribute only.** Ranking by it is explicitly not supported |
| CEFR tag | Labelling only |
| AWL / AVL flag | Tag, never a stage gate (§8.1 row 2) |
| top collocates, each with **n, MI, t-score, dependency relation, and an MI ≥ 3 flag** | The ranking input |

**Leave swappable** — these are engineering choices this evidence base does not
settle, and each must be a configurable parameter rather than a constant baked
into the scorer:

1. **The weight between MI strength and headword frequency band.** Default
   **MI-dominant**, on §3.0. Nothing establishes the exchange rate.
2. **Anything touching accuracy** — because §3.5 is unresolved.
3. **The reference corpus.** Paquot showed MI values depend on corpus choice and
   **rejected the BNC in favour of a genre-matched corpus**. **A grade-8 trainer
   must not silently inherit an academic-corpus MI table.** **[S]**

**Explicitly NOT supported by the evidence** — each of these was checked and each
fails:

| Ranking proposal | Why it fails |
| --- | --- |
| By **raw frequency band** | Word-level frequency was **the only n.s. variable** in Saito 2020 and is non-monotonic in Banerjee (§3.2's trough-then-rise table) |
| By **CEFR level** | Paquot: "no measure of lexical complexity distinguishes with statistical significance between proficiency levels" across B2/C1/C2 |
| By **AWL or collocation-list membership** | Paquot found academic-collocation-list membership **n.s.**; and §2.3 already establishes that most of the AWL sits inside the top 4,000 COCA lemmas, so the list is largely a high-frequency list |
| By **diversity** (type–token style measures over phrases) | Phraseological diversity was **n.s. and declining** with level in Paquot |
| An **accuracy-scoring module** | §3.5 **GAP**; `09` **F1** |

**Two caveats travel with every use of this specification**, and a tool that
implements it must surface them in its own documentation:

- **§3.6 GAP (i)** — no intervention study shows that *teaching* high-MI
  collocations raises a rating. This ranks on a well-evidenced **correlate**.
- **§3.6 GAP (iv)** — every MI finding comes from **B2-and-above** populations.
  Applying it at A2/B1 is an **extrapolation**.

### 8.3 Pronunciation tooling

| Decision | Recommendation | Grounded in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Keep the U1–U12 order** | Segmentals → clusters → suprasegmentals matches both the descriptor architecture (segmentals named at bands 4/6, absent at 8/9) and the discriminant loadings (chunking .745, intonation .735 > word stress .668 > segmentals .551). | Band descriptors **[S]**; Isaacs et al., N=80 **[S]** |
| **Invest most in U9–U12** | Chunking and intonation are where upper-band discrimination lives, and prosodic training transfers to spontaneous speech where segmental training does not. These units need real audio. | Isaacs **[S]**; Derwing & Rossiter 2003 via Dauer **[S]** |
| **Score intelligibility, not accent** | Self-review prompts should ask "could a listener follow this without effort?" — the descriptor language is about *effect on intelligibility*, and the CEFR explicitly rejects accent-as-deficit. | Band descriptors **[S]**; CEFR CV **[S]** |
| **No band prediction from pronunciation features** | Discriminant classification accuracy was 47%, with band 6 misclassified 80% of the time. Examiners themselves conflate bands 6 and 7. | Isaacs **[S]**; Yates et al. **[S]** |
| **Vietnamese-specific guidance** | **Unblocked as of 2026-08-08** (§5.5), with limits. Ship the four targets below; ship nothing else Vietnamese-specific. | §5.5, all **[S]** |
| **VN-1 — final consonants, especially coda /s~z/** | **Best-evidenced *difficulty* in the document — not a demonstrated payoff** (amended 2026-08-08: §5.5.7a–c found no cost evidence and no spontaneous-speech trainability evidence, so **no syllabus change is recommended**; the syllabus still has no unit for it and that is now recorded as an observation, not a request). Where this is taught at all, drill coda obstruents in content words, not function words — the six commonest /s~z/ function words carry an 11% error rate while removing them raises the overall rate to 41%. (p = .519) **[S]** |
| **VN-2 — onset-cluster voicing/aspiration** | Teach U6 /br/–/pr/ and U7 /bl/–/kl/ explicitly as **voicing-and-aspiration** contrasts, not as "clusters". Feature change is 77.4% of two-consonant onset errors; deletion is only 22.2%, and when it happens it removes the plosive, not the liquid. | Tran & Nguyen 2022, N=36 **[S]** |
| **VN-3 — deaccenting and question tunes** | The one place the Vietnamese evidence and Isaacs' loadings both point the same way. Prioritise U11–U12 audio over U9–U10 audio if only one can be resourced. Target the specific errors found: rise-overuse on Wh- and tag questions, and failure to deaccent. | Nguyễn & Đào 2018, N=30 (20 Southern) **[S]**; Isaacs intonation .735 **[S]** |
| **VN-4 — what NOT to claim** | Do not justify word-stress teaching by Vietnamese tone transfer (the premise is contested — Brunelle 2017, N=18). Do not ship Vietnamese-specific **vowel** guidance (no qualifying source). Do not ship region-specific (North/South) guidance. Do not predict errors by contrasting the two inventories. | GAPs §5.5.4–§5.5.6; Disney & Le's own anti-contrastive conclusion **[S]** |

### 8.4 Labelling rule for every lesson

**Label lessons with CEFR levels, sourced to the Companion Volume descriptor for
the relevant scale — not with IELTS bands.** This rule was previously justified by
*absence* of evidence. As of 2026-08-08 it is justified positively (§6.2a): the
official IELTS↔CEFR alignment **bottoms out at band 4.0 = the B1 threshold**, and
**no official source assigns any band score to A2 or A1.** Where A1/A2 must be
certified, the IELTS system uses a different, unscored Pass/Fail instrument. Most
grade-8 learners are at A2. **There is therefore no band to label them with, and
none can be derived.**

A lesson may say "trains toward CEFR B1 Grammatical accuracy". It may say "IELTS
band 4.0 in a skill is the published B1 threshold" and cite the Home Office SELT
list. It may **not** say "trains toward IELTS band 4.5", and it may **not** say
"IELTS band 3 = A2" — that mapping does not exist in any official document.

### Not sustained by the verification panel

Sixteen claims failed the 3-vote panel. **A failed claim is unproven, not
disproven.** Where the primary document was later found in the research cache
and states the claim verbatim, this file uses it under the **[S/NS]** marker with
the source quoted — that applies to the Milton vocabulary-size claims, the
Gardner & Davies AVL coverage figures, the Oxford list-construction criteria, the
Read & Nation speaking-lexis findings, and the Banerjee complexity, lexical
sophistication, and grammatical accuracy findings. Their failure at the panel
reflects unreachable sources, not contrary evidence.

Two failures **are** consequential for the plan and are recorded neutrally:

- **The plan assumed "Lexical Resource band 9 means precision and collocation,
  not rare words."** A confirmed experimental finding (Naismith & Juffs) shows
  rarity *causally* raises examiner ratings, and the collocational-*accuracy*
  claim errored at the panel (0-2). The assumption is not established. See §3.
  *Updated 2026-08-08:* §3 now shows the "not rare words" half of the assumption
  rested on a **false opposition** — a formulaic sequence *is* a rare item,
  measured on combinations rather than on words. The panel result is unchanged
  and no marker is upgraded; what changed is that the two sides are no longer in
  conflict. The **accuracy** half remains open (§3.5).
- **The claim that a 5,000-family vocabulary reaches 98% coverage of IELTS
  Listening** failed 0-3 and no verbatim text was recoverable. Do not use it.
