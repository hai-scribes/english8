# What makes Doraemon work — a craft foundation

A maintainer's document. It exists so that when we write **our own** serialized
story — a second-hand robot cat and a poor boy, told across the twelve units —
we are rebuilding a machine we understand rather than imitating a surface we
liked. Nothing in this file is published. Nothing in it goes on a learner's
page.

It is a study of **technique**, which is the part of a work copyright does not
reach. It is deliberately not a store of characters, names or plots to reuse —
see §9, which is the reason that distinction is drawn this sharply.

## 1. The finding that reorganises everything: there are two engines, not one

Doraemon is not one kind of story told 1,300 times. It is **two formats with
opposite laws**, kept deliberately separate, and the franchise runs both.

**Engine A — the short chapter.** Episodic **by default**: the reset is the
rule, and §2's counterexamples are the rare, deliberate exceptions. Nobita does
not change *across* chapters. This is where the comedy, the gadgets and the
character work live.

**Engine B — the long story** (大長編ドラえもん, the annual film manga, from
*Nobita's Dinosaur*, 1980). Continuous. A journey with a destination, an
ending, and consequences that stick. Wikipedia classifies these as
action-adventure and drama against the parent series' gag comedy, and describes
them as "whole volumes of longer and continuous narratives … unlike the regular
Doraemon series which is merely compilations of various self-contained
shorts." `[src]`

**Engine A cannot produce what we were asked for**, and its author said so
explicitly. Fujimoto Hiroshi, quoted in Schilling 1997 via Marshall 2016:

> "When a manga hero becomes a success, the manga suddenly stops being
> interesting," said Fujimoto. "So the hero has to be like the stripes on a
> barber pole; he seems to keep moving upward, but actually he stays in the
> same place." `[src]`

That is the reset defended by the person who built it. Marshall names its cost
without flinching: "Doraemon fails to change Nobita at all." `[src]` What the
reset *buys* is equally clear and is exactly what a self-study course needs:
entry at any point, no required reading order, permanent availability. `[src]`

**So the design question for us is not "which engine" — it is how to run both at
once.** A learner opening Unit 7 alone must not be lost; a learner going 1→12
must arrive somewhere.

The franchise's own answer is instructive but **not available to us**: it does
not blend the engines, it **quarantines** the continuity in physically separate
works. Payoff lives in the films and in a small number of designated chapters;
the daily material stays resettable. `[src]` for the film claim, `[inf]` for the
generalisation. We are proposing to put both engines inside the *same* 84
lessons, which is a different architecture, and neither Fujiko nor this file
supplies it.

> ### This file does not solve the serialization problem
>
> Read that as a finding, not a disclaimer. §10 establishes that the container
> holds enough prose and that the prescribed topics run in a suggestive order.
> **Neither of those is narrative capacity.** Topic order supplies no causality,
> no character memory, and no exposition for a learner who skipped Unit 5. The
> souvenir device in §3 supplies resonance for an informed reader; it cannot
> make an ongoing causal plot intelligible to someone who missed the cause.
>
> What is still missing is a **serialization protocol** — a written rule for
> what each unit may assume, must recap, may resolve, and must defer, and a
> recap mechanism that survives the constraint in §10 that a listening script
> may not be printed anywhere in the lesson. That protocol is the first thing
> to design before any prose is written, and it is design work, not research.
> It is question 1 in §11.

## 2. Engine A — how one chapter is built

The formula is documented, in a peer-reviewed source, as formulaic. Marshall
(*Anthropology & Aging* 37:1, 2016, p. 31):

> "Episode plots, the syntagmatic structures of structural analysis, are
> formulaic." `[src]`

He then quotes a young fan he calls Mijea — **this sentence is the fan's
characterisation, not the paper's own analysis**, and the distinction matters
because rule 1 below leans on it:

> "so many hundreds of stories start off with Nobita running home in tears,
> crying 'Doraemon! Do something!'." `[src]`

And continues in his own voice:

> "Nobita tries to get Doraemon to fish a gadget from the future out of his
> pouch to solve his problem. **Doraemon resists but finally yields.** The
> gadget performs as required, but then unintended and unforeseen consequences
> ensue, making matters worse, but funny. Nobita seems to have learned his
> lesson by the end, but tomorrow's episode reveals that he has not." `[src]`

Restated as six rules a writer can follow:

1. **Open in defeat, not in setup.** The chapter begins with the boy already
   humiliated — mid-tears, running home. The problem is arrived at, not
   introduced. `[src]` — but note this rests on the *fan's* sentence above, not
   on the paper's own analysis. One rung weaker than rules 2, 3 and 6.

   **And demote it further: this is *one common* opening, not *the* opening.**
   The single chapter we could reconstruct at beat level — "Gian Stew" — opens
   on the **bully's announcement**, with the protagonist merely present. `[src]`
   A template that hard-codes tears-on-arrival is over-fitted. Vary it.

   Same chapter, second correction, and a more useful one: **the gadget arrives
   at beat 5 of 9 — the midpoint**, after four beats of ordinary social and
   domestic pressure (an unwanted invitation, refusing his mother's dinner,
   being scolded for it). The problem is fully installed before the magic
   touches it. `[src]` For a language course that is a gift: the first four
   beats are pure A2 daily-life language carrying no fantasy vocabulary load.

   Third: in that chapter **the complication is not misuse — the gadget is
   *left behind*** at the moment it is needed. `[src]` Absence is an engine too.
   "Gadget → overreach → comeuppance" is one engine among several, not the
   engine.

   All three rest on **n=1**. Three or four more chapters would settle whether
   they generalise, and the method is cheap — see §11.
2. **The appeal is a demand, and it is resisted.** The helper does not
   volunteer; he *resists, then yields*. That beat is load-bearing — it makes
   the gadget a concession, which pre-loads the comeuppance. Skip it and the
   helper is an appliance. `[src]`
3. **The gadget works.** The failure is never a malfunction. Marshall: the
   technology "works as advertised, but **only mechanically, not
   socially**." `[src]`
4. **The turn is the boy's appetite, not the tool's defect.** Revenge on the
   bully, showing off, dodging work — misuse is what converts success into
   escalation. `[snip]`
5. **Escalate into a worse version of the *original* problem**, not into a
   different one. The comeuppance stays thematically continuous with the
   opening humiliation. `[inf]`
6. **End on apparent learning, and quietly withhold it.** `[src]`

### The two-level structure worth stealing

Marshall separates a *minor contradiction* (per chapter: the gadget both solves
and fails to solve the problem) from a *major contradiction* (series-level,
never resolved: Doraemon was sent back to reform Nobita, and by always yielding
he guarantees the reform never happens). His proportion:

> Doraemon's gadgets : Nobita's problems :: Doraemon : Nobita `[src]`

**The helper fails his charge in a similar way to how the gadget fails the
problem.** Marshall hedges this deliberately — "in a similar but not identical
way, Doraemon cannot reform Nobita's character for him," and "Like his own
gadgets, Doraemon is not quite right for the job he has been assigned." `[src]`
Do not tighten that hedge into an identity; the useful reading is that the
episode's joke and the series' premise are *close to* the same shape at two
scales. `[inf]`

Even hedged this is the most reusable craft insight in the file: it means a
well-built chapter is automatically *about* the series, without any narrator
saying so.

### Counterexamples

The formula is broken deliberately and rarely — "Goodbye, Doraemon" (1974),
written as a genuine ending `[snip]`; "The Day of My Birth", sincere rather than
comic, ending in resolve rather than comeuppance `[snip]`. The rule for us: the
formula's authority comes from being kept, so a break has to be an event.

### Pacing

45 tankōbon volumes, 821 collected chapters, 1,300+ short episodes written;
serialisation began December 1969, first volume 31 July 1974, last 26 April
1996. `[src]` — these are Wikipedia's *manga* figures. Marshall separately gives
~1,500 fifteen-minute *TV* episodes as of 2016; the two numbers count different
things and are not in conflict. `[src]` Chapter length is commonly given as 8–16 pages `[snip]` — the
figure did not survive a direct check of the page it was attributed to, so
treat it as unconfirmed. No source gives panel-level beat positions; where the
turn lands is `[inf]` only.

## 3. Engine B — the long-story beat map

Sixteen beats, each confirmed against film plots read this session unless
marked. This is the spine our twelve units will actually be plotted against.

1. **Ordinary world, ordinary humiliation.** The *specific* slight seeds the
   whole adventure. Suneo shows everyone a fossil claw except Nobita, who
   furiously claims he will find a *living* dinosaur. `[src]`
2. **The boy's wrong claim is made literally true.** The helper refuses, then
   relents, and the boast becomes fact. The premise is never imposed on the
   protagonist — it is his own foolishness realised. `[src]`
3. **The inciting intrusion**, arriving in the ordinary world *before* anyone
   leaves it: a tiny alien in the bedroom, an android girl at the door, a
   portal in the bedroom floor. `[src]`
4. **Concealment and domestic comedy** — the anomaly is hidden from adults and
   grows unmanageable. This is where the comic register is *spent*. `[src]` for
   *Dinosaur*, `[inf]` as a general beat.
5. **The fixed party assembles.** Always the same five. `[src]` for *Birth of
   Japan*, `[rec]` for the general rule.
6. **Departure, with a home-side tether that will later be cut** — the time
   machine, the portal, the shrink-ray's timer. **The tether is always the
   thing that breaks.** `[src]`
7. **The guest who needs help.** Every long story has one. `[src]`
8. **The guest's world has a *systemic* wound** — a genocidal flood plan, a
   strip-mining operation, a doomsday computer, a coup. Not a personal
   grievance. `[src]`
9. **Escalation: the children are inside the problem, not visiting it.** They
   lose the ability to leave. `[src]`
10. **The gadgets fail *as the final solution*** — not necessarily at all. See
    §4, where the strong form of this beat does not survive checking. `[src]`
11. **Separation and the dark hour.** Party split, helper down, boy alone.
    `[src]`
12. **The boy's moment of courage — his *mocked* attribute turns decisive.**
    See §6. `[src]`
13. **The guest resolves their own world.** Lilulu reprograms the ancestor
    robots; Pirika's own citizens force the dictator's surrender. Our crew
    enables; the locals decide. `[src]`
14. **A late authority arrives — but only after the children have won.** The
    Time Patrol makes the arrests. A cleanup beat, not a rescue; it lets a
    children's story avoid its heroes executing anyone. `[src]`
15. **Farewell.** Almost never "and they all lived together." The door closes.
    `[src]`
16. **Return, marked.** The reset is not total: Shizuka keeps one screw from
    the destroyed buggy; *Animal Planet* ends with the home world itself
    changed. `[src]`

### How adventures actually begin — 38 films counted

Beats 1–3 above describe *what* the opening does. This is *how often*, from a
classification of 38 of the ~44 released films against their Wikipedia plot
sections. `[src]` for every triggering event; the four-way classification is
ours.

| Trigger | Count | What it means |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| **Accident** — a portal, creature or object arrives with nobody intending it | **18 (47%)** | An eraser thrown at a wall; a father half-asleep at a TV advert; wind scattering test papers; a parcel delivered wrong |
| **Deliberate** — they set out on purpose | 10 (26%) | Running away from home; a planned expedition; a camping trip |
| **Boast** — a foolish claim made literally true | 9 (24%) | "I'll find a *living* dinosaur"; "Heaven is in the clouds" |
| **Summoned** — someone comes asking for help | 1 (3%) | |

**71% of adventures are unsought.** But two refinements matter more than the
tally:

- **Deliberate is 26%, not negligible.** An arc built on pure accident would be
  *more* accidental than the source.
- **The reliable rule is not "accidental start" — it is that the adventure is a
  swerve away from whatever the start was.** `[inf]`, on `[src]` plots. They
  plan a spring expedition and meet a dog who is a prince; they set out for
  treasure and hit real pirates; they go to cool off and find a ring in the ice.
  The intentional act gets them out of the ordinary world; a **second,
  unintended discovery supplies the actual stakes.**

Two more counts worth keeping:

- **The gadget merely equips the journey in 28 of 38; it causes it in 10.**
  `[src]` And when it does cause it, the cause is usually a *misfire or a wish
  taken too literally* — not competent use.
- **Ordinary play escalating into adventure appears 6 times** `[src]` — children
  making an amateur space film when a real alien walks in; building an ice
  playground and finding a ring. The same beat recurs thirty years apart. **This
  is probably the most transplantable single structure in the canon for a
  classroom story.**

No critic or scholar was found who names any of this. That is a real gap, not a
soft one — the two obvious holders (TV Tropes, the Doraemon wiki) are blocked
from this network.

### Rule worlds and place worlds

From a catalogue of the annual long stories. Sort every destination into two
kinds `[inf]`, on a `[src]` catalogue:

- **Place worlds** — prehistory, space, undersea, robot planets, hidden
  kingdoms. **Reused constantly.**
- **Rule worlds** — the same Earth with magic instead of science; a shared
  dream; a storybook entered; a universe the boy creates. **Each used exactly
  once.**

**Places get revisited. Rule-changes do not.** That is where an original arc has
room. `[inf]`

And the cheapest rule world of all is *this world with one thing different* —
the 1984 film keeps the same Tokyo, the same school, the same family, and only
swaps science for magic. `[src]` For a graded reader that is decisive: the
reader already knows the map, so you spend zero words on geography and all of
them on the changed rule, while the recurring vocabulary set stays intact and
compounds across chapters. `[inf]`

One more from the catalogue, for a story that wants to say something: **an
animal civilisation lets you put humans on trial without accusing any human
character** — which is how *Animal Planet* and *Kingdom of Clouds* get an
ecological argument in front of a child without it landing on their own
parents. `[inf]`

### Beat 16 is the mechanism we need most

**The souvenir is the anti-reset device.** It is how an episodic format
accumulates into an arc without requiring the reader to have been present for
every instalment. A learner who skipped Unit 6 can still read Unit 7; a learner
who did both sees the object on the shelf. `[inf]`, built on `[src]` examples.

## 4. Gadget design rules

The CHI 2025 paper "Doraemon's Gadget Lab" coded **379 gadgets** from the
original manga into 10 categories and 33 subcategories. `[src]` Some of what it
found contradicts the popular "gadgets are cautionary tales" reading, which is
what makes it useful.

1. **One gadget, one clear purpose.** "**Many** gadgets have a single, clear
   purpose, operating independently rather than relying on complex multi-step
   interactions." `[src]` — a corpus *tendency*, not a design law; the paper
   says "many", not "all". Turning it into a rule (*a gadget that does two
   things is a lazy gadget, because the chapter can no longer be about the one
   thing*) is ours. `[inf]`
2. **A tangible object with a literal name.** "Anywhere Door." "Memory Bread."
   "Small Light." **The name is the exposition** — no explanation scene
   needed. `[inf]` from the corpus naming pattern.
3. **Immediate, reversible, no irreversible risk.** The paper stresses explicit
   reversibility and built-in safety — the Flattening Iron's effect is undone
   by spraying water, "allowing characters to experiment with powerful tools
   without irreversible risks." `[src]` The reset is engineered into the
   *props*, so the writer never has to contrive an escape.

   **This is an Engine A rule only.** The long stories run on its opposite —
   destroyed transport, incapacitated helpers, erasure, permanent farewells.
   Reversibility is what makes a chapter resettable; irreversibility is what
   makes an arc land. A story running both engines has to decide, per gadget,
   which register it belongs to. `[inf]`
4. **The failure is social, not technical.** A gadget that breaks is a plot
   device; **a gadget that works perfectly and ruins everything is a
   premise.** `[src]`
5. **The best premise is one whose *correct* use is already a temptation** —
   a shortcut around effort the reader also wishes to skip: memorising,
   apologising, being liked. A gadget with no obvious abuse leaves the second
   act nothing to do. `[inf]`
6. **Introduce as: name → one-line rule → demonstration on something trivial →
   abuse at scale.** The demonstration teaches the reader the rule they will
   use to predict the disaster, so the comedy comes from the reader being a
   beat ahead of the boy. `[inf]` — no source states this sequence; it is
   reconstructed from the formula plus the naming and single-purpose findings.

**A worked example** (not "the cleanest in the corpus" — that superlative was
asserted from a blocked page and cannot be established from a search
paraphrase). Memory Bread: press a slice to a page, eat it, memorise the
contents. Nobita eats too many slices, gets a stomach ache, expels them, and
forgets everything printed on them before the test. `[snip]` The gadget never
fails. The body is the limit. Note how much of rule 5 is doing the work — every
reader has wished for exactly this.

> **For a language course this rule set is unusually well aimed.** "One object,
> one literal name, one rule, demonstrated on something small" is also a
> description of how you introduce a grammar point.

### The naming rule, warranted by the official translation

The official English Doraemon (AltJapan for Shogakukan/Voyager) renders gadgets
as **transparent two-word compounds built from A1 words**: *anywhere door*,
*shrink ray*, *magnify ray*, *translation gummy*. Names are aggressively
shortened and stressed — Noby, Sue, Big G, Sneech, D. `[src]`

Two things follow. **The name is the gloss**: a Vietnamese grade-8 learner
decodes the function from the name with no support. And **short stressed names
survive being read aloud** into a listening script. Professional translators
working for a mass children's market converged on exactly the register this
course needs, which means our naming convention is *warranted* rather than
invented.

Shogakukan also publishes a bilingual English edition positioned for junior-high
learners `[snip]` — outside validation that this material sits at our level.

### Magic instead of technology — what actually changes

Relevant because this project swapped the power source. `[inf]` throughout
except where marked.

**The load-bearing property was never that the gadget is technological. It is
that it is *specified*.** The reader is handed a rule in scene one and can
therefore predict the disaster in scene three. Folklore specifies at least as
hard as engineering: ATU 750A ("The Three Wishes") makes the *count* the plot;
*The Monkey's Paw* states its rule on the page and destroys by literalism
`[src]`; even Disney's genie carries four hard exclusions, because an unbounded
wish-granter cannot generate a second act `[src]`. **Magic does not become
"anything can happen." *Unconstrained* magic does.**

What the swap costs, priced honestly:

- **The manufactured layer.** A gadget can be borrowed, stolen, mass-produced,
  obsolete, out of battery, used by the wrong person. That is free plot,
  generated by industry rather than by story. Magic has no factory; every
  equivalent must be invented and then taught to the reader.
- **Neutrality.** A tool has no opinion about the boy. A magical helper has
  intent and therefore moral authority — which quietly converts "boy misuses a
  tool and learns" into "boy is judged by a wise being," a more didactic and
  much less funny machine. **This is the largest single cost.**
- **The teachable compound noun.** "Anywhere door" is free vocabulary. An
  archaic verbal spell is not.

What it buys, and it is the honest reason to do it: **irreversibility.** A flat
battery recharges; a spent boon does not. Episodic Doraemon survives on reset —
a serial does not want reset, and magic is what makes twelve chapters
accumulate.

**And note how Doraemon itself handles magic:** in the two magic-based long
stories, the swap is always *framed by a gadget* — a What-If Box, a storybook
device. The franchise never abandons the specified-mechanism layer; it uses one
gadget to buy a whole magical world, and then uses that world to **de-power the
helper**. In the 1984 film neither hero can use magic well. `[src]` Magic in
Doraemon is not a replacement engine. It is a **handicap device applied to the
existing engine** — which is exactly what a feature-length arc needs and an
episode does not.

### Vietnamese folk helpers — and why Bụt is the find

**Bụt** — the white-bearded old man of *Tấm Cám* and *Cây tre trăm đốt* —
**appears when a wronged child weeps**, asks why they are weeping, and hands
over a rule-bearing object or instruction. In *Tấm Cám* he appears three times,
each triggered by crying. `[src]` In *Cây tre trăm đốt* he gives not a thing but
two words with an exact inverse: **khắc nhập / khắc xuất**. `[src]`

That is beat-for-beat the opening of a Doraemon chapter, in material every
Vietnamese thirteen-year-old learns in primary school. It costs zero setup.

Two cautions. *Bụt* is the vernacular doublet of *Phật* `[snip]` — a religious
word, and a school text should keep its distance. And he is **wise and
correct**, which makes the helper a moral authority rather than a fallible
friend. The move is to **keep the entrance and lose the infallibility**, which
is a departure from Doraemon *and* from Tấm Cám. Flagged honestly: no source
found *any* canonical constraint on Bụt, so that inversion is an invention, not
an extension.

The wider corpus already contains the specification apparatus we need — most
sharply ***Ăn khế trả vàng***, where a helper gives one numeric rule (a bag of
**three spans**) and the disaster is generated purely by greed violating it.
`[src]` A Doraemon episode with Vietnamese furniture, on the primary-school
syllabus.

**On the genie, three corrections to the common picture** `[src]`: Aladdin is
not in any authenticated *Nights* manuscript — Galland took it from Hanna Diyab
in 1709–10; Galland's genie has **no wish limit** at all, and "three wishes" is
a 20th-century Western graft from ATU 750A; and the *folkloric* genie is a
**servant bound to whoever holds the lamp**, which is closer to Doraemon than
the three-wish version is. The real hazard is not copyright in the folk tale —
that is long expired — but that **Disney's genie is the version in every
thirteen-year-old's head**, so an unwary writer drifts toward protected
expression by default.

### How the long stories neutralise the gadgets

A cast with a magic pocket has no stakes. Six techniques, each with a verified
example:

| Technique | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Break the vehicle home** — converts a gadget from a solution into a *destination* | The time machine is damaged; the party must cross the Cretaceous on bamboo-copters `[src]` |
| **Put a timer on it, then make the timer a plot event** | The Small Light's shrinking expires underwater at the worst moment — and returning to giant size mid-execution immediately routs the enemy `[src]` |
| **Out-tech the tech** | A 23rd-century time criminal whose equipment simply outclasses the helper's `[src]` |
| **Wrong tool, wrong scale** | Failure by mismatch, not absence — the psychic controller wrecks buildings; sealing the mirror world only delays the invasion `[src]` |
| **Take the helper out** | Lightning damage; petrification into statues `[src]` |
| **Change the physics of the world** | Magic replaces science, and *neither* hero can use magic — the two incompetents in a world of competents `[src]` |

**The reliable law is weaker than "the gadgets stop working"** — in *Underworld*
the gadgets still function and are used as weapons. What holds across every long
story is:

> **By the climax, the gadget is never what wins.** `[src]` + `[inf]`

## 5. The cast as refillable slots

Stated as *functions*, not as characters, because functions are what we are
allowed to reuse and what we actually need. For each: what the story requires it
for, and what breaks without it.

| Slot | Function | What breaks without it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The finite dispenser** (Doraemon) | Converts an internal deficit into an external, dramatizable object, then makes it misfire so the deficit is still the subject. Framed as "family member and intimate friend rather than servant" `[src]` | The boy cannot act on his own wishes; the series becomes domestic realism with no engine |
| **The unreliable wisher** (Nobita) | The demand side. "Very untrustworthy when handling gadgets, frequently abusing their power" `[src]` — the protagonist is also the story's antagonist-of-consequence | Nothing breaks the gadgets, so there is no second act |
| **The witness who already believes** (Shizuka) | Converts shame into aspiration; supplies a motive that is not revenge; is the audience's licence to like the boy | Every chapter collapses into humiliation with no upward vector |
| **The deadline in human form** (Gian) | Immediate physical pressure — the reason the gadget is needed *today*. Given a compensating absurdity (the terrible singing he believes is great) so he is laughable, and a crisis-mode reversal so he is not disposable `[src]` | Nothing compels the boy to ask for help now |
| **The have, to the protagonist's have-not** (Suneo) | Supplies *envy* — the wound wishes are actually made of. Also physically weak and cowardly, so the hierarchy is multi-axis rather than a ladder. His mechanism is specific: he talks Gian out of hitting *him*, and "in the end, only Nobita was beaten" `[src]` | Threat is only physical; there is nothing to covet |
| **The domestic enforcer** (the mother) | The guaranteed third-act cost. Strict, anxious about household expenses `[src]` | Escapism has no price |
| **The mirror ahead** (the father) | An adult version of the boy who turned out fine-ish — bad grades as a child, real talent he did not pursue, refused a rich man's money because he "wanted to rely on his own efforts" `[src]` | The story's quiet promise goes unstated |
| **The institutional scoreboard** (the teacher) | Makes failure *public and recorded*, and physically carries it from school to home `[src]` | Failure stays private, so it costs nothing |
| **The unhateable superior** (Dekisugi) | Calibrates the inadequacy. Wikipedia states the purpose outright: he "justifies Nobita's jealousy and gadget-seeking schemes" `[src]`. Crucially he is *nice* — the rival cannot be resented, so the failure is entirely the boy's own | Inadequacy has no measure, or curdles into a grudge |
| **The superior substitute who fails at the thing that matters** (Dorami) | A stronger robot — 10,000 horsepower against Doraemon's 129.3 — who "lacks Doraemon's emotional understanding of Nobita" `[src]` | The story never proves that the *relationship*, not the hardware, is load-bearing |
| **The distant beneficiary** (Sewashi) | Stakes without a villain: someone in the future is already paying for today's laziness | The premise has no deadline and therefore no possible ending |

### Two rules extracted from the cast

**The specific-incompetence principle: name the failures and name the talents;
never say "loser."** Nobita's failings are enumerable — lack of physical
ability, procrastination, reluctance to think critically, dislikes books except
manga, locally infamous for sleeping. `[src]` And Wikipedia is explicit that his
grades are "due to his lazy nature as opposed to a lack of intellectual
ability" `[src]` — the failure is moral-neutral, so change stays possible
without insulting the reader who shares the trait.

The counterweight is that **the hidden talents are deliberately worthless**:
marksmanship, cat's cradle, falling asleep instantly. Two whole chapters exist
to prove the pattern, using the What-If Box to build a world where the useless
skill is the world championship, which he wins. `[src]` A hidden *useful* talent
makes the underdog a fraud who was secretly fine all along; a hidden useless
talent makes him a person. `[inf]`

Fujiko F. Fujio supplies the tone himself: **"Nobita is myself"** — same
weakness at sport, same weak will — and what he admired in the character was the
capacity to probe his own personality, name his flaws, and resolve "I have to
grow up." `[src]` Note the reset: the resolve does not stick. That is precisely
what makes it serializable.

**The flawed-helper principle: the helper's power is capped by his own
psychology, not by a rule of magic.** Doraemon is a *defective, cheap,
second-hand robot* — a rat chewed his ears off, he was electrocuted and
crippled "due to an accidental electrocution", he is "deemed an inferior outcast
by his peers", and he was "eventually purchased by the **poverty-stricken Nobi
family**" because his "cheap price as a substandard model" made him
affordable. `[src]` Separately, the descendant who sends him back is himself on
a short budget: "his pocket money was not much due to the debts the family
inherited from Nobita." `[src]` — two different acts on two different pages, the
purchase and the sending; keep them apart when citing. He is also afraid of
mice, obsessed with dorayaki, and distracted by a hopeless crush on a
neighbourhood cat.

The paper we read puts the payoff plainly: these mortal traits "somewhat
diminishes the robot's cold, flawless, and almighty competence. This does not
diminish Doraemon's brilliance but makes his image more approachable." `[src]`

Three consequences, all `[inf]`:

- **A defective helper is a peer, not a parent.** He can be rescued — which is
  the only reversal the format allows, and the one place the useless boy gets
  to be the competent one.
- **He can be wrong, which licenses him to scold.** A perfect helper who scolds
  is a tyrant; a flawed helper who scolds is a friend.
- **Helper and boy share a social position, so the help is not charity from
  above.** This is why the arrangement reads as friendship rather than service.

> **This matters more to us than anything else in the file.** The brief was "a
> cat robot helping a poor boy." The poverty is not our simplification of
> Doraemon — it is *load-bearing in the original*, on both sides of the
> friendship, and it is structure rather than protected expression. It is the
> part we should build on hardest.

## 6. What actually generates feeling

- **The relationship, not the inventory.** Scholars "noted the appeal stems from
  the relationship between Doraemon and Nobita rather than gadget variety
  alone." `[src]`
- **A protected children's space.** Critics praise the depiction of children's
  agency within a domain free of adult interference — "a welcome breath of
  freedom." `[src]` The vacant lot with its stacked concrete pipes is unowned
  and adult-free, which is why the peer hierarchy can play out there with no
  authority to appeal to. `[snip]` + `[inf]`
- **The departure/return motif is the payload.** Episodic comedy accrues
  affection cheaply; the threatened removal cashes all of it at once — and
  because the format resets, the threat can recur. `[inf]` on `[snip]` beats.
  The emotional lever in "Goodbye, Doraemon" is not the boy's grief but his
  father's observation that *the sadder the boy is, the more the leaver
  suffers* — so the task becomes being strong for the one who is going. That is
  what converts a departure into a growth beat rather than a pure loss. `[snip]`
- **Fixed furniture does real work.** A closed circuit of three settings — lot →
  bedroom → dinner table — maps exactly onto problem → gadget → consequence, so
  each setting owns one beat and a short chapter needs no establishing work.
  `[inf]` And the time machine lives in the desk drawer `[src]`: infinity stored
  inside the single most mundane object of a schoolchild's failure. **The
  furniture is the thesis.**
- **For a language course specifically:** fixed settings mean a fixed, recycling
  lexical field, so vocabulary compounds across chapters instead of resetting
  with the plot. `[inf]`

**The engine in one line** `[inf]`: *a boy whose failures are public and
recorded is given, daily, a means of escape that always fails, by a friend who
is also defective, in front of a girl who already likes him and a rival who
cannot be hated — and the only thing that ever actually improves is the
friendship.*

### Ending techniques, catalogued

Every one of these is a way to end without "and they all lived together."

| Technique | Where |
| --- | --- |
| **The loving act and the losing act are the same act** — he tells the creature *not to follow him*, so it can live among its own kind | *Nobita's Dinosaur* `[src]`, `[snip]` for the framing |
| **Erasure by sacrifice** — the character chooses non-existence, then is granted one wordless goodbye | *Steel Troops* `[src]` |
| **Sealing the door** — no villain to blame; the geometry of the worlds simply closes | *Spaceblazer* `[src]` |
| **Bittersweet un-happening** — victory requires deleting part of the adventure | *Birth of Japan* `[src]` |
| **The kept object** — the smallest possible relic stands in for the whole loss | *Undersea Devil* `[src]` |
| **Past kindness returns as testimony** — the ending is won by earlier small mercies, not by force | *Kingdom of Clouds* `[src]` |
| **The world itself is changed a little** — no farewell at all; the marker is a changed ordinary world | *Animal Planet* `[src]` |
| **Time travel used for looking, not fixing**, anchored to a physical object | *A Grandmother's Recollections* `[snip]` |

## 7. Fujiko F. Fujio's stated principles

All from the Fujiko F. Fujio Museum's own English-language material. `[src]`

- **"For me, SF stands for Sukoshi [a little] Fushigi [mysterious]."** Not
  science fiction: *one bizarre element intruding into an otherwise ordinary
  everyday life, and the chain reaction it triggers* — a thought experiment in
  which a single variable is changed in a familiar setting. `[src]` The
  operative constraint: **exactly one impossible thing per story, dropped into a
  wholly mundane setting.** The setting must stay ordinary — school, homework, a
  bully, an errand — or the strangeness has nothing to be strange against.
  `[inf]`
- **"To know a child, know the child inside you."**
- **"Each manga should be drawn as though you were drawing for the very first
  time"** — an explicit warning against following formulas rigidly. Hold this
  against §2: the beat structure is a chassis, and his own rule is that every
  instance must be approached fresh.
- **"I want to create a character with temperature"** — deliberately flawed,
  identifiable characters over competent ones. He believed this humanity
  explained the fifty-year appeal.
- **"I want you to be greedy for things that interest you"** — ideas go stale
  without input from outside the medium.
- **"I enjoy drawing my manga, and people enjoy reading the manga. That kind of
  manga has always been my ideal."**

Attributed at one remove and worth having, though the citation chain was not
verified: *"My purpose in creating manga is to allow readers, through 'slightly
mysterious' settings, to re-examine things they take for granted."* `[src]` for
the quoting page, chain unverified.

**Stated themes**, per Wikipedia: integrity, perseverance, courage, family and
respect; violence and eroticism avoided; environmentalism incorporated. `[src]`
Awards: Japan Cartoonists Association Award (1973, 1994), Shogakukan Manga Award
(1982), Tezuka Osamu Cultural Prize (1997); appointed Japan's first anime
ambassador (2008). `[src]`

And a note that bears directly on our project: scholarship on the series
(Wu 1994; Lin 2004; Hu 2007, via the IAFOR paper) reads it as having "an
educational purpose … in effect making it another 'textbook'," with cartoon
characters becoming "virtual identity objects for children." `[src]` We are not
the first to notice that this material teaches.

## 8. The audience — Vietnamese thirteen-year-olds in 2026

free and a hazard we have to manage.

- Kim Đồng published the first Vietnamese volume on **11 December 1992** —
  unlicensed, translated from the Thai edition rather than the Japanese, with
  names and details freely altered. `[src]` 40,000 copies of the first four
  volumes sold in a week in Ho Chi Minh City; **40–50 million copies** in
  circulation by 2006. `[src]`

  and royalties paid for the unlicensed period. The **Doraemon Scholarship
  Fund** was founded that year from his Vietnamese licensing fee, and had
  awarded 10,574 scholarships by 2017. `[src]` (Two sources disagree on the
  month — do not state one without re-checking.)
- **The names reverted in 2010.** Kim Đồng's relaunch (29 May 2010) restored
  Japanese reading order and Japanese names. `[src]`

| --- | --- | --- |
| Doraemon | Đôrêmon | **Doraemon** |
| Nobita | Nôbita | **Nobita** |
| Shizuka | Xuka | **Shizuka** |

| Suneo | Xêkô | **Suneo** |
| Dekisugi | Đêkhi `[rec]` | **Dekisugi** |

  A learner born after ~2000 grew up on the post-2010 books, the HTV3 dub and
  the annual films. **Xuka, Chaien and Xêkô are their parents' register**, not
  theirs. `[inf]` — the 2010 switch is sourced; no survey of teen usage was
  found.
- **Still entirely current.** The 2025 film reached roughly VND 160 billion in
  Vietnam, the franchise's local record, with headlines about it outgrossing
  domestic productions. `[snip]` The manga has been in print from Kim Đồng
  continuously since 1992. `[src]`
- Doraemon is **already used commercially as English-teaching material in
  Vietnam** — bilingual "Learn English with Doraemon" volumes, English
  long-story sets sold by language centres. `[snip]` Several of those hits are
  pirated PDFs hosted by Vietnamese English centres, which tells us about local
  norms rather than about permission.

**What this means for the writing.** Our readers will probably recognise the
shape of what we are doing very quickly, and if so the frame costs us no setup —
but note that this is `[inf]`, resting on publication history and box office
rather than on any measurement of teenage recognition, which §11 records as
missing. If it holds, every borrowed beat will be read *as* a borrowing, so the
originality has to be real rather than a coat of paint: a cat robot who is
visibly Doraemon with a different name will read as a knock-off to the one
audience best equipped to notice. If it does not hold — if 13-year-olds have
aged out of the frame — the borrowing is safer and the setup is more expensive.
**Both readings argue for genuine originality**, which is why the advice in §9
does not depend on settling this.

## 9. What we may build, and what we may not

Reported from sources, not as legal advice.

**The work is in copyright everywhere relevant, for the whole of any planning
horizon.** Fujiko F. Fujio Pro holds the copyright; Shogakukan publishes.
`[src]` Fujimoto died in 1996. Japan's term is life + 70 (extended from 50 in
December 2018, not retroactively), so protection runs to the end of 2066;
Vietnam's own term is life + 50, so at minimum to the end of 2046. `[snip]` for
the statutes, `[inf]` for the arithmetic. Character names are also almost
certainly registered trademarks in Vietnam — a separate and longer-lived barrier
than copyright. `[inf]`

**There is exactly one well-documented enforcement action, and its shape is the
warning.** An unofficial "final episode" doujinshi, 20 pages, released in 2005,
sold "over 13,300 copies by summer 2006" before being stopped by Shogakukan. In
May 2007 the author apologised to Shogakukan **and** Fujiko Production and
agreed to pay an undisclosed settlement sum based on the doujinshi's sales
(rumoured to be several million yen). `[src]`

Three cautions on how that case is usually retold, all of which this file got
wrong on the first pass:

- **Whether litigation was ever filed is not established** by either report.
  Neither says "no court case"; ComiPress notes that "many doujinshi creators
  have criticized Shogakukan for taking legal actions." Do not write "no
  lawsuit." `[src]`
- **The "far surpassed the normal sales of a doujinshi" line is Shogakukan's
  reply to its critics**, paraphrased indirectly by ComiPress — not a stated
  justification for acting. `[src]`
- **The teachers detail is third-hand and tagged as rumour.** ComiPress's
  headline is a question, the piece is filed under *Rumors*, and the story
  reaches it from the tabloid *Saizou* via Livedoor: Shogakukan's attitude
  "took a 180 degree turn in April, after hearing some elementary school
  teachers commenting that 'the ending in Doraemon - Last Episode was very
  touching and contains educational value, and plan to use the work as material
  for teaching moral lessons in their class.'" `[src]` for the reporting,
  unverified as fact. It is close enough to our situation to be worth knowing
  and too weakly sourced to plan around.

What is solidly established as having drawn the response is **(a) commercial
sale at scale, (b) use of the actual characters and their names, and (c) art and
story closely resembling the original.** `[src]`

No enforcement action in Vietnam was found, in either direction. Absence of
found cases is not evidence of a permissive posture.

**The line, as sources state it.** Copyright "does not protect ideas, concepts,
systems, or methods of doing something," and separately "does not protect names,
titles, slogans, or short phrases" (US Copyright Office) `[src]`; 17 U.S.C.
§102(b) and TRIPS Art. 9(2) carry the same idea/expression dichotomy `[src]`.
Copyright "may subsist in particular characters involved in a work, but
generally not in the idea or genre of the story itself" `[snip]`.

### The rule this file is written to

**Most of §§1–7 is technique, and technique is the part copyright does not
reach.** `[inf]`, and the label matters — this is the most legally load-bearing
sentence in the file and it is our reasoning from the cited idea/expression
propositions, not something any source says about this project. Beat structures,
gadget-design rules, the specific-incompetence principle, the flawed-helper
principle, "one impossible thing in a mundane setting" — studying and reusing
that is what a writer is supposed to do with a work they admire.

**But "each abstraction is unprotected" does not entail "all of them together
are."** The thing we would be shipping retains, simultaneously: a second-hand
robot cat from the future, a poor and inept boy, a pocket that dispenses
gadgets, the same helper/wisher relationship, equivalent slots for the bully,
the rich boy, the admired girl and the unhateable rival, the same per-chapter
engine, and a long-story beat map derived from the franchise. **Selection and
arrangement can be distinctive even where every element is generic**, and
nothing in §9's cited sources addresses a package this close. `[inf]` This is
the risk the first draft of this file did not name, and it is not cured by
renaming.

**Nothing in §5's parenthetical names, §3's example plots or §8's name table is
ours to use.** We take the *slots*, not the characters who fill them; the
*ending techniques*, not the endings.

Concretely, for the story we write next — **stated as prudence, not as
established legal necessity**, and the difference is worth keeping straight:

0. **Diverge on structure, not only on surface.** Given the cumulative-similarity
   point above, the measures below are the cheap half. At least one *structural*
   departure — a different reason the helper is present, a different cast
   geometry, a different relationship between the boy's flaw and the help he
   gets — does more real work than every rename combined.
1. **New names for everyone**, including the robot. Not transliterations, not
   near-misses. Note that this is a *branding and originality* measure:
   copyright does not protect names, and the Vietnamese trademark position is
   `[inf]` with no search performed. Do not record it as a legal requirement.
2. **New gadgets with new names**, invented against §4's rules rather than
   renamed from the corpus.
3. **New episode premises.** Not "the Memory Bread one, but with a different
   bread."
4. **Vietnamese setting, Vietnamese specifics** — which we need anyway, because
   the prescribed syllabus is about the countryside, ethnic groups, Vietnamese
   customs and Vietnamese natural disasters. The curriculum forces this for
   free, which makes it the cheapest measure available — but *cheapest* is not
   *most effective*, and changed scenery over an unchanged cast geometry is
   still surface. `[inf]`
5. **Never presented as Doraemon**, in the material or in any metadata,
   description or page title.

The brief already offered "changing the names." The argument of this section is
that names are the cheapest part, that the transferable value was never in the
characters anyway — §§2–6 are the value, and they cost nothing to take — and
that item 0 is the one that actually matters.

## 10. The container we are writing into

Regenerated from this repository on 2026-08-12. `[repo]` throughout.

```sh
ls units/*.md | wc -l                                    # 16
wc -l units/*.md | tail -1                               # 13880
grep -c '^## Lesson' units/unit-*.md                     # 7 each, 84 total
```

**Twelve units of seven lessons = 84 lessons, plus four reviews.** 299 `::: task`
blocks, 16 each of audio/write/clock/passage, 8 thread directives (one strand
introduced, seven checks), 29 bridges. Every unit is structurally identical:
Getting Started · A Closer Look 1 · A Closer Look 2 · Communication · Skills 1

Measured, not estimated. **Method, because the number is method-dependent and
`[repo]` is unearned without one:** *dialogue* = blockquote lines only (`^>`,
marker stripped) inside each `### Dialogue` section; *passage* = blockquote
lines inside each `::: passage` fence; *audio* = the whole `::: audio` body;
*model* = the sum of the printed `*(N words)*` figures. Whitespace tokenisation.

| Slot | 12 units | 4 reviews |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Lesson 1 dialogue | 2,498 | — |
| Lesson 5 reading passage (`::: passage`) | 2,888 | 1,285 |
| Lesson 6 listening script (`::: audio`) | 2,461 | 810 |
| Lesson 6 writing model | 1,166 | 371 |
| **Total** | **9,013** | **2,466** |

**11,479 words** across those four slots alone `[repo]`. Counting the dialogue's

free at the clause level. **The real budget is comfortably a short novel.**

Per unit that is roughly 750 words of continuous narrative in four scenes, plus
sentence-level presence everywhere else. Seven lessons therefore support seven
beats per unit — five of them real prose, two (A Closer Look 1 and 2) carrying
the story only in example sentences and exercise stems.

### The prescribed targets are fixed, and they already form an arc

The syllabus is not negotiable — `tools/check_coverage.py` currently reports
328/328 lexis coverage and no gaps on any unit. But read the twelve topics in
order:

> Leisure Time · Life in the Countryside · Teenagers · Ethnic Groups of Viet Nam
> · Our Customs and Traditions · Lifestyles · Environmental Protection ·
> Shopping · Natural Disasters · Communication in the Future · Science and
> Technology · **Life on Other Planets**

Ordinary life at one end, off-world at the other, with an environmental crisis
and a natural disaster in the middle. **The story does not have to fight the
syllabus; the syllabus is already shaped like a departure.** `[inf]`

The four reviews sit after units 3, 6, 9 and 12 and reach only into the three
units they follow. That looks like a prescribed four-act structure — but the
resemblance is thematic, and a review is a poor act break in three specific
ways: **it teaches nothing by design**, so it cannot introduce a setting,
character or word; **it may only reach into its own three units**, so it cannot
gather the arc; and it runs **two timers whose ownership depends on the fixed
Reading-then-Listening order**, so a story-driven rearrangement can silently
kill exercises even with every directive still present. Use reviews as *act
punctuation* — recombination, not revelation. `[repo]` + `[inf]`

### The constraints that will actually bite

Full detail lives in the repo's own docs; these are the ones a story-driven
rewrite trips first.

> **The dangerous one first, because it is the only one that fails silently.**
> The standing repo rule is that the site is *at least as complete as the
> official book on every target, and never less*. But `check_coverage.py`
> **reports and exits 0** — coverage is a curriculum decision, so it is
> deliberately not a build failure. **A large story rewrite can therefore go
> fully green while quietly deleting required curriculum.** Every rewritten unit
> must still carry its lexis, its grammar and pronunciation targets, its
> Everyday English function and its Communication content block — and the last
> two are exactly the ones this repo has historically dropped, and exactly the
> ones at risk when Lesson 4 is repurposed as a story scene. **Read the coverage
> report on every unit you touch; passing gates do not mean covered
> curriculum.** `[repo]`

1. **`check_write.js` breaks on nearly any change to a writing model.** Each of
   the 16 models is run through its own checklist — word bounds, required
   vocabulary from that unit's table, forbidden phrase lists, paragraph counts.
   Unit 7's model is 119 words against a 100–120 bound: one sentence of
   scene-setting overflows it. This is correct behaviour, not an obstacle to
   route around.
2. **Task keys quote their source text verbatim.** 1,628 marked items **in the
   twelve units** (1,823 course-wide, with the reviews' 195). Rewriting a
   dialogue or a passage invalidates every key that quoted it. Mechanical, but
   it is ~300 task blocks of mechanical — and how many of those items actually
   quote their source has never been counted (see §11).
3. **`check_ielts.py` reads the prose, not just the directives** — it fails on
   band promises, phrase banks, genre over-claims, and on a review naming a unit
   outside its three. **A serialized story is a standing temptation to recap
   last chapter, and a recap that quotes the listening script is a build
   failure.**
4. **Eight of the twelve writing genres ask the learner to write about their own
   life** — their free time, their family, their shopping, their stress. A
   third-person story cannot be the subject of "write about what you do for your
   family." The resolution that preserves the genre, the word count and the
   `trains=` criterion: **the model answer is in-world (a character writes it);
   the learner's task stays about the learner.** Units 7 (a notice) and 9
   (flood instructions) are impersonal by genre and can be wholly in-world.
   `[inf]`
5. **Nothing on a page may explain the page.** A framing device — "follow our
   story and you will learn X" — is exactly the prohibited construct. The story
   has to *be* the material, never a narrator commenting on the course.
6. **The lesson contract and the generated site.** Teaching precedes practice;
   the unit test unlocks only after all seven lessons. And `docs/` is generated
   and committed, with no CI gate before publication — so a rewrite ships
   whatever the last local build produced. Rebuild and re-run all eight gates
   before committing, every time.

And one structural fact worth deciding early: **there is currently no mechanism
in this repo that enforces narrative continuity.** `::: thread` is the only
construct that enforces recurrence, there is exactly one strand (`articles`,
introduced in Unit 5, checked in 6–12), and it requires a `measure`, an
evidential `marker` and an IELTS `src` — it is built to make a *language* target
recur, with a warrant. Declaring a narrative spine as a thread would either need
an honest warrant or would be an abuse of the construct. **A story spine is a
code change, not just a content change.** `[repo]` + `[inf]`

### Existing cast

The course already has recurring names, though nothing enforces continuity
between their appearances: Duy (98 occurrences across 10 files), Linh (70/8),
Mai (65/9), Hoa (51/6). `[repo]` — counts for Nam, Ha, An and Chi are inflated

## 11. Open questions

Named honestly, because each is a place where this file is thinner than it
looks.

**1. The serialization protocol does not exist yet, and it is the blocker.**
See the box in §1. Before any prose is written, someone has to decide and write
down: what may each unit assume a reader has read? What must it recap, and by
what mechanism that does not print a listening script? What may it resolve, and
what must it defer to the arc? Which units are the designated continuity
chapters and which stay resettable? Until that exists, "each lesson is a
chapter" is an intention, not a design. This is design work, not research, and
it needs decisions only the author can make.

**2. The cumulative-similarity question in §9 is unanalysed.** Every individual
abstraction we are reusing is unprotected; the package has not been assessed,
and no source cited here speaks to it.

**3. Several load-bearing rules are `[inf]`, not findings.** The
souvenir-as-anti-reset device, the gadget introduction sequence, "exactly one
impossible thing per story", the three consequences of the defective helper, and
the claim that shared poverty makes the help read as non-charitable — these are
this file's reasoning, and §9 turns some of them into production requirements
for future authors. They are good working hypotheses. They are not evidence, and
the first draft of this file let their prose sound as though they were.

Research gaps proper:

- **No primary Fujiko interview text was reached.** Every *Sukoshi Fushigi*
  citation here is a museum caption or a secondary essay. The Japanese original
  (少し不思議) and the NHK documentary *The Father of Doraemon* (2004) are the
  real sources and a Japanese-language pass would settle them.
- **We have analysed Doraemon thoroughly and read almost none of it.** Every
  legitimate route to the actual prose needed a login or was bot-blocked; every
  wide-open route was piracy and was declined. So every claim in this file about
  *how the writing reads* — pacing, exchange length, how a rule is voiced on the
  page — is summary-derived. **Two minutes of human hands closes it:** the free
  Amazon Kindle sample of Volume 1 (official English), or a one-hour archive.org
  borrow of the official bilingual edition (`doraemondoraemon0000fuji`). Do this
  before drafting, not after.
- **The chapter-structure findings are n=1.** The midpoint gadget, the
  bully-opens-it beat and the left-behind complication all come from one
  chapter. More are cheap to get: the Doraemon wiki's `/U.S. English dub` pages
  return full beat summaries through `api.php?action=parse&…&prop=wikitext`,
  which works where plain WebFetch gets a 402.
- **fandom.com (402) and tvtropes.org (403) were both unreachable** from this
  network. Every `[snip]` in this file is downstream of that. The gadget-backfire
  details, the "Goodbye, Doraemon" beats and the vacant-lot description all
  deserve one dedicated pass through a reachable mirror.
- **The one directly relevant academic paper was paywalled** — *Contemporary
  Japan* 31(1), "Explaining Doraemon's perduring popularity: Structural
  analysis, specious technology and mother's indulgent affection." Its title is
  near-identical to the Marshall paper this file leans on, so it is probably the
  same author's expanded version rather than an independent second study —
  which means this gap is smaller than it looks, and also that §§1–2 rest on
  **one** structural analysis, not two. `[inf]`
- **A Vietnamese-reception paper exists and was not fetched** — ResearchGate
