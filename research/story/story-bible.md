# The Calling Lamp — story foundation

The spine for the twelve units. This is the **foundation**: the world, the cast,
the rule, and the shape of the arc. It is not the prose. The per-unit chapters
get written against this, later.

Companion document: `doraemon-craft.md`, which is where every craft rule cited
here (`§n`) comes from. This file makes choices; that file explains why they are
the right ones.

Nothing here goes on a learner's page as explanation. The story *is* the
material — never a narrator telling a thirteen-year-old what the story is for.

---

## 0. The four decisions this is built on

Settled with the project lead:

1. **A unit is a chapter.** Twelve chapters, seven scenes each, four acts broken
   by the four Reviews. Not 84 separate chapters — two of every seven lessons
   (A Closer Look 1 and 2) can only carry the story in example sentences.
2. **All-new cast.** Duy, Linh, Mai and Hoa are retired. This costs a large
   mechanical edit across the task keys and is worth it for a clean break.
3. **One continuous adventure**, not twelve episodes. This is the decision that
   solved the serialization problem `doraemon-craft.md` §1 could not.
4. **Magic, not technology.**

5. **Chapter 12 ends the adventure, not the series.** One connected line —
   Chapter 1 is the beginning, Chapter 12 is the end of *this journey*. The cast
   and their friendship survive it, exactly as they do across the Doraemon
   films. See §6, which is written to this and states the rule for drafting.

And one call made here, open to reversal: **the lamp is kept, the genie is
dropped.** A wish-granter has no stated rule, so the reader can never get a beat
ahead of the boy — and being a beat ahead is the entire engine (§2, §4). What
replaces the genie is below.

---

## 1. The premise, in five lines

In a delta town called **Bến Sẻ**, a boy who is bad at everything measurable
dredges a brass lamp out of the canal. When his tears fall on it, it lights.
A thin black cat comes out — not a spirit who grants wishes, but an **apprentice
lamp-keeper** who has lost his own name and half of his instructions.

The lamp does exactly one thing: **light it, and the nearest lost thing comes
back.** You do not get to choose which.

There is a finite measure of oil. Twelve lightings. When it is gone, the lamp is
only a lamp.

---

## 2. The one changed rule

This world is **our world, with one rule different** — the same Việt Nam, the
same school, the same grandmother, the same market. Only: *lost things can come
back.*

That choice is not decoration. `doraemon-craft.md` §3 sorted every Doraemon
other-world into *place* worlds (prehistory, space, undersea — reused
constantly) and *rule* worlds (magic-Earth, the shared dream — each used
exactly once). Rule worlds are the scarce corner, and for a language course they
are decisive: the reader already knows the house, the school, the street, the
market, so we spend zero words establishing geography and all of them on the one
changed rule — while the recurring vocabulary the syllabus prescribes stays
intact and compounds across chapters.

### The specification

Per §4 and S9's finding that the gadget's load-bearing property was never that
it is *technological* but that it is *specified*. Folklore specifies at least as
hard as engineering does — three wishes, literal fulfilment, *khắc nhập / khắc
xuất*, the three-span bag in *Ăn khế trả vàng*.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Name** | The Calling Lamp — *đèn gọi về* |
| **The rule** | *Light the lamp, and the nearest lost thing comes back.* |
| **The catch** | *You cannot choose what comes back.* |
| **The inverse** | *Blow it out, and it goes away again.* |
| **The cost** | *There is only so much oil. Twelve lightings, and then it is only a lamp.* |
| **The words** | To call: **"Come back."** To release: **"Go well."** |

Four things that rule buys us:

- **The lamp never fails.** It performs exactly as specified, every time. The
  disaster is always social — he wanted one thing and the canal gave him
  another. That is Marshall's rule, kept intact (§2, rule 3): *a gadget that
  breaks is a plot device; one that works perfectly and ruins everything is a
  premise.*
- **The reader can get ahead of the boy.** They know what the lamp does. They
  can see, before he can, what the *nearest* lost thing is going to be.
- **The fuel gauge is the chapter counter.** Twelve lightings, twelve chapters.
  A reader can literally count how much story is left, and every lighting is
  irreversible. This is the thing technology could not give us — a flat battery
  recharges; spent oil does not. Irreversibility is what makes twelve chapters
  *accumulate* instead of reset (S9).
- **The words are A1 imperatives.** "Come back." "Go well." Two words each,
  with an exact inverse — the *khắc nhập / khắc xuất* shape, and free grammar
  practice every time the story uses its own magic.

> **Authoring rule.** Every chapter states the rule again, in-world, in one
> short line of dialogue. Never as a narrator explaining the system. A
> thirteen-year-old joining at Unit 7 must be able to learn the rule from a
> character saying it to another character.

## 3. The helper — where he comes from, and why he is wrong

**Mun.** A thin black cat with one torn ear. *Mèo mun* is an ordinary Vietnamese
name for a black cat, and it is the name **Tí gives him**, because Mun cannot
remember his own.

### His entrance is borrowed from Bụt, and only his entrance

S9's finding, and the reason this story is Vietnamese rather than Japanese in
its bones: **Bụt** — the white-bearded figure of *Tấm Cám* and *Cây tre trăm
đốt* — appears **when a wronged child weeps**, asks why they are weeping, and
hands over a rule-bearing object or instruction. In *Tấm Cám* he appears three
times, each triggered by crying. In *Cây tre trăm đốt* he gives not a thing but
two words with an exact inverse.

That is, beat for beat, the opening of a Doraemon chapter — and every Vietnamese
thirteen-year-old already knows it from primary school. It costs us no setup at
all.

**But we take the entrance and refuse the infallibility.** Two reasons:

- *Bụt* is the vernacular doublet of *Phật*. Putting the Buddha in a comedy of
  errors is a respect problem for a school text, and we are not doing it.
- Bụt is *wise and correct*. A helper who is always right is a moral authority,
  not a friend — and that kills the buddy dynamic the whole thing runs on (§5).

So Mun arrives the way Bụt arrives, and is then revealed to be **an apprentice**:
junior, out of practice, working from half-remembered instructions, and
confidently wrong about his own rules perhaps twice an act. The real Lamp-keeper
is gone. Mun is covering.

> **This is our structural departure**, and it is a real one rather than a
> rename. It is a departure from Doraemon *and* from Tấm Cám: the helper is not
> sent, not assigned, not owed, not wise. He is standing in for someone better
> and hoping nobody notices.

Honest note, carried forward from S9: no source found *any* canonical constraint
on Bụt. "Keep the entrance, lose the infallibility" is therefore an invention,
not an extension of the folklore. Also unchecked: whether Vietnamese educational
publishers would object to a Bụt-derived comic figure at all. Since Mun is a
cat and is never called Bụt, the exposure is small — but it is not zero, and
someone should ask before this ships.

### Why a cat, and why that is not a borrowing

The **Vietnamese zodiac uniquely has the Cat (Mão)** where the Chinese has the
Rabbit. A cat helper is not a nod to a Japanese robot; it is one of the twelve
*con giáp* every Vietnamese child can recite. Mun's fallibility, his poverty and
his torn ear all read as *mèo mun* — the ordinary black cat of a poor delta
house — long before they read as anything else.

### What Mun is not

No pocket. No inventory. No future. No descendant. No debt. No mission to
reform anybody. He has one lamp, twelve lightings, and an imperfect memory.

## 4. The cast

Written as *slots first*, per `doraemon-craft.md` §5 — the slot is the reusable
craft; the person filling it is ours. Names chosen to avoid every name already
in heavy use in the course.

| Slot (§5) | Ours | What they are |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The unreliable wisher | **Tí** (given name Tuấn; nobody uses it) | Thirteen. Lives with his grandmother; his parents work in a factory city and send money home. Lights the lamp for small reasons and gets large ones. |
| The finite dispenser | **Mun** | The apprentice. See §3. |
| The witness who already believes | **Thảo** | Sees the lamp work in Chapter 2 and simply accepts it. Treats Tí as already worth something, which is the reader's licence to like him. |
| The deadline in human form | **Hùng** | Big, loud, genuinely frightening at close range, and a terrible singer who believes otherwise. Supplies the reason a thing must be fixed *today*. Turns useful in a crisis. |
| The have, to the have-not | **Đạt** | Has everything, is physically the smallest, talks his way out of what Tí gets hit for. Supplies **envy** — the wound the lightings are actually made of. |
| The domestic enforcer | **Bà Sáu**, his grandmother | Strict, funny, worried about money out loud. Whatever the lamp does, she is the reckoning. Southern birth-order naming, deliberately. |
| The mirror ahead | **Chú Bảy**, an uncle | A grown man who was also bad at school and is fine, mostly. The story's quiet promise. |
| The institutional scoreboard | **Cô Yến** | Makes failure public and recorded, and carries it from school to the house. |
| The unhateable superior | **Khoa** | Top of everything and genuinely kind, so the inadequacy is entirely Tí's own and cannot curdle into a grudge. |
| The superior substitute | **Bạch**, a white cat | Appears once, in Act III. A *properly trained* keeper — faster, correct, and much worse for Tí, because she has no idea who he is. Proves the relationship is load-bearing, not the lamp. |
| The guest who needs help | **the Lamp-keeper** (*người giữ đèn*) | Lost — and, from Act IV, found. He is the one who **needs** something, the one who travels with them at the end, and **the one we say goodbye to.** The farewell belongs to him. |
| The distant beneficiary | the Lamp-keeper, before he is found | While he is missing he is also the reason Mun is covering: stakes without a villain. |

### Tí, specified

§5's rule: **name the failures and name the talents; never say "loser."**

**Fails at**, concretely: remembering numbers and dates; every running race;
writing anything at speed; the dark; doing what he was told ten minutes ago.

**Good at**, uselessly: skipping a stone eight or nine bounces; imitating any
bird he has heard once; falling asleep in about a minute, anywhere.

Those three are **deliberately worthless** — no school value, nothing the plot
can secretly cash in. That is the point: a hidden *useful* talent makes an
underdog a fraud who was fine all along; a hidden useless one makes him a
person. What happens instead, per §3's beat 12, is that **the world's demand
changes to match him** — by Act IV the story needs someone who can call a bird,
cross a surface, or go somewhere only sleep reaches. He never levels up.

One more, and it is quiet until the last chapter: **he never forgets a face.**
Nobody thinks this is a skill.

## 5. Fixed furniture

Four places, reused all twelve chapters. §6: a closed circuit means each setting
owns one beat, a short chapter needs no establishing work — and for a language
course, a fixed set of places means a **recycling lexical field**, so vocabulary
compounds instead of resetting with the plot.

| Place | Its job | The beat it owns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The canal landing** (*bến sông*) | Unowned, adult-free, where the water gives things up | The problem, and the lighting |
| **Bà Sáu's kitchen** | Warm, strict, smells of something | The consequence |
| **The school yard** | Where failure is public | The humiliation |
| **The market** | Everyone, all at once, all talking | The complication and the rumour |

The lamp lives **under the bed**, wrapped in a rice sack. Infinity stored in the
most ordinary object in a poor house — §6's point about the desk drawer: the
furniture is the thesis.

## 6. The arc

Twelve chapters. Each unit's prescribed topic supplies **what is lost**, and the
losses escalate: an object → an animal → a person → a place → a practice → a
language → a habitat → a year → a name.

The prescribed syllabus was never fighting us. Read in order it is already
shaped like a departure — ordinary life, then out to the countryside, then the
highlands, then a flood, then the sky.

| # | Unit topic | Grammar the unit must teach | What comes back |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Leisure Time | V-ing vs to-V | **A game.** Something small, playful, and completely useless — so the rule is learned cheaply. |
| 2 | Life in the Countryside | Comparative adverbs | **A path.** The old way to the fields, which the new road replaced and nobody can find. |
| 3 | Teenagers | Simple & compound sentences | **A friend.** Someone who left the class last year. He comes back, and he has not aged. |
| 4 | Ethnic Groups of Viet Nam | Questions; countable/uncountable | **A house.** A stilt house, upriver, and the questions they must ask to learn whose it was. |
| 5 | Our Customs and Traditions | Articles | **A festival.** One night that stopped happening, happening again. |
| 6 | Lifestyles | Future simple & 1st conditional | **A way of living.** The conditional is the chapter: *if we call it back, then…* |
| 7 | Environmental Protection | Complex sentences; time clauses | **A forest.** All at once, in the wrong place. The first time the lamp frightens them. |
| 8 | Shopping | Adverbs of frequency | **A market.** With its sellers, its prices, and its old money. |
| 9 | Natural Disasters | Past continuous + past simple | **A town.** The one under the river. Past continuous is the grammar of a flood, exactly. |
| 10 | Communication in the Future | Prepositions; possessives | **A voice.** A message that was sent and never arrived. |
| 11 | Science and Technology | Reported speech — statements | **An inventor.** The Lamp-keeper's own work, and what he *said* the lamp was never meant to do. |
| 12 | Life on Other Planets | Reported speech — questions | **A name.** Mun's own — and the place it came from. |

That the grammar targets fit this well is not luck to be relied on twice; it is
the reason the losses were chosen in this order. Chapters 9, 11 and 12 in
particular exist in that shape *because* the prescribed grammar wanted them
there.

### The chain — this is one story, not twelve

**The binding rule for anyone drafting: every chapter's ending must cause the
next chapter's problem.** Not "and then another week went by." A reader must be
able to say *because* between any two consecutive chapters. If a chapter could
be swapped with another chapter, it is not written yet.

> **1 → 2** The first lighting brings back something small and silly, and
> teaches them the rule — but it reaches further than any of them expected.
> Mun is visibly surprised, which is the first hint he does not know what he is
> doing.
> **2 → 3** The path that comes back is not just a path. It is *the route
> upriver*. Everything in Act II is only reachable because of Chapter 2.
> **3 → 4** The friend who comes back has not aged. Blowing the lamp out would
> send him back, and Tí cannot do it. **The first thing he will not un-lose** —
> and the friend knows something about what else has been called back before.
> **4 → 5** Following that upriver, a stilt house returns. Whose? The
> questions are the chapter.
> **5 → 6** The house's people had a festival, and it returns with them — and
> at the festival **somebody recognises the lamp.** The rumour starts here.
> **6 → 7** Because of the rumour, other people now want the lamp. The
> question stops being *what shall we call back* and becomes *what happens to
> now, if we keep doing this*.
> **7 → 8** A forest returns in the wrong place and does real damage. The
> lamp is now dangerous and the adults know about it.
> **8 → 9** A whole market returns, with its old money, and the disorder is
> big enough that **Bạch arrives** — someone competent has noticed.
> **9 → 10** The flood. The town under the river comes back. Mun's
> instructions were wrong and somebody is nearly lost. This is the dark hour.
> **10 → 11** A voice: a message the Lamp-keeper sent long ago that never
> arrived. It tells them what the lamp actually is.
> **11 → 12** They find the keeper's work — and what he *said* the lamp was
> never meant to do. One measure of oil left.

The four Reviews sit at the act joints and **recombine, never advance** — a
Review teaches nothing by design and may only reach into its own three units.

### Four acts

The Reviews after Units 3, 6, 9 and 12 are the act breaks — but they are poor
places for revelation. A Review teaches nothing by design, may only reach into
its own three units, and runs two order-dependent timers. **Use them as act
punctuation: recombination, never new plot.**

- **Act I — Bến Sẻ (1–3).** The lamp found by accident. Small losses, the rule
  learned, the cost noticed. Ends on the first thing he cannot un-lose.
- **Act II — Upriver (4–6).** Older and larger losses: a house, a festival, a
  way of living. The world widens and Mun's instructions start being wrong.
- **Act III — The water (7–9).** The lamp begins returning things nobody asked
  for. Bạch arrives and is better and worse. The flood is the dark hour.
- **Act IV — Reaching (10–12).** Voices across distance, the keeper's work, and
  the last measure of oil.

### The ending — of the adventure, not of the children

**This is the rule that governs Chapter 12, and it is easy to get wrong.**

Chapter 12 ends **this adventure**. It does not end Tí, it does not end Mun, and
it does not end their friendship. That is how the Doraemon long stories
actually work: the *guest* departs and the door closes, the core cast goes home
with the arrangement intact, and there is another adventure next year. The
"Goodbye, Doraemon" model — where the helper leaves for good — is a **different
thing**, used once, and we are not using it.

So the farewell is spent on **the Lamp-keeper**, not on the cat.

**How it goes.** Tí lights the lamp for the twelfth and last time. What comes
back is **Mun's name** — and with his name, Mun remembers everything, including
the keeper. The keeper is found, thanked, and goes home; the empty lamp goes
with him, because it was never ours. Door closed, in the *Spaceblazer* manner:
no villain to blame, the geometry just closes.

**And Mun stays.** He could go back now and be properly trained. He doesn't.
That is a **choice**, and a choice is stronger than a departure — it also leaves
every character standing where the next adventure can find them.

Four rules the ending obeys:

- **The magic is not what wins.** Across every long story, by the climax the
  gadget is never the thing that wins. The last light is *given away*, not spent
  on the boy's own want.
- **The ending was promised in Chapter 1.** Twelve measures of oil, counted
  down in the reader's sight the whole way. When the adventure ends it ends
  because the thing that started it is used up — not because an author decided
  to stop.
- **The magic closes; the world does not.** The oil is gone, the lamp is cold,
  the town goes back to ordinary. Tí still can't remember a date. Bà Sáu still
  wants to know where he's been. The cat is still on the roof.
- **A souvenir, so the world stays changed.** Each act leaves one thing behind
  that does not go back — the anti-reset device (§3, beat 16). At the very end:
  one thing in Bến Sẻ that stayed returned, and a boy who never forgets a face.

**The last beat leaves the door open.** Not "and they never saw magic again."
Something small and ordinary that implies tomorrow — because if this works, the
next thing anyone will ask for is a second adventure, and Chapter 12 must not
have made that impossible.

> **Authoring rule for whoever drafts Chapter 12:** nothing in it may be
> irreversible *about the cast*. The lamp is spent, the keeper is gone, a
> loss is real — but no character is written out, no relationship is closed,
> and no sentence forecloses another journey.

## 7. How a chapter is built — the seven-scene template

Per unit: roughly 750 words of continuous prose in four real scenes, plus three
lighter beats carried in example sentences and exercise stems.

| Lesson | Slot | Scene |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 — Getting Started | dialogue, ~210 w | **The trouble.** Two characters, present tense, an ordinary problem. |
| 2 — A Closer Look 1 | example sentences | Story words and story sentences. No plot. |
| 3 — A Closer Look 2 | example sentences | The unit's grammar, spoken by our people. |
| 4 — Communication | content block, ~200 w | **The lighting.** Must still teach the book's named content block. |
| 5 — Skills 1 | reading passage, ~240 w | **What came back.** Topic fixed by the syllabus; the story surrounds it. |
| 6 — Skills 2 | audio script ~200 w + writing model ~95 w | **It goes wrong**, then a character writes something. |
| 7 — Looking Back | error-hunt paragraph, ~70 w | **Where we are now.** Doubles as the recap. |

### Two structural findings that change this template

**The gadget arrives at the midpoint, not the opening.** From the one chapter we
could reconstruct at beat level, the gadget landed at beat 5 of 9, after four
beats of ordinary domestic and social pressure. That is a gift here: Lessons 1–3
are pure A2 daily-life language — invitations, refusing food, being told off —
carrying no fantasy vocabulary load at all. The lamp does not appear until
Lesson 4.

**The adventure is a swerve away from whatever the start was.** Across 38 films,
71% of adventures are unsought — but the sharper rule is that even in the
*planned* ones, the plan is never the story: they set out for treasure and meet
pirates. So: **every chapter opens on an ordinary want, and what the lamp
returns is never it.**

And one correction to our own model, worth carrying: "opens with the boy already
in tears" is **one common opening, not the opening.** The chapter we read opens
on the *bully's* announcement, with our protagonist merely present. Vary it.

## 8. Voice

- **A2/B1, and the register is not a compromise.** Shogakukan itself publishes a
  bilingual English Doraemon positioned for junior-high learners.
- **Name magic the way the official translation names gadgets.** "Anywhere
  door." "Shrink ray." "Translation gummy." Every one is a transparent compound
  of A1 words whose function a learner decodes without a gloss. **The Calling
  Lamp** is built to that rule. So is everything we add.
- **Short names, stressed, easy to say aloud.** Tí. Mun. Thảo. Khoa. These get
  read aloud into listening scripts.
- **Explaining happens outside the dialogue.** The official translation pushed
  cultural notes into footnotes because balloon space was tight. Our equivalent:
  the dialogue stays simple, the reading passage does the explaining.
- **One impossible thing, in a wholly ordinary setting.** Fujiko's *Sukoshi
  Fushigi* — "a little bit strange." The school, the homework, the market and
  the grandmother must all stay completely ordinary, or the lamp has nothing to
  be strange against.
- **Never a narrator explaining the course.** No "in this chapter you will
  learn." The story is the material.

## 9. What this deliberately does not take

Codex's sharpest finding on the craft document was that *every abstraction being
individually unprotected does not make the whole package safe* — selection and
arrangement can be distinctive even where each element is generic. So, listed
plainly, what we changed at the skeleton and not just the surface:

| Doraemon | Ours |
| --- | --- |
| A pocket of unlimited gadgets | One lamp, one rule, twelve lightings |
| Sent from the future by a descendant, to reform the boy | Found by accident in a canal; nobody sent anybody |
| A debt owed by the family | No debt, no mission, no obligation |
| A helper who knows what he is doing | An apprentice covering for someone better |
| Episodic; every chapter resets | One continuous journey with a spent, counted resource |
| Adventures to other planets and other times | One world, one changed rule, one province |
| The boy is judged and never changes | The world's demand changes to match him |

Plus: new names throughout, new magic, new losses, a Vietnamese folk entrance,
and a Vietnamese setting the syllabus was going to force on us anyway.

The rename was always the cheap half. This table is the expensive half.

## 10. Open, and needing a decision

1. **The lamp-not-genie call** is mine, argued in §0 and §2. Overturn it if you
   want a wish-granter; everything downstream changes if you do.
2. **Religious sensitivity on the Bụt entrance** — unchecked, and worth asking a
   Vietnamese teacher or editor before this ships. Mun is a cat and is never
   called Bụt, so exposure is small, not zero.
3. **Chapter 3's friend "has not aged."** That is the darkest beat in Act I and
   the one most likely to need softening for the age group. Flagged now rather
   than discovered in draft.
4. **Whether Bà Sáu or the absent parents carry the poverty.** Both are true to
   Vietnamese life; only one should carry the *emotional* weight, or the
   chapters get crowded.
5. **The primary-source gap is still open.** We have analysed Doraemon's
   structure thoroughly and read almost none of its actual prose — every
   legitimate route needed a login. Two minutes with a free Kindle sample of
   Volume 1, or a one-hour archive.org borrow of the official bilingual edition,
   converts our line-level craft claims from inference to evidence. Worth doing
   before Chapter 1 is drafted, not after.
