# The Sea Gives Back — story foundation

*Biển trả lại.*

The spine for the twelve units. This is the **foundation**: the world, the cast,
the rule, and the shape of the arc. It is not the prose. The per-unit chapters
get written against this, later.

Companion documents: `chapter-briefs.md`, which is the frozen interface a
drafter is actually handed, and `doraemon-craft.md`, which is where the craft
rules cited here (`§n`) come from. That file explains why these shapes work;
this file makes the choices. It analyses a different series from the one this
story now resembles, and it is still the right companion: what it establishes is
how a serialized children's adventure is *built* — the beat map, the cast slots,
the rule that a magic which works perfectly is a premise and a magic that breaks
is a plot device. None of that is about robots.

Nothing here goes on a learner's page as explanation. The story *is* the
material — never a narrator telling a thirteen-year-old what the story is for.

---

## 0. The decisions this is built on

Settled with the project lead:

1. **A unit is a chapter.** Twelve chapters, seven scenes each, four acts broken
   by the four Reviews. Not 84 separate chapters — two of every seven lessons
   (A Closer Look 1 and 2) can only carry the story in example sentences.
2. **One continuous adventure**, not twelve episodes.
3. **Magic, not technology.**
4. **Chapter 12 ends the adventure, not the series.** The cast and their
   friendship survive it. See §6, which is written to this and states the rule
   for drafting.
5. **Tí and Thảo are kept.** They are drawn, they are written, and they work.
   Everything else in the cast is re-slotted around them.
6. **The magical friend *is* the magic.** There is no device. This is the
   decision that separates this version from the one it replaces, and §2
   explains what it costs and what it buys.
7. **The place is real.** Quy Nhơn, on the central coast — a beautiful beach
   city with a working fish harbour in it. §5 says which parts are real and
   which are ours.

## 1. The premise, in five lines

In **Quy Nhơn**, a boy who is bad at everything measurable pulls a fish out of a
net that should not have held it. He keeps it in a bucket behind the house and
feeds it, and it will not stay a fish.

She is a child of the sea, ashore without permission, and the sea has come
looking for her. It searches the way water searches — by rising.

And everywhere it reaches, it puts back what it took.

There is a wall along the harbour. Every time it happens, the water stands a
little higher against it, and somebody makes a mark. **Twelve marks**, and the
sea is over the street.

---

## 2. The one changed rule

This world is **our world, with one rule different** — the same Việt Nam, the
same school, the same grandmother, the same market. Only: *the sea gives back
what it took.*

That choice is not decoration. `doraemon-craft.md` §3 sorted every other-world
into *place* worlds (prehistory, space, undersea) and *rule* worlds (magic-Earth,
the shared dream). Rule worlds are the scarce corner, and for a language course
they are decisive: the reader already knows the house, the school, the street,
the harbour, so we spend zero words establishing geography and all of them on
the one changed rule — while the recurring vocabulary the syllabus prescribes
stays intact and compounds across chapters.

### The specification

Per §4 and S9's finding that the load-bearing property of a magic is never that
it is *technological* but that it is **specified**. Folklore specifies at least
as hard as engineering does — three wishes, literal fulfilment, *khắc nhập /
khắc xuất*, the three-span bag in *Ăn khế trả vàng*.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **The rule** | *The sea gives back what it took.* |
| **Why now** | *It is looking for Bống.* |
| **The aim** | *Wherever she is standing when the tide turns, it gives back the nearest thing it took there.* |
| **The catch** | *You never choose what comes back. You choose where you are standing.* |
| **The inverse** | *Carry a thing into the water and it goes home again.* |
| **The cost** | *Every time, the sea stands a little higher. Twelve marks on the harbour wall, and it is over the street.* |
| **The words** | To call: **"Come back."** — only she can say it. To release: **"Go well."** — anyone can. |

Five things that rule buys us:

- **The magic never fails.** It performs exactly as specified, every time. The
  disaster is always social — they stood in the wrong place and the sea was
  generous. That is Marshall's rule, kept intact (§2, rule 3): *a magic that
  breaks is a plot device; one that works perfectly and ruins everything is a
  premise.*
- **The reader can get ahead of the children.** They know what the sea does.
  They can see, before Tí can, what the nearest lost thing is going to be — and
  they can see him walking to the place where it is.
- **The counter is in the world, not in anybody's head.** Twelve chalk marks
  climbing a concrete wall. A reader can count how much story is left by looking
  at a wall, and the marks only ever go up. Irreversibility is what makes twelve
  chapters *accumulate* instead of reset (S9).
- **The rule has an asymmetry, and the asymmetry is the plot.** Only Bống can
  call. Anyone can release. So every return is hers and every letting-go is
  theirs, which means the children are never passengers and she is never a
  vending machine.
- **The words are A1 imperatives.** "Come back." "Go well." Two words each, with
  an exact inverse — the *khắc nhập / khắc xuất* shape, and free grammar
  practice every time the story uses its own magic.

> **Authoring rule.** Every chapter states the rule again, in-world, in one
> short line of dialogue. Never as a narrator explaining the system. A
> thirteen-year-old joining at Unit 7 must be able to learn the rule from a
> character saying it to another character.

### What this version gives up, stated honestly

The version this replaces had a **spendable resource** — twelve measures of oil
in a lamp, burned one per chapter. That is a cleaner gauge than a rising tide,
because spending is a decision and rising is weather. We lose a little of that.

What we get for it: the wonder and the danger are now the same person. In the
lamp version the farewell at the end belonged to a guest the reader had barely
met, and the boy's relationship with the magic was a relationship with an
object. Here the thing the story is about and the thing the story is made of are
one girl, and Chapter 12's question — *does she stay* — has been the question
since Chapter 1 without anybody having to say so.

**Mitigation, and it binds:** the marks are made **on the page, by a character,
with chalk, at the end of every chapter.** Never by a narrator. The count is
part of the story's furniture, not its bookkeeping.

## 3. Bống — where she comes from, and why she is wrong

**Bống.** Named by Tí, for what she was when he found her: a *cá bống*, a goby,
the small ordinary fish of every Vietnamese ditch and estuary.

### Her entrance is borrowed from *Tấm Cám*, and only her entrance

S9's finding, and the reason this story is Vietnamese rather than Japanese in
its bones: in *Tấm Cám*, the wronged girl is given **a fish to keep in the
well**, and she feeds it what little she has, and she calls it up out of the
water with a rhyme, and it comes. *Bống bống bang bang.* Every Vietnamese
thirteen-year-old learned it in primary school.

A lonely child with a secret fish in the water, fed in secret, that answers when
called — that is our Chapter 1, beat for beat, and it costs us no setup at all.

**But we take the entrance and nothing after it.** In the tale the bống is found
out, killed and eaten, and its bones become the next piece of magic. We are not
doing any of that, and a reader who knows the story should feel the difference
land as relief rather than as a gap. Our fish is not a victim, is not a gift
from a god, is not owed to anybody, and does not end up as bones. She gets out
of the bucket by herself on the third day.

> **This is the structural departure**, and it is a real one rather than a
> rename. In the folk tale the fish is *acted upon* — it is the passive object
> of a kindness and then of a cruelty. Ours is the most active person in the
> book.

### Why she is wrong

She is a child, and she has been ashore for about a week, and **nobody has
finished teaching her anything.** She knows what she can do. She does not know
what it costs, she cannot always tell what the sea has taken from what it merely
covers, and she is confidently wrong about her own rules perhaps twice an act.

That is the same job the helper did in the version this replaces, and it is
still the right job — a helper who is always right is a moral authority, not a
friend, and that kills the buddy dynamic the whole thing runs on (§5). What is
new is that being wrong now costs *her* something, because she is not operating
the magic from outside it. She is the one the sea is coming for.

### What Bống is not

No pocket. No inventory. No wishes. No mission to reform anybody. She is not a
princess, she is not a spirit to be worshipped, and she is not a pet. She is a
guest in somebody's kitchen who eats too much and does not understand doors.

### What she looks like, and the one hard line

Small, six or seven to look at though that is not what she is. Black hair cut
blunt at the jaw and always slightly wet. Eyes a little too round. A pale yellow
shirt several sizes too big, which is Thảo's.

**She is not orange-haired and she does not wear a red dress.** See §9.

## 4. The cast

Written as *slots first*, per `doraemon-craft.md` §5 — the slot is the reusable
craft; the person filling it is ours.

| Slot (§5) | Ours | What they are |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The one it happens to | **Tí** (given name Tuấn; nobody uses it) | Thirteen. Lives with his grandmother; his parents work in a factory city and send money home. Goes to the water for small reasons and gets large ones. |
| The magic, as a person | **Bống** | See §3. |
| The witness who already believes | **Thảo** | Sees it work in Chapter 1 and simply accepts it. Treats Tí as already worth something, which is the reader's licence to like him. |
| The deadline in human form | **Hùng** | Big, loud, genuinely frightening at close range, and a terrible singer who believes otherwise. Supplies the reason a thing must be fixed *today*. Turns useful in a crisis. |
| The have, to the have-not | **Đạt** | His family has the hotel on the promenade. Physically the smallest, talks his way out of what Tí gets hit for. Supplies **envy** — the wound half the chapters are made of. |
| The domestic enforcer | **Bà Sáu**, his grandmother | Sells at the fish market before light. Strict, funny, worried about money out loud. Whatever the sea does, she is the reckoning. Southern birth-order naming, deliberately. |
| The mirror ahead | **Chú Bảy**, an uncle | Works a boat. Was also bad at school and is fine, mostly. The story's quiet promise. |
| The institutional scoreboard | **Cô Yến** | Makes failure public and recorded, and carries it from school to the house. |
| The unhateable superior | **Khoa** | Top of everything and genuinely kind, so the inadequacy is entirely Tí's own and cannot curdle into a grudge. |
| The one who came back | **Minh** | Chapter 3. A boy from Tí's class whose family sold the boat and left in the night a year ago. What comes back is the boat, and he is on it, and he is still thirteen. |
| The guest who needs help | **the keeper** (*người giữ đèn biển*) | The lighthouse keeper who measured the tides and went into the water and stayed. From Act III he comes for Bống. He is the one who **needs** something, and **the one we say goodbye to.** The farewell belongs to him. |
| What is bigger than everybody | **the whale** | Unnamed, and never explained. Appears twice: once at the festival in Chapter 5, once at the end. She is what the coast already worships, which is why nobody in the story is surprised by her and everybody is quiet. |

**Retired:** Duy, Linh, Mai, Hoa, the per-unit one-offs (Khang, Trang, Nam and
their siblings), and Mun. **Not retired:** any name inside a prescribed Lesson 4
content block. Those are the book's people, not ours.

**Diacritic-free, and therefore safe as typed answer keys: Khoa, Minh.** Plus
the untitled roles — *the keeper*, *the whale*. Everything else may appear
freely in prose and in multiple choice and must never be a required typed
answer. This is a real constraint and it has bitten before.

### Tí, specified

§5's rule: **name the failures and name the talents; never say "loser."**

**Fails at**, concretely: remembering numbers and dates; every running race;
writing anything at speed; the dark; doing what he was told ten minutes ago.

**Good at**, uselessly: skipping a stone eight or nine bounces; imitating any
bird he has heard once; falling asleep in about a minute, anywhere.

Those three are **deliberately worthless** — no school value, nothing the plot
can secretly cash in. That is the point: a hidden *useful* talent makes an
underdog a fraud who was fine all along; a hidden useless one makes him a
person. What happens instead, per §3's beat 12, is that **the world's demand
changes to match him** — by Act IV the story needs someone who can call a bird
across water, or go somewhere only sleep reaches. He never levels up.

One more, and it is quiet until the last chapter: **he never forgets a face.**
Nobody thinks this is a skill. §6 is where it turns out to be the whole ending.

## 5. Fixed furniture

Four places, reused all twelve chapters. §6: a closed circuit means each setting
owns one beat, a short chapter needs no establishing work — and for a language
course, a fixed set of places means a **recycling lexical field**, so vocabulary
compounds instead of resetting with the plot.

| Place | Its job | The beat it owns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The harbour wall** | Unowned, adult-free, where the water gives things up | The return, and the mark |
| **Bà Sáu's kitchen** | Warm, strict, smells of fish and lemongrass | The consequence |
| **The school yard** | Where failure is public. The sea is below it. | The humiliation |
| **The fish market** | Everyone, before light, all talking at once | The complication and the rumour |

Bống sleeps **in the water butt behind the kitchen**, under a tin lid, which is
the most ordinary object in a poor house and now has the sea in it — §6's point
about the desk drawer: the furniture is the thesis.

### What is real, and what is ours

The city is **Quy Nhơn**, on the central coast. Since 1 July 2025 it is part of
**Gia Lai province**, the coast and the highlands in one — which is why Chapter
4 can go upriver to a Bahnar village without leaving home. Real, and used
truthfully: the crescent of city beach and its promenade; the fishing port and
its market; **Ghềnh Ráng**, the cliffs and the pebble beach at the south end;
**Tháp Đôi**, the twelfth-century Chăm towers standing in the middle of the
town; **Bãi Xép**, the fishing hamlet down the coast; **Nhơn Lý** across the
bay, its wind-gap cliffs at **Eo Gió**, its coral, and its **Lăng Ông Nam Hải**
— a working whale shrine with a living festival.

Ours, and invented: **Bãi Sẻ**, the small fishing quarter at the north end of
the bay where Tí lives, and everybody in it. We do not put a fiction on a real
street or a real family.

**The whale is not invented.** *Thờ Cá Ông* is what this coast actually does:
the whale is the protector who brings drowning fishermen in, the shrines hold
her bones, and the festival prays for a calm year. We use it as it is, we get it
right, and we never explain it to the reader — the characters already know.

## 6. The arc

Twelve chapters. Each unit's prescribed topic supplies **what the sea gives
back**, and the returns escalate: a thing → a way → a person → a house → a night
→ a life → a reef → a market → a town → a voice → a life's work → a name.

The prescribed syllabus was never fighting us. Read in order it is already
shaped like a coast: leisure and the water, out to the countryside, the
highlands, a festival, the sea itself, a flood, and the moon.

| # | Unit topic | Grammar the unit must teach | What the sea gives back |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Leisure Time | V-ing vs to-V | **A game.** Something small, playful and completely useless — so the rule is learned cheaply. |
| 2 | Life in the Countryside | Comparative adverbs | **A path.** The old track round the headland, which the sea took and the new road replaced. |
| 3 | Teenagers | Simple & compound sentences | **A boat**, and Minh is on it. He left a year ago and he has not aged. |
| 4 | Ethnic Groups of Viet Nam | Questions; countable/uncountable | **A house.** Upriver, and the questions they must ask to learn whose it was. |
| 5 | Our Customs and Traditions | Articles | **A festival.** The whale night, as it used to be, with the whale in it. |
| 6 | Lifestyles | Future simple & 1st conditional | **A way of living.** The conditional is the chapter: *if we call it back, then…* |
| 7 | Environmental Protection | Complex sentences; time clauses | **A reef.** All at once, under the boats. The first time the sea frightens them. |
| 8 | Shopping | Adverbs of frequency | **A market.** With its sellers, its prices and its old money. |
| 9 | Natural Disasters | Past continuous + past simple | **A town.** The one the sea took, and the water it displaces has to go somewhere. Past continuous is the grammar of a flood, exactly. |
| 10 | Communication in the Future | Prepositions; possessives | **A voice.** A call from a boat that never came in. |
| 11 | Science and Technology | Reported speech — statements | **A life's work.** The keeper's own measurements, and what he *said* would happen. |
| 12 | Life on Other Planets | Reported speech — questions | **A name.** Bống's own — and with it, the choice. |

That the grammar targets fit this well is not luck to be relied on twice; it is
the reason the returns were chosen in this order. Chapters 7, 9 and 12 in
particular exist in that shape *because* the prescribed vocabulary wanted them
there: Unit 7 prescribes *coral*, *dugong*, *ecosystem*, *habitat* and
*endangered species*; Unit 9 prescribes *tsunami*, *flood*, *warn* and *rescue
worker*; Unit 12 prescribes *gravity*, and the moon pulls the water.

### The chain — this is one story, not twelve

**The binding rule for anyone drafting: every chapter's ending must cause the
next chapter's problem.** Not "and then another week went by." A reader must be
able to say *because* between any two consecutive chapters. If a chapter could
be swapped with another chapter, it is not written yet.

> **1 → 2** The first return reaches further back than a week, and Bống is
> visibly surprised by that — the first hint she does not know her own range.
> **2 → 3** The path that comes back is not just a path. It is the way to the
> old landing, and it teaches Tí the thing the whole of Act I turns on: *the sea
> gives back across years, not days.* That is what makes Minh thinkable.
> **3 → 4** Minh has not aged, and he could be sent home with two words, and Tí
> will not say them. **The first thing he refuses to give back** — and Minh
> knows something about a house upriver that the water reached once before.
> **4 → 5** The house's people kept a festival, and the questions in Chapter 4
> are what tell the children when it was and what it was for.
> **5 → 6** At the festival **somebody recognises what Bống is.** The rumour
> starts here, and so does the keeper.
> **6 → 7** Because of the rumour the lane is at war, and the town's compromise
> is a harbour clean-up. Tí goes to it meaning to stop. That is why they are
> standing on the breakwater when the tide turns.
> **7 → 8** The reef comes back through the moorings and does real damage. The
> adults now know, and Bống can no longer be kept behind the kitchen.
> **8 → 9** A whole market returns with its old money, the disorder is large
> enough to be news, and **the keeper arrives.** He says the sea has been coming
> for a week and will not stop, and that Bống has been told none of this.
> **9 → 10** Stung, she insists she can hold it. The storm is the moment she is
> proved wrong: the water goes over the ninth mark and somebody is nearly lost.
> In the flood they find something that was **sent and never arrived**.
> **10 → 11** The voice is the keeper's, from before. It names his work and
> where it is.
> **11 → 12** His measurements say what happens at the twelfth mark, and they
> say what a called-back person needs in order to stay. One mark left.

The four Reviews sit at the act joints and **recombine, never advance** — a
Review teaches nothing by design and may only reach into its own three units.

### Four acts

The Reviews after Units 3, 6, 9 and 12 are the act breaks — but they are poor
places for revelation. **Use them as act punctuation: recombination, never new
plot.**

- **Act I — Bãi Sẻ (1–3).** A fish in a bucket. Small returns, the rule learned,
  the cost noticed. Ends on the first thing he will not give back.
- **Act II — Upriver and the festival (4–6).** Older and larger returns: a
  house, a night, a way of living. The world widens, Bống is recognised, and her
  own account of herself starts being wrong.
- **Act III — The water (7–9).** The sea begins returning things nobody asked
  for. The keeper arrives and is right about everything and no help at all. The
  flood is the dark hour.
- **Act IV — The moon (10–12).** A voice across distance, the keeper's work, and
  the last mark on the wall.

### The ending — of the adventure, not of the children

**This is the rule that governs Chapter 12, and it is easy to get wrong.**

Chapter 12 ends **this adventure**. It does not end Tí, it does not end Bống,
and it does not end their friendship. The *guest* departs and the door closes,
the core cast goes home with the arrangement intact.

So the farewell is spent on **the keeper**, not on the girl.

**How it goes.** The moon comes closest and the water stands at the twelfth
mark, over the street, and the town is not destroyed — it is quiet, and there
are boats where the buses were, and lanterns, and everybody is alive. The keeper
comes to take Bống home, because that is what the sea is doing and it will not
stop while she is ashore. What comes back at the twelfth is **her own name**,
which the sea took when she left it — and knowing it is what makes the choice a
choice instead of a drift.

The law that decides it is the one the keeper's notebooks state in Chapter 11
and Chapter 3 has been waiting for since it happened: **a called-back thing
stays as long as somebody holds on to what it was.** That is why Minh is still
here. It is also, exactly, why Bống can stay — and the boy standing in front of
her **never forgets a face.**

She stays. The keeper goes home, and the sea goes back out, and the tide takes
the marks off the wall.

Four rules the ending obeys:

- **The magic is not what wins.** By the climax the magic is never the thing
  that wins. The last call is *given away*, not spent on the boy's own want.
- **The ending was promised in Chapter 1.** Twelve marks, counted up in the
  reader's sight the whole way. When the adventure ends it ends because the
  thing that started it has arrived — not because an author decided to stop.
- **The magic closes; the world does not.** The water goes back, the wall dries,
  the town goes back to ordinary. Tí still can't remember a date. Bà Sáu still
  wants to know where he's been.
- **A souvenir, so the world stays changed.** Each act leaves one thing behind
  that does not go back — the anti-reset device (§3, beat 16). The path from
  Chapter 2 is never given back. Minh stays. And at the very end: a girl who
  used to be a fish, in a yellow shirt, going to school.

**The last beat leaves the door open.** Not "and they never saw the sea do
anything again." Something small and ordinary that implies tomorrow.

> **Authoring rule for whoever drafts Chapter 12:** nothing in it may be
> irreversible *about the cast*. The sea goes back, the keeper is gone, a loss
> is real — but no character is written out, no relationship is closed, and no
> sentence forecloses another journey.

## 7. How a chapter is built — the seven-scene template

Per unit: roughly 750 words of continuous prose in four real scenes, plus three
lighter beats carried in example sentences and exercise stems.
`chapter-briefs.md` §0 carries the binding version of this table, including the
correction about Lesson 4. **Where the two differ, the briefs win.**

| Lesson | Slot | Scene |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 — Getting Started | dialogue, ~210 w | **The trouble.** Two characters, present tense, an ordinary problem. |
| 2 — A Closer Look 1 | example sentences | Story words and story sentences. No plot. |
| 3 — A Closer Look 2 | example sentences | The unit's grammar, spoken by our people. |
| 4 — Communication | **prescribed — do not restory** | The book's own content block, untouched. |
| 5 — Skills 1 | reading passage, ~240 w | **The turn of the tide, and what came back.** |
| 6 — Skills 2 | audio script ~200 w + writing model ~95 w | **It goes wrong**, then a character writes something. |
| 7 — Looking Back | error-hunt paragraph, ~70 w | **Where we are now**, and the mark. Doubles as the recap. |

### Two structural findings that shape this template

**The magic arrives at the midpoint, not the opening.** From the one chapter we
could reconstruct at beat level, the gadget landed at beat 5 of 9, after four
beats of ordinary domestic and social pressure. That is a gift here: Lessons 1–4
are pure A2 daily-life language — invitations, refusing food, being told off —
carrying no fantasy vocabulary load at all. The tide does not turn until Lesson
5.

**The adventure is a swerve away from whatever the start was.** Across 38 films,
71% of adventures are unsought — but the sharper rule is that even in the
*planned* ones, the plan is never the story: they set out for treasure and meet
pirates. So: **every chapter opens on an ordinary want, and what the sea returns
is never it.**

And one correction to our own model, worth carrying: "opens with the boy already
in tears" is **one common opening, not the opening.** Vary it.

## 8. Voice

- **A2/B1, and the register is not a compromise.** This is the level the
  bilingual junior-high editions of this kind of story are actually published
  at.
- **Name things the way the official translations name gadgets.** "Anywhere
  door." "Shrink ray." Every one is a transparent compound of A1 words whose
  function a learner decodes without a gloss. *The sea gives back what it took*
  is built to that rule, and so is everything we add.
- **Short names, stressed, easy to say aloud.** Tí. Bống. Thảo. Khoa. Minh.
  These get read aloud into listening scripts.
- **Explaining happens outside the dialogue.** The dialogue stays simple; the
  reading passage does the explaining.
- **One impossible thing, in a wholly ordinary setting.** *Sukoshi Fushigi* —
  "a little bit strange." The school, the homework, the market and the
  grandmother must all stay completely ordinary, or the sea has nothing to be
  strange against. **This is why the town is Quy Nhơn and not a fantasy coast**,
  and why the tourist half of it is in the book: hotels, minibuses and a boy
  whose family has neither.
- **The sea is never angry.** It is not a villain and there is no villain. It is
  doing one thing, patiently, and the trouble is that it is very large and the
  town is in the way. Nobody drowns. The flood in Chapter 9 is frightening and
  then beautiful, in that order.
- **Never a narrator explaining the course.** No "in this chapter you will
  learn." The story is the material.

## 9. What this deliberately does not take

Codex's sharpest finding on the craft document was that *every abstraction being
individually unprotected does not make the whole package safe* — selection and
arrangement can be distinctive even where each element is generic. The house art
style is openly borrowed and that is fine; style is not the thing that needs
defending. The plot is. So, plainly, what we changed at the skeleton:

**From the serialized-gadget model:**

| Theirs | Ours |
| --- | --- |
| A pocket of unlimited gadgets | One rule, one girl, twelve marks |
| Sent from the future by a descendant, to reform the boy | Came ashore by herself; nobody sent anybody |
| A debt owed by the family | No debt, no mission, no obligation |
| A helper who knows what he is doing | A child who has been ashore a week |
| Episodic; every chapter resets | One continuous journey with a counter that only rises |
| The boy is judged and never changes | The world's demand changes to match him |

**From the film the art style is borrowed from**, which matters more here
because the resemblance is closer and deliberate. What we take is the *shape* —
a sea-child ashore, water that rises without malice, a town that floods and is
beautiful rather than tragic, and a magical friend who chooses the ordinary
world. What we do not take, and none of it may appear:

- A fish-child who becomes human by **tasting blood**, or by any transformation
  the story stages as a threshold crossing. Bống is already a girl when the
  reader meets her, and how that happened is never dramatised.
- A **wizard father with potions and a well of life**, or any magical apparatus
  as the source of the imbalance. Our keeper is a man who measured tides, wrote
  them down, and did not come back; he has no powers and no laboratory.
- The **moon physically falling** or satellites raining down. Our moon comes to
  perigee, which is a real thing the sea responds to, and Unit 12 teaches
  *gravity*.
- A **test of true love**, a kiss, or a bargain with a sea goddess as the price
  of staying. Our rule is the keeper's plain finding from Chapter 11, stated in
  a notebook, and it turns on memory rather than love.
- The mother of the sea as a **speaking, radiant, named** figure who
  adjudicates. Our whale says nothing, is never named, and is not asked
  permission. She is a local religious reality treated with respect, not a
  character with lines.
- Ponyo's **visual design**: no orange hair, no red dress, no ham. §3 fixes
  Bống's appearance away from it deliberately.

The rename was always the cheap half. These two tables are the expensive half.

## 10. Open, and needing a decision

1. **The *Tấm Cám* borrowing.** Naming her Bống buys instant recognition and
   carries the tale's worst beat with it — in the original the fish is eaten. §3
   is written to make the divergence feel like relief, but somebody should ask a
   Vietnamese teacher whether the association lands that way for thirteen-year-
   olds, before this ships.
2. **The whale.** *Thờ Cá Ông* is live religious practice on this coast, not
   folklore. We have kept her silent, unnamed and unexplained precisely to stay
   respectful, and the festival in Chapter 5 is written from what the shrines
   actually do. Still worth a read by someone from the coast.
3. **Chapter 3, and how a boy comes back.** The loss is economic — a boat sold,
   a family gone in the night — expressly so that it is not a drowning. In a
   fishing town the other reading is available to any reader who wants it. The
   drafter must close it off on the page, in Chapter 3, in a line.
4. **Whether Bà Sáu or the absent parents carry the poverty.** Both are true to
   Vietnamese life; only one should carry the *emotional* weight, or the
   chapters get crowded.
5. **The real-place responsibility.** Quy Nhơn is a real city with real
   fishermen. Bãi Sẻ is invented so that no real quarter is given a fictional
   poverty, and the erosion, the reefs and the whale shrine are used as they
   actually are. Anything added later that is specific and unflattering should
   be invented too.
